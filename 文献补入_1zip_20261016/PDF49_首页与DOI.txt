Valorizing palm date wastes for 
supercapacitor electrode synthesis 
and evaluation
Amany Mostafa1, Sodky H. Mohammed2, Nouby M. Ghazaly1 & Ahmed ElSheikh 1
Climate and energy issues continue to drive researchers to develop fossil-free energy technologies that 
are both sustainable and scalable. In this work, date seeds (DS) are used to produce activated carbon 
(DSAC), which is then used as a supercapacitor electrode. The synthesis method uses KOH activation 
coupled with argon-gas flow calcination at 950 °C. The produced DSAC has a high specific surface area 
of 1374 m2/g and a turbostratic microporous structure (pore size = 1.14 nm), in addition to oxygen-
containing functional groups (–C–O–, –OH). The DSAC electrochemical performance exhibits an electric 
double-layer capacitor (EDLC) with a minor pseudocapacitive contribution and delivers a 3-electrode 
specific capacitance of 1800 F/g at 4 A/g (estimated full-device specific capacitance is 450 F/g, energy, 
and power densities: 40 Wh/kg and 1440 W/kg, respectively). These numbers are remarkably higher 
than the current state-of-the-art carbon supercapacitor findings. Furthermore, it retains 83.3% of its 
initial discharge capacity after 2000 galvanostatic charge-discharge cycles at 10 A/g. Finally, the DSAC 
electrode performance is slightly enhanced with increasing the KOH concentration from 1 M to 3 M.
Keywords Biomass, Activated carbon, Supercapacitor, KOH activation
The climate change and energy supply crises continue to call for accelerated research and development efforts 
of sustainable energy technologies1. This is particularly true in electric mobility, where supercapacitors and Li-
ion batteries play a vital role. Supercapacitors or electrochemical capacitors are electrochemical cells comprised 
of two electrodes, a separator, and an electrolyte. Their inherent merits, which occasionally enable them to 
outperform Li-ion batteries, include fast charging and discharging, high power density, and long cycle life 2. 
There are two main mechanisms for storing electrical charge in supercapacitors: electric double-layer capacitors 
(EDLCs) and pseudocapacitors. The former adopts the electrostatic adsorption and desorption of electrolyte 
ions at the electrode-electrolyte interface, forming the double-layer configuration. The latter is achieved via a 
reversible redox charge-transfer reaction at the electrode-electrolyte interface. Occasionally, both mechanisms 
occur simultaneously in a single material, combining the advantages of both 3. Furthermore, supercapacitors 
could be classified based on their materials into metal oxide, polymer, and carbon-based. Unlike oxide and 
polymer-based supercapacitors, carbon-based supercapacitors have demonstrated stable performance in 
addition to their cost-effectiveness4.
Biomass-based electrodes have garnered considerable attention due to their inherent properties, such 
as raw material abundance, high surface area, chemical stability, high specific surface area, low weight, and 
interconnected porous structure5. Wood residues, coconut shells, apricot pits, agricultural wastes, and marine 
algae are some of the examples of biomass precursors that have been extensively studied in the preparation of 
porous activated carbons with well-developed micro/mesoporous structure that can be used in supercapacitor 
applications6–11. The chemical activation process can be used to produce hierarchical porous carbons with a 
large surface area and tunable pore structure using these lignocellulosic resources. Recent research work has 
shown that carbons produced by biomass have the potential to provide promising electrochemical properties 
when optimized activation conditions are used and thus can serve as sustainable alternatives to standard carbon 
materials. As an example, it has been reported that porous carbons obtained using many natural precursors as 
hydrogen storage and energy-related materials have been investigated in the past, thus showing the success of the 
chemical activation strategies in the development of pore structure and surface chemistry12,13.
A palm date tree (Phoenix dactylifera) generates 40 kg of waste from leaves, surface fibres surrounding the 
trunk, and fruit-bearing seeds per year 14. Palm dates are a crucial food whose total production reached 9.66 
million metric tons in 2024, with the Middle East countries (especially Egypt) as the top producers due to ideal

Recent studies confirmed that date-seed activated carbon (DSAC) is a promising and viable electrode 
substance in supercapacitors since it combines cost-effectiveness, environmental compatibility, large surface 
area, and porosity control. In a study of this type, a DSAC was assembled with Ni-doped α-Bi₂O₃ to form a 
hybrid electrode; the carbon part of the electrode provided electronic conductivity, increased ion-accessible 
surface area, and strengthened charge transportation, and the Ni-doped oxide part of the electrode provided 
pseudocapacitive redox properties. Accordingly, the Ni-doped α-Bi₂O₃ /DSAC composite exhibited a specific 
capacitance of 174 F/g at 5 mV/s, which is better than the oxide on its own and supports the positive synergy of 
electric double layer capacitance and faradaic charge storage 15. In another work DSAC was confirmed to give 
a parallel trend to a Zn 2O3/AC composite; their activation has significantly increased the BET surface area of 
1.6779 to 114.17 m2/g, promoted a porous nanosheet-like structure, reduced the charge transfer resistance, and 
provided a high specific capacitance of 281 F/g at 5 mV/s 16. In a different related study, discarded dates were 
transformed into porous carbon aerogels by hydrothermal pretreatment, freeze-drying, and KOH activation, 
which offered a three-dimensional heteroatom-rich porous structure with a specific surface area of 1348.87⁻¹ m 
2 g− 1 , the optimized material had a high capacitance of 769 F/g at 0.5 A/g, highlighting the high energy-storage 
capability of porous DSAC17.
Although date-seed-derived carbons have been previously reported for supercapacitor applications 14,18–21, 
most studies focus primarily on demonstrating feasibility rather than systematically optimizing activation 
conditions and correlating pore structure with electrochemical performance. In this work, emphasis is placed 
on tailoring the activation strategy and elucidating the structure–performance relationship to achieve enhanced 
capacitive behavior.
Experimental
Chemicals and methods
The raw material is date seed (DS) waste from households in Qena, Egypt. The chemicals used in this synthesis 
are KOH, HCl, poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) as a binder, and dimethylformamide (DMF) as a solvent, in 
addition to argon and nitrogen gases. The devices are a D8 Advance XRD diffractometer (Bruker, Germany), 
a Confocal Raman Microscope (WiTec, 300R alpha, Germany), an ST2 on NOV A touch 4LX equipment (SN 
17016062702), an FEI Quanta 250, Thermo Fisher Scientific (Czech Republic), a ATR-FTIR Bruker Alpha IFS-
125, a JEM-2100 (Japan), and a Quartz Kejia Tube furnace. For the evaluation of electrochemical capacitors, an 
Origalys OFG + 500 potentiostat/galvanostat (France) was used.
Synthesis of date seed activated carbon (DSAC)
Figure 1 exhibits the synthetic procedure of activated carbon synthesis from date seeds. Household DSs were 
collected, washed with tap water, and dried. Then, they were carbonized at 350 °C for 4 h in a muffle furnace in 
an air atmosphere. The pre-carbonization was designed to remove volatiles, promote initial carbon framework 
formation, and introduce some oxygen functionalities. Following, DSs were manually ground using a zirconia 
planetary ball mill at 400 rpm for 2 h, followed by 30-minute hot sonication (50 °C) to dissolve the carbon in 
distilled water. Thereafter, they were chemically activated using KOH, since it is a versatile method to activate

and mesoporosity23–25. The reaction continued for 24 h at room temperature. Following, the solid material was

they were activated at 950 °C under argon flow for 1.5 h. Although it reduces the yield due to extensive burn 
off, the 950 °C was chosen to promote the development of graphitic carbon structures, which enhance electrical 
conductivity, a desirable property for supercapacitor applications. For example, increasing the temperature 
from 800 °C to 1000 °C enhances the graphitic order 25–27. The argon flow was 350 sccm. The temperature was 
increased from room temperature to 950 °C within 30 min, corresponding to a relatively rapid heating ramp. 
Such a heating profile can influence the activation pathway by affecting volatile release, KOH etching kinetics, 
pore widening, and carbon framework contraction. The activated carbon powder was rinsed with 0.1 M HCL to 
neutralize the KOH, followed by a cold water-ethanol mixture bath washing. Subsequently, it was dried at 90 °C 
for 24 h. The DSAC yield following this method is 41%.
Physiochemical characterization
X-ray diffraction (XRD) data of the samples were collected with a D8 Advance with Davinci design (Bruker, 
Germany), using an X-ray source the Cu Kα radiation (wavelength λ = 1.5418 Å), at 40 kV and 40 mA, a 2θ range 
of 20–80°, a step size of 0.02°, and a time/step of 0.6 s. A Si zero-background sample holder operated by Diffrac 
was used. Raman spectroscopy was applied to study the structure of the produced carbon. A confocal Raman 
spectrometer equipped with a laser source of 532 nm was utilized to collect the spectra. N2 adsorption/desorption 
measurements, at 77.35°K, utilizing the St 2 on NOV A touch 4LX equipment to analyze the Brunauer-Emmett-
Teller (BET) surface area and porosity, were performed. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-
SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) were performed using FEI Quanta 250, Thermo Fisher 
Scientific. The ATR-FTIR measurement was carried out by Fourier transformation infrared spectrophotometer 
model Bruker Alpha IFS-125 in the wavenumber range 400–4000  cm − 1, scan time = 32, resolution = 4.High-
resolution transmission microscopy (HRTEM) was performed utilizing JEOL JEM-2100. The suspension of the 
material was sonicated for 10 min on an ultrasonicator (Crest Ultrasonics Corp., USA). Then, a few drops were 
loaded onto a carbon-coated copper grid and left to dry.

T o prepare the working electrode, DSAC powder (10 mg) was sonicated in 1 mL of N, N-Dimethylformamide 
(DMF) for 30 min. Then, Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) (40 µL, 3 wt%) was added to the DSAC suspension, 
and the whole mixture was stirred for 30 min again. Meanwhile, the glassy carbon electrode (GCE) was polished 
with alumina powders (1 and 0.5 μm). Following, slurry (40 µL) was drop-casted on the GCE surface. Then, it 
was dried overnight at 60 °C. The mass loading of DSAC on the electrode was 0.5 mg/cm 2. Then, a 3-electrode 
cell was prepared, and the electrolyte was KOH (1 M). Another experiment was performed with 3 M to study 
the effect of increasing the concentration on electrode performance. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) scans at 5, 10, 20, 
50, 100, 200 mV/s between 0 and − 800 mV vs. Hg/HgO were performed. Galvanostatic charge and discharge 
(GCD) scans were measured at 2, 4, 8, and 10 A/g. T o evaluate the material stability, a 2000-cycle GCD test was 
performed.
Results and discussions
Physiochemical characterization
The DSAC yield for the current work is 41%, close to other reported yields in28 , 29. However, it is slightly lower, 
likely due to the 350 °C air precarbonation. The XRD pattern (Fig. 2. A) shows a broad hump around ~ 23–26° 
(2θ) corresponding to the (002) plane of turbostratic/amorphous carbon and a weak, broad peak around 44° 
associated with the (100) and (101) planes of disordered graphite-like domains. The broad diffraction peak 
observed at ~ 23–26° (2θ), corresponding to the (002) plane, was used to estimate the interlayer spacing (002) via 
Bragg’s law, yielding values in the range of ~ 0.34–0.38 nm. This spacing is slightly larger than that of crystalline 
graphite (~ 0.335 nm), indicating a turbostratic and disordered carbon structure with expanded interlayer 
distance. These diffraction features are characteristic of poorly ordered graphitic carbon and are consistent with 
the standard ICDD Powder Diffraction File (PDF) card No. 41-1487 for graphitic carbon. Similar patterns were 
obtained with other DSAC samples3,14,30,31. The absence of any other minor sharp or broad peaks signals the clean 
carbon production. The high intensity at low diffraction angles (< 30°) does not reflect structural information but
