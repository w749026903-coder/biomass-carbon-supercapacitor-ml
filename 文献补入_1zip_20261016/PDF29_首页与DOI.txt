ABSTRACT
Selective ion separation is essential for effective water treatment and resource recovery. Conventional techniques, such as 
adsorption, coagulation/flocculation, and membrane technologies, suffer from low selectivity, substantial cost, and high energy 
requirements. Alternatively, ion separation using capacitive deionization (CDI) has shown strong potential for energy efficiency, 
cost‐effectiveness, environmental sustainability, and selective removal. However, traditional carbon electrodes used in CDI have 
limitations for selective separations. MXene‐based electrodes present an attractive alternative for CDI due to their exceptional 
electrochemical properties. Nevertheless, MXenes are typically unstable over extended cycles, and to achieve ion selectivity, 
controlled interlayer spacing adjustment is required. Methods such as hybridization and functionalization can improve the 
selectivity properties of MXenes by tailoring their interlayer spacing. Several studies have reported the use of MXenes as CDI 
electrodes; however, most focus on brackish water desalination, while selective ion separation is rarely addressed. This review 
presents an in‐depth analysis of the role of modified MXene electrodes in advancing ion‐selective CDI technologies, exploring 
various strategies to achieve the desired selectivity. Moreover, it highlights critical challenges that hinder scalability and 
practical implementation. Finally, the review showcases the strategic roadmap for future opportunities toward next‐generation 
water purification and resource recovery solutions.

Introduction
Water scarcity and wastewater generation have become critical 
challenges of the 21st century due to increasing population and 
industrial growth [ 1, 2 ]. Projections indicate that global water 
demand may rise by over 50% in the coming decades, whereas 
nearly one‐fourth of major urban centers are already under sig -
nificant water stress [ 3 ]. Consequently, the need for water recla -
mation and resource recovery is growing rapidly to bridge the 
increasing gap between water demand and supply. Similarly, 
wastewater generation is progressively rising. Every year, the 
world produces around 380 billion m
3 
of municipal wastewater. 
This number is expected to rise by 24% by 2030 and 51% by 2050 
[ 4 ]. Yet, wastewater is predominantly regarded as a problematic 
waste stream and is typically treated as a pollutant requiring 
removal. However, wastewater presents a valuable resource, 
containing recoverable water, energy, and high‐value nutrients.
To meet the increasing water demand, desalination technologies, 
including reverse osmosis (RO), membrane distillation, and 
electrodialysis, are found to be more efficient technologies in 
comparison with others [ 5–7 ]. However, these technologies often 
suffer from high energy consumption required for pumping or

Pseudocapacitive materials bridge the gap between capacitive and 
battery‐type electrodes, offering a combination of high capaci -

rials. Various types of pseudocapacitive materials have been uti -

Electrical Double‐Layer (EDL) Model
In carbon‐based CDI, the electrosorption is largely based on the 
electrical double‐layer (EDL) mechanism. EDLs are formed near 
the interface of the highly porous carbonaceous electrode ma -
terial and water, in which charges can be stored. Alternatively, 
when using pseudocapacitive materials, ion storage is facilitated 
by either a reversible surface redox mechanism or an ion de/ 
intercalation mechanism in the case of crystals or multilayered 
materials (Section 2.2 ).
A common EDL concept is depicted in Figure 2 by taking the 
positive electrode with the negative ions. When a potential 
difference is applied across the electrodes (only one electrode is 
shown for matters of simplicity), the ions present in the water 
move toward the electrodes having opposite polarities and start 
forming EDLs on the available surfaces. This explains why, in 
CDI, materials with high surface areas are preferred because 
they provide a larger surface for the formation of larger 
EDLs [ 24 ].

larger than the thickness of the EDL, we can assume that the
