ABSTRACT
Water scarcity and salinity pose critical challenges that necessitate the development of efficient 
desalination processes for brackish water treatment. This study investigates the use of activated carbon 
derived from date palm pollen as a potential electrode material for batch capacitive deionization (CDI). 
Activated carbon exhibits an exceptionally large surface area of 2586.9 m²·g −1, as determined by 
Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area analysis, which provides numerous sites for electrostatic ion 
accumulation during CDI operation. When activated carbon is used as an electrode in batch CDI 
experiments with a 500 ppm NaCl feedstock at an applied cell voltage of 1.2 V, the electrode exhibits a 
salt adsorption capacity (SAC) of 38.0 mg·g−1 after 120 min of adsorption. These results indicate effective 
electrosorptive salt removal from brackish water under practical operating conditions. Additionally, 
electrochemical measurements reveal that the activated carbon electrodes exhibit a high specific 
capacitance of 92 F·g −1, indicating efficient storage of electric charge in electric double layers. The high 
SAC and specific capacitance and the large BET surface area confirm that the date-palm-pollen-derived 
activated carbon is a promising electrode material for batch CDI desalination systems.

1. Introduction
Water scarcity is now recognized as one of the most significant global concerns of this century, threatening 
to affect billions of people around the world because of the increased pressure created by population 
explosion, rapid industrialization, and climate change. Additionally, water quality is declining owing to 
pollution generated by agricultural activities and industrial emissions [1],[2]. Thus, new methods have 
garnered tremendous interest in producing clean water through desalination technologies such as reverse 
osmosis (RO) and thermal distillation. Existing methods used to purify seawater through either RO or 
thermal distillation have proven highly effective but require significantly high energy, which makes these 
processes economically unfeasible for producing fresh water from brackish or low-salinity feedstocks 
[3],[4]. The urgent need for sustainable, energy-efficient, and economical methods for producing purified 
water has led researchers to develop capacitive deionization (CDI) technology. CDI is an attractive 
alternative technology that provides results similar to those of RO and thermal distillation; it requires 
significantly lower energy consumption, has a reduced environmental impact due to the chemicals used, 
and possesses modular design capabilities [5],[6]. CDI is an electrochemically operated, water desalination 
technology that removes ionic impurities by reversibly storing them into the electric double layers (EDLs) 
formed at the interface between two oppositely charged porous electrodes [7],[8]. Unlike pressure-driven 
or thermal methods, CDI utilizes low applied voltages (typically between 0.8 and 1.6 V) to electrosorb ions 
onto the surfaces of two oppositely charged electrodes. Ions are then released back into the aqueous 
solution when the voltage is reversed or reduced, allowing electrode regeneration and continuous CDI 
operation through repeated cycles of adsorption and desorption [9],[10]. The distinct advantages of CDI

over traditional desalination processes include significantly reduced energy consumption for brackish 
water applications. Reportedly, energy requirements depend on feed salinity and system configuration, 
and various studies indicate a range of 0.27–2.0 kWh·mol⁻¹ for CDI systems, whereas RO systems treating 
similar feed solutions require 3–5 kWh·mol⁻¹ [11]. Additionally, CDI does not need high-pressure pumping 
or thermal energy input, reducing system complexity and capital costs and requiring minimum 
maintenance [12],[13]. CDI is a modular and scalable solution for decentralized water treatment systems 
in remote and resource-limited areas [14]. Additionally, CDI generates only a concentrated brine waste 
stream and does not produce secondary waste streams; the concentrated brine can be treated or reused 
as a valuable resource [15]. 
Recent developments in CDI architecture have enabled greater operational capability and expandability 
of the technology. The incorporation of ion-exchange membranes into membrane CDI (mCDI) systems to 
impede co-ion expulsion allows a practical increase in the charge efficiency of CDI systems from low 
percent values to ranges of 80–95% [15],[16],[17]. By utilizing continuously flowing carbon slurries as 
electrodes, flow-electrode CDI systems have eliminated periodic electrode regeneration, thus allowing 
continuous operation [18],[19]. Furthermore, hybrid CDI systems that utilize both capacitive and Faradaic 
(charge storage) mechanisms have demonstrated enhanced salt hay-stacking characteristics and 
improved selectivity for several targeted ions [20],[21],[20]. These technological advancements have 
introduced several challenges in CDI operation, including limited salt adsorption capacity (SAC) in relation 
to that achieved through the RO process, co-ion expulsion in symmetric CDI systems, parasitic Faradaic 
reactions that reduce CDI charge efficiency, and long-term cycling instability [22],[23]. Electrode materials 
are critical to the operation of CDI systems and contribute significantly to how effectively a CDI system 
can desalinate water [24],[1]. The performance of CDI electrodes is influenced by several interrelated 
material parameters, including (i) specific surface area (SSA), which indicates the number of available sites 
for ion adsorption; (ii) pore size distribution, where micropores provide sites for high capacitance (less 
than 2 nm) and mesopores promote rapid ion transport (2–50 nm); (iii) electrical conductivity of the 
electrode material, which enables low resistance by minimizing ohmic losses and allowing a more uniform 
charge distribution; (iv) surface wettability and hydrophilicity of the electrode material, which promote 
improved ion access to the internal porous structure of the electrode; and (v) electrochemical stability of 
the electrode material over several charge–discharge cycles [25],[26],[27],[26]. Activated carbon has 
become a dominant electrode material used in CDI applications owing to a distinct combination of its 
properties, namely, large SSA (usually between 1,000 and 3,000 m²·g⁻¹), hierarchical porosity, excellent 
chemical stability, good electrical conductivity, and low cost [28],[29]. Activated carbon can be tuned to 
improve CDI performance using various activation methods, including physical activation with CO₂ or 
steam and chemical activation using KOH, NaOH, H₃PO₄, or ZnCl₂, alongside post-processing modifications 
[30],[31].
