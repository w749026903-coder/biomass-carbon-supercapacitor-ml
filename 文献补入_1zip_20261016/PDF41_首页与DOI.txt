Short-Process Preparation of Ultrathin Spherical Hierarchical Porous
Activated Carbon by Spray Pyrolysis of Papermaking Black Liquor for
Electrochemical Energy Storage
Yuelin Guo, Yang Yan, Ming Xu, Xiaojia Zhang, Yuan Zhang, Xiaofeng Wang, Xuezhi Song, *
and Zhenquan Tan *

ABSTRACT: Black liquor is a typical wastewater and troublesome pollutant in the
papermaking industry, which is rich in lignin, hemicellulose, and residual alkali. Here,
we developed a short and e ﬃ cient process to prepare spherical hierarchical porous acti-
vated carbon from papermaking black liquor. The precursor was prepared by spray pyrol-
ysis of black liquor, where carbonization of black liquor, preliminary activation, and pore
formation are achieved under the synergistic e ﬀects of high temperature, water vapor, and
sodium/potassium hydroxides. The further activation was carried out using the residual
sodium and potassium compounds in the precursor material as etching agents.
Hierarchical porous amorphous carbon was achieved with the merits of ultrathin-walled
(10–20 nm) porous hollow spheres and a large amount of thin, bending, and reticular car-
bon nanosheets. Owing to its unique morphology, microstructure, and hierarchical pores,
the optimized sample AC700 presents a large speci ﬁc surface area of 2104.3 m
2 g–1 and a
pore volume of 1.139 cm 3 g–1. When evaluated as the cathode material of lithium-ion
capacitors, AC700 presents a high speci ﬁc capacity of 67.3 mAh g –1 within 2.0–4.0 V, along with excellent rate capability and cycling
stability. This work o ﬀers an e ﬃ cient and scalable pathway for high-value utilization of papermaking black liquor.
KEYWORDS: porous carbon, activated carbon, spray pyrolysis, black liquor, hierarchical pores, lithium-ion capacitors
1. INTRODUCTION
With the popularization of arti ﬁcial intelligence and battery-
driven electri ﬁcation, the demand for high-performance energy
storage devices is becoming increasingly urgent. 1–3 Lithium-
ion capacitors (LICs), characterized by high-power density,
excellent cycling stability, and relatively high energy density,
are considered promising candidates for applications requiring
rapid charge/discharge and long cycle life.
4,5 The performance
of LICs greatly depends on the properties of electrode mate-
rials.
6–8 Among various candidates, biomass-derived activated
carbon materials (orange peel, 2 coconut shells, 9 rice husks, 10,11
soybean straw, 12 wood,13 persimmon cores, 14 biowastes,6 etc.),
featuring high speci ﬁc surface area, hierarchical porosity, and
facile synthesis, are the most widely employed electrode mate-
rials for LICs
8,15 and supercapacitors,16 as well as catalyst carrier
materials.17,18
Lignin is a naturally occurring macromolecular polymer and
the second most abundant biopolymer in plants after cellulose,
making it one of the most abundant aromatic biomass resources
on Earth. Owing to its relatively high carbon content and favor-
able carbonization behavior, lignin has been widely regarded as
a promising precursor for the preparation of activated carbon
and other porous carbon materials.
19 For example, Nune
et al. 20 prepared porous carbon via lignin carbonization, which
exhibited a speci ﬁc surface area of 1092 m 2 g–1 and a capaci-
tance of 114 F g –1. Qiu et al. 21 synthesized a lignin-derived
mesoporous carbon cage by multi-stage explosion through ther-
mal decomposition of calcium oxalate (CaC
2O4), achieving a
speciﬁc surface area of 1175.6 m 2 g–1. Wang et al. 22 prepared
porous carbons derived from sodium lignosulphonate using
chemical activation and template methods, where KOH-
chemically activated porous carbons exhibited the largest spe-
ciﬁc surface area of 1998.4 m
2 g–1. Despite these advances,
the large-scale utilization of lignin remains challenging due to
its heterogeneous structure and the need for complicated
extraction processes.
Black liquor is the wastewater in the papermaking industry,
which contains substantial amounts of lignin, together with

Black liquor contains approximately 90% of the pollutants gen-
erated during the papermaking process. 25,26 At present, black
liquor is typically treated and discharged after pollutant
removal,
27 or concentrated and burned as a low-value fuel. 28,29
Alternatively, lignin can be recovered through acid precipita-
tion,30,31 membrane separation,32 or ﬂocculation precipitation25
processes. However, these treatment and recovery routes gener-
ally involve complex procedures, high energy consumption, and
additional environmental burdens, which limit the e ﬃ cient and
high-value utilization of lignin in black liquor. It is of great sig-
niﬁcance to ﬁnd novel ways for the e ﬃ cient treatment of black
liquor from papermaking.
Spray pyrolysis is a new technique developed in recent years
for fast preparation of powder materials.
33,34 The solution is
atomized and sent into a high-temperature furnace, where the
sample undergoes instantaneously series of processes, such as
solvent evaporation, solid particle formation, particle drying,
thermal decomposition, sintering, etc., and then obtains super-
ﬁne powder materials.
35 The operation process is simple, and
the ﬁnal product can be obtained in one step. Huang et al. 34
prepared a highly e ﬃ cient catalyst of Fe 0.18Co0.82 alloy anchor-
ing on the nitrogen-doped porous carbon hollow sphere by
spray pyrolysis. Kubo et al. 36 synthesized N-doped nanoporous
carbon from ZIF-8 metal –organic framework via a one-step
ultrasonic spray pyrolysis process, achieving a speci ﬁc surface
area of 1059 m 2 g–1. Taking the characteristics of papermaking
black liquor and spray pyrolysis into account, fast spray pyroly-
sis is thus an ideal method for preparing black liquor-derived
activated carbon, as it o ﬀers environmental and energy storage
beneﬁts.
In this work, a short-process strategy based on spray pyrolysis
is developed to directly convert papermaking black liquor into
spherical hierarchical porous activated carbon. By coupling spray
pyrolysis with in situ alkali-metal activation, carbonization and
activation are achieved sequentially within a single system, elimi-
nating the multistep lignin extraction and the addition of an
external activating agent required in the conventional process.
During spray pyrolysis, the residual sodium and potassium spe-
cies in black liquor are transformed into active etching agents
and subsequently promote deep pore formation during post-
heat treatment, resulting in a hierarchical architecture composed
of thin-walled porous hollow spheres interconnected with carbon
nanosheets. The resulting material is an oxygen- and sulfur-co-
doped amorphous carbon with a high speci ﬁc surface area
(2104.3 m
2 g–1) and a large pore volume (1.139 cm 3 g–1), out-
performing the commercial activated carbon YP50 (Kuraray
Co., Ltd, Japan). When evaluated as a cathode for lithium-ion
capacitors, the optimized sample (AC700) delivers a speci ﬁc
capacity of 67.3 mAh g
–1 (121.1 F g –1)w i t h i n2 . 0–4.0 V, along
with excellent cycling stability and rate capability. In summary,
this work demonstrates an e ﬃ cient and scalable approach for
the high-value conversion of papermaking black liquor into
advanced electrode materials.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials
The black liquor was obtained from Yueyang Forest and Paper Co.,
Ltd. As listed in Table S1 , the total dissolved solids are 124 g L –1,
and the total organic carbon (TOC) is 21.3 g L –1. The concentrations
of OH -,N a +,K +, and S are 5.54, 20.83, 2.724, and 1.773 g L –1,
respectively. The concentrations of other impurities are below 0.1 g
L–1. The commercial activated carbon YP50 was purchased from
Kuraray International Trade (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.
2.2. Material Preparation
Spherical hierarchical porous activated carbon (SHPAC) was prepared
by a short process of spray pyrolysis, followed by activation, as illus-
trated in Figure 1 . Apart from water and nitrogen, papermaking black
liquor served as the sole raw material for the synthesis of SHPAC.
The black liquor was ultrasonically atomized using nitrogen as the car-
rier gas. The atomized droplets, along with nitrogen (5 L min
–1),
entered a furnace tube of a vertical furnace from the bottom and exited
from the top. The resulting product was collected by a nickel mesh
ﬁxed at the outlet of the furnace tube. The as-obtained material, com-
posed primarily of carbon and Na/K-containing compounds, was used
as the precursor for subsequent activation. High-temperature etching
activation for pore formation was carried out by utilizing the Na/K
compounds present in the precursor as an in situ activating agent.
The precursor was placed in a tubular furnace under an inert atmo-
sphere, heated to 800 °C at a rate of 3 °C min
–1, maintained for 2 h,
and then naturally cooled to room temperature. The resulting material
was washed with deionized water to remove Na 2O and K 2O, yielding
SHPAC. The precursors obtained at pyrolysis temperatures of 600,
650, and 700 °C were denoted as P600, P650, and P700, respectively.
Higher temperatures (e.g., 750 °C) were not employed because they
would signi ﬁcantly promote the water –gas reaction (reaction of H
2O
with C) during spray pyrolysis, which gasi ﬁes the carbon material
and makes the collection of any solid product nearly impossible. The
corresponding activated samples derived from P600, P650, and P700
were named AC600, AC650, and AC700, respectively.
2.3. Material Characterization
The contents of impurities in the raw material (black liquor) and ﬁnal
product were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma (ICP, Thermo
Scientiﬁc, iCAP PRO X) and a carbon –sulfur analyzer (LECO,
CS600). The morphology and elemental distribution of the samples
were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL,
JSM-IT1700HR), energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping,
and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI, Titan G2 60-300).
The microstructure was analyzed by X-ray di ﬀraction (XRD, Rigaku,
RINT-2000) and Raman spectroscopy (Renishaw, U.K.). Functional
groups and bonding information were collected by Fourier transform
infrared (FTIR, Thermo Scienti ﬁc, Nicolet iS20) spectroscopy and
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scienti ﬁc, K-

2.4. Electrochemical Tests
The working electrodes were fabricated using the as-prepared activated
carbon samples or commercial YP50 as the active material, Super P as
the conductive additive, PVDF as the binder with a mass ratio of 8:1:1.
The mixture was ground enough in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone to obtain a
uniform slurry, and then the slurry was coated on an aluminum foil and
dried in a vacuum oven overnight. The mass loading of the active mate-
rial on each working electrode was approximately 2.0 mg. CR2025 coin
cells were assembled with AC cathodes as working electrodes, lithium
foils as counter electrodes, a polyethylene membrane as a separator,
and a solution of 1M LiPF
6 in ethylene carbonate/dimethyl carbon-
ate/methyl ethyl carbonate (EC/DMC/EMC = 1:1:1 vol %) as elec-
trolytes. The speci ﬁc capacity, rate capability, and cycling stability
were determined by galvanostatic charge –discharge (GCD) tests with
a LAND CT3002A battery tester (Wuhan LAND) between 2.0 and
4.0 V. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves were recorded by an electro-
chemical workstation (CHI660D, Shanghai Chenhua).
