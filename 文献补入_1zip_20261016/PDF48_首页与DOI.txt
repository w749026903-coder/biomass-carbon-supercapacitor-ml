Unveiling the energy storage potential of valorized 
unripe plantain peel-derived activated carbon
Ayesha Samrin · Mohammed Rehaan Chandan · Igor Levchenko · Katia Alexander  · 
Mandhakini Mohandas
Received: 27 February 2026 / Accepted: 25 August 2026
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
The damage from excess waste generation on the environment has made it necessary to re-think how we 
approach product development and the waste management lifecycle. Within this framework, conversion of 
biowaste into energy storage materials has emerged as an effective means of reducing the environmental 
impact of food production. In this work, activated car-bon with superior supercapacitive properties is produced 
directly from unripe plantain peel by optimizing the use of two different activating agents KOH and ZnCl 2 dur-
ing pyrolysis. Activated carbon Z7 which was pyrolyzed at 700 °C using ZnCl 2 as the activating agent had a 
higher sur-face area of 1636 m 2/g and higher electrochemical performance when compared to activated carbon 
produced under all other experimental conditions. The specific capacitance obtained for Z7 in a two-electrode 
Swagelok set-up was 167 F g −1 at 0.5 A/g and an excellent energy density of 23 Wh/kg at a power density of 
174 W/kg at 0.5 A/g current was obtained. Further-more, a capacitance retention of 79% was achieved after 
4500 cycles at a current density of 5 A/g with a coulombic efficiency of 99%.
Keywords Unripe plantain peels · KOH and ZnCl 2 activation · Porosity · Specific capacitance
Introduction
Driven by the growing population and its increasing use of energy-intensive technologies, the global consump -
tion of energy continues to increase, at a significant cost to the environment. To slow down the depletion of fos-
sil fuels, and reduce associated emissions, there have been significant global efforts to transition to clean forms 
of energy, with a particularly strong focus on renewable energy sources, such as solar or wind. However, their 
intermittent nature as well as potential asynchronicity between peak generation and peak demand necessitates the 
development of efficient systems for energy storage, such as batteries, flywheels, supercapacitors. Supercapaci -
tor is an efficient energy storage system due to its high-power density and high stability. The energy is stored 
in a supercapacitor by two mechanisms, namely (i) Electric double layer (EDLC) and (ii) Faradaic redox reac -
tions. For a clean energy storage system, the electric double layer capacitor (EDLC) materials are highly suitable 
as they are potentially sustainable, have a longer life cycle, chemically stable and are also economical [ 6, 36].

Among promising EDLC materials, activated carbon is one of the most readily available materials with good 
supercapacitive properties [14, 18]. Although activated carbon can be produced from a variety of sources, the use 
of biowaste as the carbon feedstock offers a better choice for the environment [ 22, 38] with an activating agent 
such as KOH, NaOH, NaHCO 3, ZnCl2, HPO3, etc. typically used to chemically activate the carbon in order to 
enhance its properties [4, 28, 33]. Amongst possible biowaste feedstocks, peel from bananas has received notable 
attention due to the substantial volume of waste (almost 32% of the fruit) that is produced during consumption. 
The research has been particularly active in India, as it is the largest producer of bananas globally, according to 
the data from the National Horticulture Board, Ministry of Agriculture, Govt. of India ( https://nhb.gov.in) [34]. 
As banana peel waste contains a high amount of nitrogen and phosphorous, its disposal presents a notable envi -
ronmental hazard, whereas its prolonged decomposition of almost two years contributes to higher greenhouse 
gas emissions [1, 35].
Most of the studies conducted to date on banana peel-derived EDLC materials have focused on the reforming 
of waste from ripe bananas. For example, Tripathy et al., synthesized activated carbons with the highest specific 
capacitance value of 227 F g−1 at 1 A/g in a three-electrode setup with a capacitance retention of 97% after 5000 
cycles. The energy density for the activated carbon was 7 Wh/kg at a power density of 250 W/kg [40]. In another 
example, Lv et al., synthesized a hierarchical porous carbon foam with a specific capacitance of 206 F g−1 at 1 A/g 
and a capacitance retention of 88% [26], whereas Liu et al., prepared an n-doped activated carbon with a specific 
capacitance of 185.8 F g−1 at 5 mV/s and 210.6 F g −1 at 0.5 A/g [25]. Kandasamy et al., investigated the use of 
sulfuric acid to activate the carbon produced from the peel from a triploid cultivar of banana Musa paradisiaca, 
yielding samples with a high specific capacitance of 306.2 F g−1 at 30 mV/s (Kandasamy et al., 2016).
However, in southern parts of India, namely Kerala and Tamil Nadu, unripe plantains are widely used to 
prepare many culinary products, thus yielding a large amount of green plantain peel. While the use of the lat -
ter to prepare activated carbon have been studied for application in oil adsorption [ 41], lead(II) and 3,7-Bis 
(dimethylamino)-phenothiazin-5-ium chloride adsorption [15], reduction of carbon monoxide from biogas [ 11], 
anthracene bioadsorption [2], as a feedstock for ethanol production [46], removal of hexachlorocyclohexane iso-
mers from wastewater [9], and heavy metal adsorption [42], unripe peel-derived EDLC materials have received 
limited attention. Tadesse et al., synthesized activated carbon from raw banana peels using a two-step process 
of carbonization and chemical activation using KOH. They obtained a surface area of 565 m 2/g and a specific 
capacitance of 0.821 F g−1 [37]. Though the surface area is high the specific capacitance values were low which 
could be due to poor pore development or pore accessibility achieved in a two-step synthesis process. The self-
doping of heteroatoms and retention of biomass has been reported to be higher in a single-step process than a 
two-step process as there is biomass loss and also heteroatoms tend to etch out during the activation process [24].
Unripe plantain peels were selected as the biowaste precursor in this work as it has many advantages such as 
high availability, large yield and good scalability when compared to previously reported biomass materials. The 
unripe plantain peels have more rigid and stable lignocellulosic structures containing cellulose, hemicellulose 
and lignin which yields higher carbon content during thermal treatment. Furthermore, it has lower sugar content 
and less volatile content than ripe plantain peels reducing tar formation and structural collapse during thermal 
treatment. These rigid structures have low microbial degradation and are highly suitable for pore development 
and increased char formation required for easy handling and scalable production. Additionally, the plantain peels 
have nitrogen content naturally present paving way to self-doping of nitrogen [3].
This work aimed to optimize the synthesis process to obtain activated carbon with a high surface area and 
higher specific capacitance value from unripe plantain peel in a single step. To do that, a single step carboniza -
tion with activation by either KOH or ZnCl2 was chosen, and the surface and electrochemical properties of thus-
produced materials were analyzed for energy applications. The impregnation ratio was selected as 2:1, where 2 is 
the mass of activating agent and 1 is the mass of the carbon source based on its performance in our previous work 
[33]. Such pyrolysis strategy based on chemical activation enables the direct transformation of unripe plantain 
peel, a structurally complex agricultural biowaste, into high-performance nanostructured carbon, where the use

Experimental section
The unripe plantain peel was collected from a local savory shop in Chennai, India. The peel was cut into small 
pieces and washed thoroughly in water first and then in ethanol and acetone (Fig. 1). The washed peel was drained 
well and dried in a hot air oven at 100 °C for 48 h until completely crisp dry. The resulting dried material was then 
used as a carbon feedstock. Potassium hydroxide and Zinc chloride were used as activating agents and purchased 
from SRL chemicals, Chennai.
In each experiment, 20 mg potassium hydroxide was mixed in 50 ml distilled water until the salt dissolved 
completely. Then, 10 mg of dried plantain peel (2:1 impregnation ratio, where 2 is the mass of activating agent 
and 1 is the mass of the carbon source) was added into the activating agent solution. The dried peel was then 
allowed to fully soak in solution under sonication for 2 h, at which point the soaked peel was transferred into a 
hot air oven and kept at 110 °C overnight until completely dry. Once dried, the peel was transferred to an alumina 
boat and subjected to pyrolysis at 700 °C/800°C for 2 h under nitrogen atmosphere. Thus-obtained activated
