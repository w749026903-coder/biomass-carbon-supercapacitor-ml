ABSTRACT: Pesticide residues in aquatic systems pose persistent risks to
ecosystems and public health, underscoring the need for low-cost and
sustainable remediation strategies. In this study, biochars prepared from
Conocarpus branch waste (CBB) and lemon tree branch waste (LBB) via
slow pyrolysis at 450 °C were evaluated as adsorbents for two widely
applied agrochemicals: imidacloprid (IMI) and myclobutanil (MYC).
Comprehensive physicochemical characterization (SEM, TEM, BET, and
elemental analysis) revealed mesoporous structures with distinct surface
features. Batch adsorption experiments demonstrated strong dependence
on solution pH, adsorbent dosage, contact time, and initial pesticide
concentration. Under near-neutral conditions (pH 7.0, 5 g L
−1
, 25 °C, 24
h), maximum capacities reached 8.69 mg g
−1
(IMI) and 12.31 mg g
−1
(MYC) for CBB. Adsorption kinetics followed a pseudo-second-order
model (R
2
> 0.98), while equilibrium data fit both Langmuir and the Freundlich isotherms (R
2
≥ 0.99). Thermodynamic analyses
(ΔG° = −1.3 to −4.5 kJ mol
−1
; ΔH° = −16.4 to −29.8 kJ mol
−1
) indicated spontaneous, exothermic processes dominated by
physisorption. The reusability of both biochars across multiple cycles further confirmed their practical applicability. These results
highlight pruning-waste biochars as effective and sustainable alternatives to conventional adsorbents for mitigating pesticide
pollution in water.
1. INTRODUCTION
Pesticides play a vital role in modern agriculture, serving as
essential tools for controlling pests and diseases while
significantly enhancing crop yields and ensuring food security.
1

demand for food production is expected to increase,
necessitating a greater reliance on pesticides to sustain
agricultural output.
2

widespread use of these products has raised serious environ-
mental and health concerns. As pesticides are extensively
applied, their residues and degradation byproducts can
infiltrate aquatic ecosystems through spray drift, surface runoff,
and leaching, posing substantial threats to water quality,
biodiversity, and human health.
3,4

exacerbates these concerns, creating long-term environmental

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Preparation and Characterization of Biochar. Air-
dried pruning residues from Conocarpus and lemon trees were
sorted to remove dust, bark fragments, and leaves; the branch
fractions only were retained for pyrolysis. Sorted material was
washed with deionized water, oven-dried at 105 °C to constant
mass, cut to 2−3 cm, and then pyrolyzed at 450 °C for 4 h

temperature, the resulting biochars were mechanically ground
in a clean stainless-steel grinder and sieved to <190 μm to
standardize particle size for adsorption tests (details mentioned
in Supporting Information 2.2). Then subjected to phys-
icochemical characterization. Surface morphology was exam-
ined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (FEI Inspect
S50, Netherlands), elemental composition by energy-dispersive
X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Bruker, Germany), and surface
area and porosity by Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET)
nitrogen adsorption−desorption analysis (Belsorp Max II
Microtrac, Japan).
2.2. Batch Adsorption Experiments. Batch adsorption
experiments were conducted to evaluate the removal of
imidacloprid (IMI) and myclobutanil (MYC) from aqueous
solutions. Parameters examined included pH (4−10),
adsorbent dosage (1−10 g L
−1
), contact time (10 min−24
h), and initial pesticide concentration. All experiments were
performed in triplicate at 25 ± 1 °C with continuous shaking
to ensure equilibrium conditions.
2.3. LC-MS/MS Quantitation and Method Validation.
Quantification was performed by UHPLC−MS/MS (triple
quadrupole, MRM) on a C18 column with water/methanol
(each containing 1% formic acid + 5 mM ammonium formate)
under gradient elution. Matrix-matched calibration and quality
controls were used to correct for matrix effects. Method
validation (linearity, LOD/LOQ, precision) followed standard
acceptance criteria aligned with SANTE/11312/2021. Full
operating conditions and MRM transitions are provided in the
Supporting Information (2.10. LC-MS/MS). Method valida-
tion metrics are presented in Table 1.
2.4. Data Analysis and Modeling. Removal efficiency
was calculated based on the difference between initial and
equilibrium concentrations, and adsorption data were fitted to
kinetic (pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order) and equili-
brium isotherm (Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubi-
nin−Radushkevich) models. Thermodynamic parameters
(ΔG°, ΔH°, ΔS°) were derived from temperature-dependent
adsorption experiments. Comprehensive experimental proto-
cols, instrument settings, calibration data, and model-fitting
procedures are provided in the Supporting Information.

molecules, whereas LBB provides marginally more external
surface for adsorption.
Elemental analysis further revealed that LBB had a higher
carbon content (61.84%) than CBB (52.15%), reflecting
greater carbon richness, which may enhance its capacity for
carbon sequestration or adsorption of polar species. Particle
size distributions were broadly similar, with LBB containing
slightly finer particles (minimum 5 μm) than CBB (minimum
8 μm). Thus, CBB’s wider pore network favors the adsorption
of larger molecules, whereas LBB’s higher carbon fraction may
promote stronger carbon-based interactions.
The particle size distribution, determined by laser diffraction
(Malvern Mastersizer 2000E) after ultrasonic dispersion in
deionized water, indicated mean particle diameters of
approximately 8 μm for CBB and 5 μm for LBB, confirming
that LBB consisted of slightly finer particles. In contrast, the
pore size distribution derived from N
2
adsorption−desorption
data using the Barrett, Joyner, and Halenda (BJH) method
(Quantachrome Nova 2200e or similar high-precision (BET)
surface area analyzer) confirmed the mesoporous nature of
both materials (Figure 2). Thus, “particle size” refers to the
external physical dimensions of ground biochar particles,
whereas “pore size” describes the internal porous network.
Nitrogen adsorption−desorption isotherms (Figure 2)
confirmed the presence of mesoporosity in both materials,
displaying Type IV curves with H3 hysteresis loops. CBB
showed a sharper increase in nitrogen uptake at high relative
pressures (P/P
0
> 0.8), indicating the presence of larger
mesopores and a broader distribution. LBB, in contrast,
exhibited a smoother uptake profile, consistent with narrower
pores. These textural variations highlight that adsorption
efficiency will differ depending on pesticide molecular size.
SEM micrographs (Figure 3) provided additional insights
into morphology. CBB displayed a rough, porous surface with
interconnected voids, consistent with its larger pore volume.
LBB surfaces appeared smoother with smaller, rounded pores
at low magnification, and fibrous, compact features at higher
magnification. These differences underline the suitability of
CBB for the adsorption of bulky molecules, while LBB’s denser
morphology may favor applications involving smaller species or
catalysis.
