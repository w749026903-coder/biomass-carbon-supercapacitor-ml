Potassium Bicarbonate-Assisted Pore Regulation of 
Corn-Straw-Derived Interconnected Porous Carbon for 
Supercapacitors 
Xiaodong He1 Zhidong Sun1 Zilong Zhou1 Fanen Zeng2 Chenglong Jing1 Bing Xu2 
1 School of Transportation Engineering, Dalian Jiaotong University, Dalian, China 2 School of Materials Science and Engineering, Dalian Jiaotong University,

Keywords: corn straw | KHCO3 activation | porous carbon | supercapacitor 
ABSTRACT 
Biomass-derived porous carbons are promising electrodes for sustainable supercapacitors, but their performance depends on 
balancing charge-storage sites and ion-transport pathways. Herein, corn straw was converted into interconnected porous carbon 
through mild KHCO3 -assisted activation. The effects of activation temperature and holding time on pore architecture and 
capacitive behavior were comparatively evaluated, and the surface chemistry of the best-performing sample was further examined. 
CSK-750-2, prepared at 750◦C for 2 h, exhibited a hierarchical porous structure containing micropores and mesopores with a 
specific surface area of 1701 m2 g− 1 . Micropores provided abundant surface area for electric double-layer charge storage, whereas 
mesopores facilitated electrolyte-ion transport, and surface oxygen-containing groups may provide a limited Faradaic contribution. 
CSK-750-2 delivered 157 F g− 1 at 0.5 A g− 1 with 86% capacitance retention at 5 A g− 1 . The symmetric supercapacitor achieved 10.5 
Wh kg− 1 at 500 W kg− 1 and approximately 106% capacitance retention after 10,000 cycles. These results highlight the importance 
of balancing micropore-based charge storage and mesopore-assisted ion transport in biomass-derived carbon electrodes. 
1 Introduction 
The increasing demand for efficient energy conversion and 
storage, together with growing environmental concerns, has 
stimulated extensive research into sustainable electrochemical 
energy-storage technologies. Among various energy-storage sys- 
tems, rechargeable batteries generally provide relatively high 
energy density, whereas electrochemical capacitors, particularly 
supercapacitors, have attracted considerable attention because 
of their high power density, rapid charge–discharge capability, 
long cycling life, and operational safety [ 1–8 ]. Recent advances 
in graphene-based and flexible electrochemical energy-storage 
systems have further demonstrated the importance of elec- 
trode architecture, surface chemistry, and electrode–electrolyte 
interactions in determining energy-storage performance [ 9–11 ]. 
Carbon materials have been widely investigated as supercapac- 
itor electrodes because of their physicochemical stability, large 
specific surface area, favorable charge-transport characteristics, 
and tunable pore architecture [ 12–18 ]. In particular, biomass- 
derived porous carbons are promising electrode candidates 
because of their renewable origin, abundant availability, low cost, 
environmental compatibility, and adjustable surface chemistry. 
Engineered biochar and biomass-derived carbon materials have 
also received increasing attention for sustainable energy and 
environmental applications [ 19 ]. 
The capacitive behavior of porous carbon is strongly depen- 
dent on its pore architecture. Micropores mainly provide ion- 
accessible surface area and charge-storage sites, whereas meso- 
pores facilitate electrolyte-ion transport between the external

serve as ion-buffering reservoirs and shorten transport pathways 
[ 20 ]. Consequently, neither a high specific surface area nor a 
large pore volume alone necessarily guarantees high capacitance. 
Instead, an appropriate balance between accessible micropores 
for charge storage and larger pores for ion transport is required to 
achieve favorable electrochemical performance. Therefore, ratio- 
nal regulation of hierarchical porosity is essential for the effective 
conversion of agricultural and forestry wastes into high-value 
porous carbon electrodes for supercapacitor applications. 
Chemical activation is one of the most widely employed 
approaches for developing porous structures in biomass-derived 
carbons. Strong alkaline activators such as KOH and NaOH are 
commonly used because of their high etching capability and 
pore-forming efficiency. Nazhipkyzy et al. prepared chemically 
activated carbons from karagash and pine sawdust, obtaining 
specific capacitances of 147 and 114 F g− 1 , respectively [ 21 ]. Le Van 
and Luong Thi demonstrated that NaOH activation effectively 
promoted pore development in rice-husk-derived carbon [ 22 ], 
Singh et al. reported biomass-derived activated carbon with a 
specific capacitance of 115 F g− 1 for electric double-layer capacitor 
applications [ 23 ], while Perez-Chavez et al. further compared 
KOH and H3 PO4 activation of corn-husk- and agave-bagasse- 
derived carbons, demonstrating the important influence of the 
activating agent on electrochemical performance [ 24 ]. These 
studies demonstrate the effectiveness of strong alkaline activation 
in developing porous carbon structures. However, the strong 
alkalinity and corrosiveness of KOH and NaOH can increase 
equipment-corrosion risks and post-treatment requirements. 
Moreover, excessive activation may induce severe carbon etching, 
structural deterioration, or an unbalanced pore-size distribution, 
such that the resulting high surface area cannot be fully utilized 
electrochemically. 
Alternative pore-regulation strategies have also been investi- 
gated. Bandara et al. incorporated TiO2 into activated carbon 
derived from Jack wood biochar, obtaining a specific capacitance 
of 147 F g− 1 [ 25 ], whereas Diantoro et al. reported sponge- 
like activated carbon derived from Manihot esculenta tubers 
with a gravimetric capacitance of 146.6 F g− 1 [ 26 ]. Hoffmann 
et al. demonstrated the feasibility of converting potato waste 
into carbon electrodes for supercapacitor applications [ 27 ]. Hor 
and Hashmi prepared hierarchical porous carbon from biomass 
pollen cones through ZnCl2 -assisted activation, achieving capac- 
itances of approximately 126–146 F g− 1 [ 28 ]. ZnCl2 activation 
has also been applied to biomass-derived biochar, including 
corn-cob-derived carbon [ 29 ]. Other activation routes, such as 
molten-salt-assisted carbonization, have also been explored to 
construct porous carbon structures for supercapacitor electrodes 
[ 30 ]. Lee et al. further modified activated carbon through nitric- 
acid treatment and obtained a capacitance of 106 F g− 1 in an 
all-solid-state supercapacitor [ 31 ]. Recent studies have further 
demonstrated the electrochemical potential of biomass-derived 
carbons prepared from different biomass sources and through 
different modification strategies [ 32–34 ]. Collectively, these stud- 
ies indicate that pore development, morphology regulation, and 
surface chemistry can all influence capacitive performance. How- 
ever, approaches involving additional inorganic components, 
metal salts, or strong acids can increase the complexity of material 
preparation and subsequent purification. These considerations 
motivate the development of simpler and milder activation 
strategies capable of simultaneously regulating pore formation 
and preserving the carbon framework. 
Compared with strongly alkaline or metal-salt-based activation 
routes, KHCO3 has emerged as a relatively mild activating 
agent for porous-carbon preparation. During thermal treat- 
ment, KHCO3 decomposes into potassium-containing species 
and gaseous products, which promote carbon etching, pore 
generation, and pore expansion. Such activation can facilitate 
the development of interconnected hierarchical porous structures 
while reducing the likelihood of severe framework destruction. 
Compared with KOH and NaOH, KHCO3 exhibits lower alka- 
linity and corrosiveness, whereas its use also avoids the direct 
introduction of Zn-containing activation residues associated with 
ZnCl2 -based processes [ 28, 35 ]. Therefore, KHCO3 -assisted activa- 
tion provides a promising strategy for balancing pore generation, 
structural preservation, chemical consumption, and environmen- 
tal compatibility. Nevertheless, the relationships among KHCO3 
activation conditions, hierarchical pore evolution, surface chem- 
istry, and the electrochemical behavior of agricultural-waste- 
derived porous carbons remain insufficiently clarified. 
Corn straw (CS), an abundant agricultural residue, contains 
carbon-rich components and intrinsic longitudinal channel- 
like structures, making it a promising precursor for porous 
carbon electrodes. However, the direct conversion of corn 
straw into interconnected porous carbon through a simple 
KHCO3 -assisted activation route, without the use of additional 
templates, transition-metal-based activators, or intentional het- 
eroatom dopants, remains relatively underexplored. In particular, 
the effects of activation temperature and holding time on pore 
architecture, and electrochemical behavior require further clar- 
ification. In this work, CS-derived porous carbons (CSKs) were 
prepared through a KHCO3 -assisted activation strategy, and 
the effects of activation temperature and holding time were 
separately evaluated using a one-factor-at-a-time approach by 
correlating morphology, structural disorder, pore characteristics, 
and electrochemical performance, while the surface chemistry 
of the best-performing sample was further examined. Among 
the investigated conditions, CSK-750-2, prepared at 750◦C for 2 
h, exhibited the most favorable combination of pore structure 
and electrochemical response, delivering a specific capacitance 
of 157 F g− 1 at 0.5 A g− 1 . The present work focuses on the direct 
construction of interconnected porous carbon from corn straw 
through mild KHCO3 activation without additional templates 
or transition-metal-based activators. Particular attention is given 
to the balance between micropore-based charge storage and 
mesopore-assisted ion transport, as well as the possible contri- 
bution of surface oxygen-containing functionalities to capacitive 
behavior. These findings provide a relatively mild and simple 
route for the valorization of corn straw into porous carbon 
electrodes for supercapacitor applications.

2.2 Synthesis of Porous Carbon Materials 
The preparation procedure for CS-derived porous carbon mate- 
rials is illustrated in Figure 1 . Prior to use, the collected corn 
straw was thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove 
surface dust and impurities, followed by drying at 60◦C for 
12 h. The initial moisture content of the raw corn straw was 
approximately 39 wt.%. After drying, the corn straw was crushed 
and passed through a 200-mesh sieve to obtain relatively uniform 
particles. The pretreated corn straw was mixed with KHCO3 at a 
fixed mass ratio of 1:1 and subsequently subjected to ultrasonic 
treatment for 30 min to improve the uniformity of the mixture. 
The 1:1 mass ratio was selected as a fixed activation condition and 
was maintained throughout the experiments because the present 
study focused on the effects of activation temperature and holding 
time rather than activator dosage. The mixture was then dried in 
an oven at 60◦C for 12 h. The dried mixture was transferred into a 
tubular furnace and heated to the target temperature at a heating 
rate of 5◦C min− 1 under a continuous N2 flow of 100 mL min− 1 . 
Using a one-factor-at-a-time approach, the effect of activation 
temperature was investigated at 700, 750, and 800◦C with the 
holding time fixed at 2 h, whereas the effect of holding time was 
investigated at 1, 2, and 3 h with the activation temperature fixed 
at 750◦C. After activation, the products were naturally cooled 
to room temperature under a continuous N2 atmosphere and 
subsequently washed with 1 mol L− 1 HCl and deionized water 
until the filtrate reached neutral pH. Finally, the washed products 
were dried at 60◦C for 12 h. The obtained samples were denoted as 
CSK-X-Y, where X and Y represent the activation temperature and 
holding time, respectively. For comparison, CSC was prepared by 
carbonizing the pretreated corn straw at 750◦C for 2 h under the 
same N2 atmosphere without the addition of KHCO3 . 
2.3 Characterization Methods 
The micromorphology of the CS-derived porous carbon materials 
was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS 
Sigma 300). The phase composition and crystal structure were 
examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Empyrean X) over a 2 θ
range of 10◦–80◦and further identified by comparison with 
standard PDF cards. Raman spectra were collected using a Raman 
spectrometer (XploRA PLUS) in the range of 500–2000 cm− 1 to 
analyze the structural features of the samples. X-ray photoelec- 
tron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi) was 
performed to investigate the elemental composition and chemical 
states. In addition, N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms were 
measured at 77 K using a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 surface area 
and porosity analyzer. Prior to the measurements, the samples 
were degassed under vacuum at 300◦C for 8 h. The specific surface 
area (SBET ) was calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller 
(BET) method. The micropore surface area (Smic ) and micropore 
volume (Vmic ) were determined using the t-plot method. The 
pore-size distribution was derived from the N2 adsorption data 
using an N2 -DFT model. The total pore volume (Vtotal ) was 
estimated from the amount of N2 adsorbed at a relative pressure 
(P/P0 ) of approximately 0.99. The external surface area (Sext ) was 
obtained from the t-plot analysis, while the mesopore volume 
(Vmes ) was calculated as Vtotal − Vmic . 
2.4 Electrochemical Measurements 
Nickel foam sheets were ultrasonically treated with hydrochloric 
acid, absolute ethanol, and deionized water for 10 min, followed 
by washing and drying. The mass of the cleaned nickel foam 
sheet was recorded as m1 . The prepared porous carbon material, 
BP2000, and PTFE were ground into a slurry at a mass ratio of 
8:1:1; the slurry was then coated onto the nickel foam sheet and 
dried at 60◦C for 12 h [ 33 ]. The mass of the dried electrode was 
recorded as m2 . Accordingly, the effective mass of active carbon 
loaded on the nickel foam sheet was calculated as M = (m2 − m1 ) 
×0.8, with the loading mass controlled within 3–5 mg [ 36 ]. The 
nickel foam sheet was pressed at 10 MPa to obtain the working 
electrode. 
Electrochemical measurements were carried out on a CHI660 
electrochemical workstation in 6 M KOH electrolyte using a 
three-electrode system, in which Hg/HgO, platinum mesh, and 
the active-carbon-loaded electrode served as the reference elec- 
trode, counter electrode, and working electrode, respectively [ 37 ].
