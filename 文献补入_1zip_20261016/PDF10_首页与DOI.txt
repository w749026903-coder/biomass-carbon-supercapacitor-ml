3 Faculty of Information Technology and Communication Sciences, Tampere University,

Keywords: activated carbon, birch wood, NaOH activation, pore structure, supercapacitors, 
aqueous electrolyte
The valorization of biomass residues into advanced carbon materials offers a promising route 
toward more sustainable supercapacitor technologies. Birchwood was evaluated as a 
sustainable precursor for activated carbon electrodes in aqueous supercapacitors. Carbon was 
produced via carbonization, grinding, and activated at 700 or 800 °C using varying quantities 
of NaOH. Structural characterization (N₂ sorption at 77 K, XRD, Raman, SEM, elemental 
analysis, XPS) indicated specific surface areas up to 2401 m 2 g-1 and a temperature‑dependent 
pore architecture: predominantly microporous at 700 °C and mixed micro/mesoporous at 
800 °C. Electrochemical performance was assessed in symmetric CR2032 coin cells with a 
phosphate buffer electrolyte, using CV and GCD. The highest specific capacitance reached 
247 F g-1, exceeding that of commercial carbon Kuraray YP-80F by up to 115% under given 
test conditions. Although more pronounced graphitic structures develop after treatment at 
800 °C and may enhance conductivity, they also coincide with reduced surface area and 
consequently do not improve capacitance. Samples activated at 700 °C generally delivered 
superior capacitance and competitive rate capability, likely due to enhanced microporosity 
which increases the accessible surface area for electric double‑layer formation. These results 
suggest birchwood is a viable high‑value feedstock for carbon-based supercapacitor electrodes.

1. Introduction
Supercapacitors have been widely researched due to their advantageous properties as energy 
storage devices - high power density, rapid charge-discharge rates, and long cycle life. 1 They 
play a key role in bridging the gap between traditional dielectric capacitors and batteries or fuel 
cells. Consequently, they are used in diverse technologies such as electric traction drives, wind, 
solar and wave energy generation systems, and microgrid applications.2,3 
However, supercapacitors have notable shortcomings limiting their potential applications. They 
generally have a lower energy density than batteries, as the charge storage is limited to surfaces 
or near the active surface area of the electrodes. 1 Carbon-based materials are considered ideal 
candidates for supercapacitor electrodes because of a combination of various beneficial 
properties and the ability to modify these properties. These include high specific surface area, 
good electrical conductivity, chemical stability, low cost, and abundant availability.4
In recent years, research on supercapacitors has increasingly focused on raising energy density 
while retaining fast charge-discharge and long cycle life. While commercial carbon‑based 
EDLCs can range from 5 to 11 Wh kg -1, many studies now target ≥10-20  Wh kg -1 without 
sacrificing >90% capacitance retention over tens of thousands of cycles.5–8 
Progress in carbon materials has been central to this shift. Beyond maximizing surface area, 
current work emphasizes hierarchical pore architectures – micropores that store charge 
combined with small mesopores that shorten ion pathways – as well as heteroatom doping to 
improve wettability and interfacial charge storage. Representative examples from existing 
studies include eucalyptus‑leaf‑derived activated carbons, which delivered 196  F g -1 when 
measured in a three‑electrode configuration by cyclic voltammetry at 5 mV s -1 in 2M KOH, 
while N‑doping increased the capacitance to 258  F g -1 under the same conditions 9. Another 
study reported porous carbons derived from a mixture of corncob and fly ash waste, achieving 
240  F g-1 at 1 A g-1 in a three‑electrode setup in 6M KOH electrolyte.10 In addition, hierarchical

Activated carbons (ACs) produced from wood and other biomass sources have shown potential 
for use in energy storage devices, particularly supercapacitors. 4,15 This can be related to their 
role as environmentally friendly and sustainable alternatives to traditional fossil fuel-based 
precursors such as coal and petroleum. 16–18 The utilization of biomass waste, e.g. agricultural 
by-products, offers a low-cost, abundant, and readily available raw material source, which 
lowers production costs and provides economic incentives.15,16,19–21 
Another abundant waste material in Europe and a notable carbon source is wood sawdust. For 
example, Sweden produces approximately 1.9 million tons of sawdust annually22 as a byproduct 
from various sawmills. Converting these waste materials into high-value activated carbons 
supports the development of diversified products and contributes to a circular bioeconomy.15,23 
During the carbonization and activation processes, biomass develops a high specific surface 
area and an abundant pore structure, making it a good candidate for electrode materials in 
supercapacitors.20,24,25 The unique structure inherent to some biomass types, such as wood, can 
facilitate efficient electrolyte transport and ion diffusion, which are important for 
electrochemical performance.26
