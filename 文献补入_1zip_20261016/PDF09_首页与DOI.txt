ABSTRACT
Biomass‐derived carbons are eco‐friendly and sustainable materials, making them ideal for supercapacitors due to their high
surface area, excellent conductivity, cost‐effectiveness, and environmental benefits. This review provides valuable insights into
biomass‐derived carbon and modified carbon for supercapacitors, integrating both experimental results and theoretical cal-
culations. This review begins by discussing the origins of biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors, including plant‐based,
food waste‐derived, animal‐origin, and microorganism‐generated sources. Then, this review presents strategies to improve the
performance of biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors, including heteroatom doping, surface functionalization, and hybrid
composite construction. Furthermore, this review analyzes the functions of biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors both in
its pure form and as modified materials. The review also explores composites derived from biomass‐based carbon, including
carbon/MXenes, carbon/MOFs, carbon/graphene, carbon/conductive polymers, carbon/transition metal oxides, and carbon/
hydroxides, providing a thorough investigation. Most importantly, this review offers an innovative summary and analysis of the
role of biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors through theoretical calculations, concentrating on four key aspects: energy
band structure, density of states, electron cloud density, and adsorption energy. Finally, the review concludes the future
research directions for biomass carbon‐based supercapacitors, including the discovery of novel biomass materials, tailoring
surface functional groups, fabricating high‐performance composite materials, exploring ion transfer mechanisms, and
enhancing practical applications. In summary, this review offers a thorough exploration of the sources, functions, and mech-
anisms of biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors, providing valuable insights for future research.

make environment‐friendly and renewable energy storage so-
lutions for harnessing sources, such as wind, solar, and tidal
energy, prompted by rising fossil fuel consumption and envi-
ronmental degradation [ 1–3 ]. Among the key electrochemical
energy storage technologies are batteries and supercapacitors
[ 4–6 ]. Although batteries could offer high energy density,

cycle life, necessitating regular replacements, and their chemi-

Rapid charging and discharging capabilities allow super-

deliver immediate energy output, meeting diverse high‐power
demands [12]. Their quick charge and discharge features
render supercapacitors applicable across various areas,
including transportation, renewable energy, industrial machin-
ery, consumer electronics, emergency power solutions, military
uses, aerospace, and broader energy storage systems [13].
Supercapacitor electrodes demand materials with excellent
electrical conductivity and a large specific surface area to
enhance capacitance and energy storage efficiency [14, 15].
Candidates with high electrochemical performance include
various carbon nanomaterials (such as activated carbon, carbon
nanotubes, and graphene), metal oxides (such as Fe

Recently, biomass has emerged as a crucial resource for pro-
ducing carbon materials used in supercapacitors [23, 24]. Unlike
other carbon sources, biomass‐derived carbon offers distinct
benefits, including abundant supply, renewability, natural pore
structures, enhanced safety, and affordability. These materials
demonstrate superior specific capacitance and notable rate
performance due to their abundant active sites and improved
conductivity [25–27]. Electrochemical energy is stored in
biomass‐derived carbon through reversible adsorption and
desorption of charged species at the carbon electrode–electrolyte
interface, facilitating rapid energy transfer within seconds or
even microseconds [28]. The porosity and structure of these
materials can be modified via various activation techniques,
influencing ion diffusion rates in supercapacitors. Biomass
sources encompass a wide range of waste and byproducts from
plants, animals, and other organic matter while maintaining
sustainability and cost‐effectiveness [29].
Using biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors provides
several advantages over traditional carbon materials, particu-
larly in terms of sustainability, structural features, electro-
chemical performance, and cost‐effectiveness. (1) Sustainability
and environmental benefits. Carbon materials produced
through conventional methods often rely on nonrenewable
fossil‐based precursors and the use of hazardous chemicals,
resulting in a significant environmental impact [30]. Compared
with traditional carbon materials, biomass carbons are derived
from renewable sources such as agricultural waste, forestry
residues, and food byproducts. This contrasts with traditional
carbons, which often rely on nonrenewable fossil‐based pre-
cursors such as coal, petroleum coke, or synthetic polymers [31].
(2) Cost‐effectiveness. The synthesis processes for traditional
carbon need expensive precursors and complex equipment,
making the production process costly [32]. Compared with
traditional carbon materials, biomass feedstocks (e.g., coconut
shells, sawdust, corn cobs, and rice husks) are inexpensive or
even free, as they are often agricultural or industrial byproducts
[33]. The production of biomass carbon generally involves
simpler processes, such as pyrolysis and activation, which are
less costly than the high‐temperature graphitization or chemical
vapor deposition (CVD) techniques often required for tradi-
tional carbons [34]. (3) Rich hierarchical porous structure.
Carbon materials produced via conventional methods may lack
the hierarchical porous structure and functional groups that are
often inherent in biomass‐derived carbons. These features in
biomass‐derived carbons enhance electrochemical performance
in applications such as supercapacitors [35]. Biomass‐derived
carbons often feature naturally occurring hierarchical pore
structures (micro‐, meso‐, and macropores). These structures
promote efficient ion transport and electrolyte accessibility,
enhancing supercapacitor performance [36]. During the
carbonization process, the organic composition of biomass (e.g.,
cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin) facilitates the formation of
interconnected pores without the need for complex templating
techniques [37]. (4) Richer surface functionalities. Compared
with traditional carbon materials, Biomass carbons are more
easily doped with heteroatoms, such as nitrogen, sulfur, or
phosphorus, either through the natural composition of biomass
or post‐synthesis treatments [38]. Doping enhances the con-
ductivity, wettability, and electrochemical activity of the carbon
material [39]. In addition, biomass‐derived carbons naturally
retain oxygen‐containing groups (–OH, –COOH, and –C=O)
during carbonization, which improve electrolyte wettability and
enables pseudocapacitive charge storage via faradaic reactions
[40]. (5) Lower environmental and energy costs. Unlike tradi-
tional graphitization, which requires temperatures above
2000°C, biomass carbonization typically occurs at moderate
temperatures (500–900°C), reducing energy consumption [41].
The activation of biomass carbon often uses safer and less toxic
agents (e.g., KOH and ZnCl
2
) compared to the hazardous
chemicals sometimes used in traditional carbon processing [42].
Carbon materials derived from pure biomass store energy pri-
marily through surface adsorption and desorption, which is
evident in the triangular or triangular‐like shapes observed in
their CV curves (Figure 1A–D). Consequently, electric double‐
layer capacitors (EDLCs) are developed utilizing biomass‐
derived carbon as active electrodes [43]. Nevertheless, EDLCs
exhibit lower capacitance compared to other energy storage sys-
tems. Thus, biomass‐derived carbon is utilized as a framework,
substrate, or intercalation material for synthesizing composites
(pseudocapacitors or battery materials), facilitating the creation
of high‐capacitance devices such as pseudocapacitors and hybrid
capacitors. Pseudocapacitor materials store energy through sur-
face redox reactions, which are characterized by prominent redox
peaks in the CV curves (Figure 1B–E). In contrast, battery ma-
terials demonstrate high capacitance from deep redox reactions
within the lattice of active components indicated by sharp redox
peaks in the CV curves (Figure 1C–F) [44].
