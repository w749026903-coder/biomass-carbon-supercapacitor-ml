RESEARCH ARTICLE
Unveiling the Crosslinking Architecture Governed Carbon 
Yield in Phenolic Resin-Derived Porous Carbons for 
Cost-Effective Energy Storage 
Zhaojin Li Chenze Di Di Zhang Huilan Sun Qujiang Sun Qiujun Wang Fei Yuan Ranran Li 
Bo Wang
Hebei Key Laboratory of Flexible Functional Materials, School of Materials Science and Engineering, Hebei University of Science and Technology, Hebei, China 
Correspondence: Huilan Sun ( sunhl@hebust.edu.cn) Bo Wang ( wangbo@hebust.edu.cn) 
Received: 4 June 2026 Revised: 23 July 2026 Accepted: 25 July 2026 
Keywords: activated carbon | carbon yield | crosslinking degree | phenolic resin | porous carbon | supercapacitor 
ABSTRACT 
Porous carbons are indispensable for supercapacitors and as hosts for silicon anodes in next-generation lithium-ion batteries, 
yet their commercialization is crippled by the low carbon yield of phenolic resin precursors. What fundamentally controls the 
yield and how to improve it have long puzzled both academia and industry. Here, we address the root cause by systematically 
tuning the formaldehyde-to-phenol (F/P) molar ratio. We discover that the methylene bridge density in the cured resin is the key 
determinant of carbon yield. At the optimal F/P ratio of 2.0, the resin achieves the most complete crosslinked network, boosting 
the porous carbon yield from below 36% (PC-1.0) to 47.15% (PC-2.0), without compromising pore development. PC-2.0 retains a 
high specific surface area of 2580.6 m2 ⋅g− 1 and delivers an outstanding specific capacitance of 371.8 F ⋅g− 1 at 0.5 A ⋅g− 1 . Moreover, 
it exhibits a high capacitance retention of 95.76% after 10 000 cycles at a current density of 10 A ⋅g− 1 , demonstrating competitive 
advantages over various electrode materials reported in recent years. Quantitative analysis confirms a strong positive correlation 
between methylene content and yield, solving the long-standing puzzle. This work provides a simple, scalable strategy to break 
the yield-performance trade-off, reducing raw material cost by 16.47% compared with commercial resin. 
1 Introduction 
Against the backdrop of the escalating global energy crisis 
and worsening environmental degradation, the development 
of advanced and sustainable electrochemical energy storage 
technologies has become pivotal to achieving the societal goal 
of carbon neutrality [ 1–4 ]. The pursuit of carbon neutrality 
has driven the rapid diversification of electrochemical energy 
storage technologies, ranging from alkali metal-ion batteries 
[ 5, 6 ], supercapacitors [ 7 ], and aqueous zinc-ion batteries to 
emerging systems such as ammonium-ion batteries [ 8–11 ], 
hybrid-ion batteries [ 12 ], and zinc-ion hybrid capacitors [ 13–15 ], 
as well as biofuel-based energy conversion devices [ 16 ]. In many 
of these systems, porous carbons, owing to their high specific 
surface area (SSA), tunable pore structure, excellent electrical 
conductivity, and good chemical/thermal stability, demonstrate 
irreplaceable potential in these fields [ 17, 18 ]. Porous carbon 
not only serves as a catalyst support for biofuel production, 
reducing catalyst deactivation [ 19 ], but also functions as an ideal 
electrode material for electrical double-layer capacitors (EDLCs) 
in supercapacitors, delivering high power density and ultra- 
long cycle life [ 20 ], and additionally acts as an excellent host 
for silicon-based anodes, mitigating the volume expansion dur- 
ing charge/discharge processes, among other applications [ 21 ]. 
However, despite its immense performance potential, the com- 
mercialization of phenolic resin-based porous carbon remains

severely constrained by the high cost of raw materials. Compared 
with inexpensive precursors such as biomass or coal, the phenolic 
resin route allows for more precise control over pore structure 
and yields superior electrochemical performance [ 22, 23 ]. Never- 
theless, it holds a relatively low market share, primarily because 
of the higher precursor cost. More importantly, the generally low 
carbon yield during pyrolysis and activation directly inflates the 
unit production cost of the final material, thereby weakening its 
competitiveness in price-sensitive application scenarios. 
For a long time, the research community has devoted extensive 
efforts to enhancing the electrochemical performance of porous 
carbons, with strategies primarily focused on heteroatom doping 
to introduce pseudocapacitance, fine-tuning of pore structure 
through template methods or activator regulation, and optimiza- 
tion of pyrolysis and activation conditions [ 24–27 ]. Although 
these efforts have improved the specific capacitance or ion 
transport kinetics of the materials to a certain extent, they have 
generally overlooked a more fundamental bottleneck for indus- 
trialization: the low carbon yield of phenolic resin precursors 
during carbonization. The excessively low yield means that a 
large amount of raw material is lost in the form of volatile 
matter during the conversion process, which not only results in 
resource waste but also directly leads to a significant increase in 
the cost of the final product. A deeper core scientific question 
thus emerges: what factors fundamentally determine the carbon 
yield of phenolic resin? How can the carbon yield be improved 
while ensuring that the critical porous structure and electro- 
chemical performance are not significantly compromised? These 
two fundamental questions have become key obstacles limiting 
the large-scale application of high-performance porous carbons 
in the energy storage industry. Existing studies have mostly 
focused on post-carbonization modifications, with relatively little 
attention paid to the resin synthesis stage. Investigating the 
chemical structure of the precursor itself and how it affects its 
thermal stability and resistance to etching during subsequent 
high-temperature treatment may be the key to unraveling the 
mystery of carbon yield. 
In response to this long-standing conundrum, the present study 
proposes the following hypothesis: the final carbon yield of 
phenolic resin-based porous carbon is likely predetermined by the 
crosslinking network integrity of its precursor, and, in particular, 
by the degree of methylene ( ─CH2 ─) bridging. To verify this 
hypothesis, we deliberately forgo complex doping or composite 
strategies and instead adopt an exceedingly simple, fundamental, 
and highly controllable synthesis parameter, the formaldehyde- 
to-phenol molar ratio (F/P), which serves as a core lever to 
systematically regulate the crosslinking degree of the resin. By 
varying the F/P ratio, we aim to alter, at the molecular level, 
the crosslinking extent and methylene bridge content of the 
resin network. We employ a combination of characterization 
techniques, including Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy 
(FT-IR), solid-state 13 C nuclear magnetic resonance (ssNMR), 
and nanoindentation testing, to quantitatively characterize the 
crosslinking structure and mechanical properties of the resins 
prepared at different F/P ratios. The distinct advantage of this 
strategy is that it does not rely on expensive additives or complex 
post-treatment processes, thereby inherently possessing natural 
feasibility and economic viability for industrial scale-up from the 
outset. 
Herein, by systematically designing a series of phenolic resins 
with different formaldehyde-to-phenol molar ratios and employ- 
ing ssNMR and FT-IR spectroscopy, this study reveals the 
key structural parameters governing the porous carbon yield, 
elucidates the underlying mechanisms, and ultimately provides 
a simple, efficient, and universally applicable strategy for the 
rational design and large-scale production of low-cost, high- 
performance porous carbon electrode materials, thereby serving 
practical energy storage applications such as supercapacitors and 
silicon anodes for lithium-ion batteries. 
2 Experimental Section 
2.1 Experimental Materials 
Phenol (C6 H5 OH, 99%), formaldehyde (CH2 O, 37%), ammonia 
solution (28%), and hydrochloric acid (37%) were purchased from 
Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Activated 
carbon was purchased from Fujian Xinsen Carbon Industry Co., 
Ltd., and commercial phenolic resin was purchased from Hebei 
Zetian Chemical Co., Ltd. 
2.2 Synthesis of Phenolic Resin 
Phenolic resins were synthesized from phenol (P) and formalde- 
hyde (F) by varying the molar ratio of formaldehyde-to-phenol. 
In a typical synthesis, 23.5 g (0.25 mol) of phenol was added 
into a round-bottom flask and heated to 80◦C in a water bath. 
Then, 1.5 mL of ammonia solution (28%) was added, followed by 
the addition of a specific volume of 37% formaldehyde solution 
according to the desired molar ratio, for example, 37.5 mL for a 
formaldehyde-to-phenol ratio of 2:1. The mixture was stirred and 
heated at 80◦C for 2 h, and subsequently subjected to vacuum 
distillation at 60◦C to remove impurities. The resulting resins 
were designated as PF- x , where x represents the molar ratio of 
formaldehyde-to-phenol ( x = 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0). Finally, 
the resins were transferred into PTFE beakers and cured by 
sequential heating at 80◦C for 1 h, 120◦C for 1 h, and 160◦C for 
3 h. After curing, the resins were ground to obtain resin particles. 
2.3 Synthesis of Porous Carbon 
The porous carbon was synthesized via a two-step process 
consisting of pre-carbonization and KOH activation. Briefly, 
2 g of the as-prepared phenolic resin was placed in a tube 
furnace and heated to 600◦C at a rate of 5◦C ⋅min− 1 under Ar 
atmosphere, and held at this temperature for 3 h. After cooling, 
the resulting char was mixed with KOH at a mass ratio of 1:3 
and ground thoroughly to obtain a homogeneous mixture. The 
mixture was then transferred to a tube furnace and heated to 
800◦C at 5◦C ⋅min− 1 under an Ar atmosphere, held for 3 h, and 
subsequently cooled to 300◦C at a rate of 5◦C ⋅min− 1 , followed by 
natural cooling to room temperature. The product was washed 
repeatedly with hydrochloric acid and deionized water, and 
finally dried in an oven at 70◦C for 8 h. The resulting porous 
carbons were designated as PC- x , where x corresponds to the 
molar ratio of formaldehyde-to-phenol in the precursor resin 
( x = 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0). For comparison, porous carbon

2.4 Characterization 
The surface morphology of the samples was characterized by 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission elec- 
tron microscopy (TEM). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDS) was employed to determine their elemental composition. 
Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and nuclear 
magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy provided insights into 
the chemical environment, while X-ray diffraction (XRD) was 
used to analyze the crystalline structure of the materials. Raman 
spectroscopy was utilized to evaluate the degree of disorder 
and graphitization. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was 
employed to monitor the heat released during resin curing. The 
specific surface area (SSA) and pore size distribution of the 
samples were determined using nitrogen as the adsorption gas 
at 77 K, based on the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) theory 
and the density functional theory (DFT) model, respectively. 
Nanoindentation was used to measure the hardness and elastic 
modulus of the samples to evaluate their mechanical stability.

3 Results and Discussion 
3.1 Synthesis and Structural Characterization 
To elucidate the influence of the formaldehyde-to-phenol ratio 
on the porous carbon yield, a series of phenolic resins were 
synthesized by varying the formaldehyde proportion and subse- 
quently converted into porous carbons. The overall preparation 
procedure is illustrated in Figure 1a . The synthesis of the 
phenolic resin mainly involves the addition reaction between 
phenol and formaldehyde, followed by the polycondensation 
of hydroxymethyl derivatives [ 28, 29 ], while the porous carbon 
fabrication consists of two steps, pre-carbonization and KOH 
activation. To ensure the accuracy of the yield determination, 
each resin was subjected to six replicate experiments, and the 
average yield was calculated after eliminating the maximum and 
minimum values. The individual yields of the pre-carbonization 
and activation steps were also recorded, as presented in Figure 
1b–d and Tables S2–S4 . With increasing formaldehyde content, 
the overall porous carbon yield first increased and then decreased. 
The maximum yield of 47.15% was achieved at a F:P molar ratio 
of 2:1, and a similar trend was observed in the pre-carbonization 
step, where the resin with F:p = 2:1 delivered the highest pre- 
carbonization yield of 64.7%. In contrast, the activation yields 
varied only slightly among the different resins, remaining in the 
range of 70%–72%. Regarding the final yields, the value for PC-2.5 
was slightly lower than that for PC-3.0. This can be explained by 
the fact that in PF-3.0, the presence of excess formaldehyde leads 
to a more disordered structure, which offers greater resistance 
to KOH etching during activation. The XRD pattern in Figure 
S1 also confirms this point. PF-3.0 exhibits a slightly larger full 
width at half maximum (FWHM) in the 10–30◦range than PF- 
2.5, indicating its relatively more disordered crosslinked structure 
[ 28 ]. Meanwhile, PC-2.5 gave a higher pre-carbonization yield, 
so the amount of KOH added in the activation step was slightly 
larger for this sample, resulting in deeper etching. Consequently, 
the porous carbon yield of PC-3.0 was marginally higher than that 
of PC-2.5. 
The morphology and microstructure of the porous carbons were 
further characterized by SEM. Taking PC-2.0 as a representative 
example (Figure 2a ), the KOH-activated porous carbon exhibited 
irregular block-like particles with abundant defect structures on 
the surface. These etched defects are beneficial for exposing 
more active sites [ 22 ]. EDS mapping revealed that both C and 
O elements were uniformly distributed across the porous carbon 
surface (Figure S2 ). The TEM images in Figure 2b,c show that 
after activation, PC-2.0 possessed a highly disordered “worm- 
like” carbon layer arrangement with numerous micropores 
[ 30, 31 ]. The XRD pattern (Figure S3 ) further confirmed the low 
graphitization degree of the material: a typical amorphous carbon 
peak appeared at 2 θ∼ 44◦, which is assigned to the (100) plane 
of carbon materials [ 32, 33 ], indicating that the resin-derived 
porous carbon belongs to the typical hard carbon category. In 
the Raman spectra (Figure S4 ), all samples exhibited high AD /AG 
ratios, which also corroborates this finding. To characterize the 
pore structure of the porous carbons and to compare their 
performance with that of a commercial activated carbon (PC- 
C) and a porous carbon prepared from a commercial resin 
using the same activation procedure, N2 adsorption-desorption 
measurements were performed. As shown in Figure 2d , according 
to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry 
(IUPAC) classification [ 34 ], all the resin-derived porous car- 
bons exhibited type I isotherms, indicating a predominantly 
microporous structure [ 35 ]. The sharp uptake at low relative 
pressures (P/P0 < 0.1) corresponds to micropore filling [ 36 ]. The 
PC-C sample displayed a type IV isotherm with a pronounced 
hysteresis loop, characteristic of a hierarchically porous material 
with a multimodal pore size distribution. The SSA of PC-1.0, 
1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0 was 2620.3, 2187.4, 2580.6, 2426.1, and 2557.9 
m2 ⋅g− 1 , respectively (Table S5 ). PC-1.0 exhibited the highest SSA, 
which may be attributed to the relatively unstable structure of 
its precursor resin; during pre-carbonization, PF-1.0 underwent 
greater mass loss, thereby pre-forming more pores. However, 
from a practical perspective, PC-1.0 is not the optimal choice 
because of its very low yield. In contrast, PC-2.0, which showed 
the highest carbon yield, possessed an SSA only slightly lower 
than that of PC-1.0 and significantly higher than those of PC-C 
and PC-B, demonstrating excellent application potential. Besides, 
PC-1.0 and PC-2.0 displayed relatively higher cumulative pore 
volumes (Table S5 ), which are favorable for more ion adsorption 
[ 37 ]. Notably, PC-1.5 exhibited the lowest SSA among all resin- 
derived porous carbons. We suggest that this may be explained 
by its relatively moderate crosslinking degree: unlike PC-1.0, 
whose loosely crosslinked structure underwent substantial mass 
loss during pre-carbonization and thus pre-formed abundant
