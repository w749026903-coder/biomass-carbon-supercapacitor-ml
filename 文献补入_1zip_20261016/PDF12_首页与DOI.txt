Recovery of Cathode and Solid Electrolyte in All-Solid-State Sodium-

ABSTRACT: Metals in spent all-solid-state sodium-ion batteries
(ASIBs) pose severe environmental pollution, resource depletion,
and health risks. However, selective and sustainable recovery of
these metals from ASIBs remains highly challenging. Herein, we
introduce a coordination-solvation dual-regulation (CSDR) mech-
anism that enables, for the first time, the recovery of both the
cathode and solid electrolyte from ASIBs with exceptional
selectivity and high sustainability. Guided by this CSDR
mechanism, amino acid−based green solvents are employed with
a dual function: abundant functional groups provide coordination
sites for target metal ions while facilitating favorable solvation
interactions, collectively enabling metal leaching. Mechanistically,
the superior Na over Zr selectivity originates from differential
binding energetics, Na
+
forms stable chelate complexes with amino acid ligands with lower binding free energies, whereas Zr
4+
,
owing to its higher charge density and stronger hydrolysis tendency, exhibits weaker coordination affinity and poorer solvation
compatibility, thus remaining preferentially in the solid residue.

In recent years, novel green solvent systems, such as ionic

Herein, we propose a coordination-solvation dual-regulation
(CSDR) mechanism using amino acid−based LoMMSs for the
selective recovery of metals from spent ASIBs. NMF and
NZSP were selected as the typical cathode and solid
electrolyte, respectively. Amino acids (Figure 1a) are chosen
as the hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs) for LoMMSs due to
their green and biodegradable nature, as well as the ability of
their amino and carboxyl groups to coordinate effectively with
metals. Notably, the functional amino and carboxyl groups in
HBAs not only offer strong coordination sites for metals but
also influence the solvation effect, enabling fine-tuning of metal
ion solubility and selectivity. By balancing coordination and
solvation regulation, the CSDR mechanism achieves remark-
able metal selectivity, thereby allowing efficient separation of
target metals from both the cathode and solid electrolyte.

2.5. Antisolvent Precipitation
The antisolvent precipitation was conducted by mixing 2 mL of
leachate with 2 mL of antisolvent, allowing the mixture to stand at
room temperature for 24 h, and then centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for
20 min to collect the precipitate. The supernatant was analyzed by
ICP-OES for metal ion concentration. The reuse of leachate was
conducted under the conditions of a solid-to-solvent ratio of 1:50 and
at 80 °C for 24 h.
