ABSTRACT: The development of high-performance, low-cost super-
capacitors holds significant importance for the use of renewable energy.
However, enhancing their energy density without compromising their
inherent properties remains a formidable challenge. In this study, the
method of flame-induced oxidation is introduced to enhance the
wettability and porosity of lignin-based carbon nanomaterial. The results
of FTIR, XRD, XPS, and Raman spectroscopy confirmed the
effectiveness of flame-induced oxidation. The finally obtained carbon
nanomaterial possesses a specific surface area of 497.84 m
2
g
−1
and
abundant heteroatom content (O: 7.3%, N: 6.9%, and S: 1.5%). As a
result, the assembled supercapacitors demonstrated an energy density of 26.45 W h kg
−1
at a power density of 800 W kg
−1
. The
Trasatti method and ion diffusion analysis reveal that the outstanding energy storage properties are attributed to the synergistic effect
of enriched heteroatom content and developed nanopore structure. This work introduces an approach for designing carbon material
with appropriate pore size and heteroatom content to develop high-performance supercapacitors.
KEYWORDS: flame-induced, lignin, nanomaterial, supercapacitors, carbon
1. INTRODUCTION
The effect of the greenhouse has intensified, leading to an
increased focus on environmental protection. As a result,
various new energy sources such as wind energy, solar energy,
and tidal energy have been vigorously developed.
1−4
However,
these new energy sources are often restricted by the natural
environment during power generation and require energy
storage devices for peak load treatment. Supercapacitors have
emerged as a core component of large-scale energy storage
systems due to their ultrahigh power density, fast charge/
discharge rate, excellent cycle stability, etc.
5−7
They play a
crucial role in the process of new energy generation and peak
regulation. Nevertheless, the relatively low energy density and
pollutant resources of supercapacitors cause a demand to
reduce the cost for their widespread use.
8−11
Therefore, it is
imperative to improve their electrochemical performance
without compromising their inherent excellent characteristics.
Lignin-based carbon nanofibers (CNFs) have emerged as a
novel type of carbon nanomaterial, which usually displays a
high specific surface area, large length-breadth ratio, good
mechanical properties, and excellent electrical conductiv-
ity.
12−14
Currently, CNF has been proven to possess great
potential in energy storage, electromagnetic shielding, sensors,
and triboelectricity. In particular, the use of CNF as an active
nanomaterial in supercapacitors has garnered significant
attention from researchers. For example, CNF based on lignin
composites has demonstrated the merits of low-cost, renew-
able, and ultralong cyclability.
13−15
To further boost the
electrochemical performance of lignin-based CNFs, it is
efficient to increase the operating voltage by using an
asymmetric assembly. Heteroatoms doped carbon nanoma-
terial has larger specific capacitance suitable for use as negative
material (capacitive-type electrode) as the power source, and
the pseudocapacitive metal oxides as positive electrode
material (Faraday electrode) as the energy source.
5,6,8,9
Due
to the combination of the two storage mechanisms,
asymmetric supercapacitors can make full use of the different
working potential windows of the two electrodes, thereby
increasing the energy storage capacity. The use of activation or
incorporation methods to improve the porosity of electrode
materials is another efficient strategy to improve the electro-
chemical performance.
16−18
However, most of these methods
typically require complicated processes, toxic chemicals, and
high temperatures, which result in significant risks for large-
scale production and subsequent scalable applications. Inspired
by plasma activation processes,
19,20
we propose that flame can
be considered a combustible gas and high-temperature flame

In this study, we present a clean and rapid method for the
flame-induced oxidation of activated CNFs. Compared with

and practical applications in the high-value utilization of lignin
and the establishment of energy systems.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Material. Reed cane was provided by Shengquan Group
Share Holding Co., LTD (Jinan, China). Polyacrylonitrile (PAN),
N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), hydrochloric acid (HCL
aq
), and
KOH were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., LTD (Shanghai,
China). Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), ethanol, and carbon black
were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich Trading Co., Ltd. All chemical
reagents were of analytical grade and used as received.
2.2. Preparation of Lignin. Lignin was extracted by the sulfate
pulping process, and the specific steps are as follows. 150 g of reed
cane was added to an electric heating digester equipped with a 4 × 1 L
stainless-steel reactor at 170 °C and kept there for 120 min, the
heating rate was 1 °C min
−1
, the amount of alkali was 18% (measured
in Na
2
O), and the sulfide held for 30%. After the reaction, the
obtained mixture was cooled to room temperature and strained with
gauze. The resulting filtrate was poured into four volumes of
deionized water, and the pH was adjusted to 2 using hydrochloric
acid. After settling for 12 h, the mixture was filtered, the filter cake was
washed to neutral by deionized water, and the sulfate lignin was
obtained by lyophilization under −40 °C of 36 h.
2.3. Fabrication of Activated Electrode Material. The
preparation steps of activation lignin-based carbon nanofiber
(ACNF) were as follows: 3 g of Lignin, 2 g of PAN, and 25 g of
DMF were mixed in a 100 mL conical flask, and the mixture was
heated at 60 °C with magnetic stirring for 24 h. The resulting solution
was using an electrospinning machine (TL-Pro, Tongli Micronano-
Technology Co., Ltd. China) to produce precursor nanofiber (PNF)
with an applied voltage of 18 kV (+10 kV and −8 kV) and a feed rate
of 0.5 mL h
−1
. The obtained PNF was carefully removed from the
collector and dried at 50 °C under a vacuum. Subsequently, the PNF
was heated in a Muffle furnace (LX0711, Tianjin labotery instrument
Co., LTD) with a heating rate of 1 °C min
−1
to 300 °C and held for
120 min to obtain stabilized nanofiber. The SF is further carbonized
to 1000 °C in a tube furnace (GSL-1700X, Hefei Kejing material
technology Co., LTD, China) under N
2
atmosphere to obtain CNF.
Finally, 1 g of CNF and 10 mL of KOH (40 g L
−1
) were mixed
uniformity by ultrasonic treatment, and evaporate the water, which is
then heated to 600 °C in a tube furnace with a heating rate 5 °C
min
−1
, and the product was washed and dried to obtain the ACNF.
Detailed preparation steps are shown in Figure 1.
The oxidation process of ACNF was conducted using a flame as
follows: A piece of ACNF sheet was securely held above a commercial
alcohol burner (the combustion liquid is ethanol with a concentration
of 95%, purchased from Xiangbo Glass Co., LTD). The oxidation
treatment involved exposing the ACNF above the flame envelope for
10 s, followed by removal from the flame and cooling for 30 s. The
flame-induced oxidation process occurs in an atmospheric environ-
ment under standard air pressure. The flame envelope temperature of
the alcohol lamp is approximately 520 °C, and the flame length
measures about 34 mm with a thickness of around 16 mm when the
alcohol lamp burns fully. To avoid the potential problem of decreased
material homogeneity caused by mismatch in size between the flame
and the reactant, we control the reactant size within 0.8 × 0.8 cm
2
to
ensure that the reactant is completely enveloped by the flame
envelope during the flame-induced oxidation, as shown in Figure S1a.
This process was repeated three times to obtain flame-oxidized
ACNF, referred to as F-ACNF. Under this condition, lignin-based
CNFs begin to oxidize above 400 °C and reach their ignition point
around 600 °C. Therefore, the application of the alcohol lamp flame
to induce oxidation in this experiment will not result in a combustion

2.5. Electrochemical Measurements. The electrochemical
performance of ACNF/F-ACNF-based electrodes and supercapacitors
were characterized in CHI 660E electrochemical workstation by using
the technology of cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge−
discharge (GCD), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy
(EIS). The electrode material was obtained by combining the active
material (ACNF or F-ACNF), polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), and
carbon black in ethanol in a weight ratio of 90:5:5. And then, 2−3 mg
of electrode material were loaded on the surface of treated nickel foam

electrode. Two pieces of electrodes were assembled to form a
sandwich structure with glass microfiber filters as a membrane to
obtain supercapacitors, and the electrochemical properties of devices
were conducted in a two-electrode system. More detailed calculation
formulas of electrochemical performance, ion diffusion coefficient of
electrodes, and Trasatti analysis method are referred to Supporting
Information and our previous studies.

more electrochemical reaction sites, thereby enhancing the
electrochemical properties.

through EDX is presented in Figure 3e, where a uniform
distribution of purple, red, and blue dots indicates homoge-

following flame-induced oxidation without escape. Highly
electronegative N can activate sp
2
hybrid carbon atoms, while S
can adjust electron configuration and polarization to increase
electrochemically active sites. The synergistic effect between N
and S leads to redistribution of spin density and charge density,
as well as enhanced electrochemical activity.
The TEM of ACNF and F-ACNF is shown in Figure 4a,c,
respectively. The ACNF sample demonstrates a compact
structure in Figure 4a, with fiber diameters ranging from 350 to
450 nm. After flame-induced oxidation, the diameter of the F-
ACNF fibers decreases to between 250 and 350 nm, as shown
in Figure 4c. Additionally, numerous white spots are evident in
the TEM image of F-ACNF, which likely arise from pores
formed when amorphous carbon oxidizes into small molecular
gases that subsequently escape. HRTEM images indicate that
both samples exhibit distinct dark and light graphite stripes,
which suggest substantial graphitization of lignin following
high-temperature carbonization. This transformation results in
an amorphous carbon structure characterized by short-range
order and long-range disorder. A comparison of Figure 4b,d
reveals that the graphite microcrystals in ACNF are
approximately 10 nm thick, while those in F-ACNF appear
thicker, indicating that the graphitic microcrystalline layers in
F-ACNF have undergone an expansion phenomenon.
Furthermore, the arrangement of graphite microcrystals in
ACNF is more ordered than that in F-ACNF, suggesting a
higher degree of graphitization for ACNF. The elemental
mapping of F-ACNF, as illustrated in Figure 4e, demonstrates
that elements C, N, O, and S are uniformly distributed
throughout the electrode material. This uniform distribution
indicates that the nanoscale composition of F-ACNF
comprises C, N, O, and S elements. Furthermore, this even

enhancing the cycling stability of the electrode material.

The wettability of the electrode material holds a pivotal role
in their electrochemical performance. Enhanced wettability can
facilitate the electrolyte into the internal electrode material,
thereby increasing the number of active sites that can react
with electrolyte ions and concurrently minimizing charge
transfer resistance. As depicted in Figure 5b, the contact angle
of the aqueous electrolyte on the electrode material’s surface
undergoes a significant reduction, from 114.1° in ACNF to
27.8° in F-ACNF following flame-induced oxidation. This
noteworthy reduction in the contact angle can be attributed to
two primary factors. First, the increased content of
heteroatoms within the electrode material leads to a
strengthened hydrogen bonding force with the aqueous
electrolyte, effectively reducing the solid−liquid interface.
Second, the bombardment of flame ions on the carbon
material enhances the porosity and surface roughness of the F-
ACNF, thereby enlarging the contact area between electrode
material and electrolyte. This augmentation in the surface area
further intensifies the interaction force between the material
and the liquid surface, rendering the electrolyte solution more
permeable. These combined effects contribute significantly to
the improved wettability observed in the F-ACNF.
The XRD patterns of ACNF and F-ACNF are presented in
Figure 5c, revealing two distinct peaks positioned at
approximately 2θ of 25 and 44°, corresponding to (002) and
(100) crystal planes within the graphite crystals, i.e. edge
carbon structures and graphitic carbon structures, respectively.
The layer spacing of the electrode material, both before and
after flame-induced oxidation, is accurately calculated by using
the Bragg equation. Notably, the distances between the (002)
crystal plane (d
002

0.3604 for F-ACNF, suggesting an expansion in the graphite
layer spacing of the electrode material postflame-induced
oxidation. This expansion is expected to enhance the
electrolyte particle infiltration into the electrodes. Additionally,
the diffraction peak of F-ACNF exhibits a broader width
compared with that of ACNF, suggesting a reduction in grain
size and crystallinity. There is an increase of edge carbon
content in F-ACNF, which also disrupts the crystal structure of
carbon material as well as heightens the likelihood of lattice
distortion. These defects compromise the periodicity of the
crystal structure, resulting in a broadening of the diffraction
peak.

oxidation temperature, directly impacting the electrochemical
performance of electrode materials. If future research identifies
a more stable flame source that ensures complete combustion,
this approach will offer valuable insights for the commercial
applications of lignin-based carbon materials in the field of
energy storage, especially in terms of durability and functional
effectiveness.
