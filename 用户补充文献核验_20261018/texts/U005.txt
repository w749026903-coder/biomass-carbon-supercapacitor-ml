ABSTRACT 
Biomass-derived porous carbon has emerged as a sustainable electrode material for supercapacitor applications. In this work, 
activated carbon (AC) was prepared from Eucalyptus seeds through a simple KOH activation process using an abundant and 
renewable biomass precursor. The synthesized carbon exhibited a hierarchical porous structure with a specific surface area of 
610.248 m2 g− 1 . Electrochemical measurements in 2 M KOH delivered specific capacitances of 266 F g− 1 in a three-electrode 
configuration and 120 F g− 1 for the symmetric device in a two-electrode configuration at 0.5 A g− 1 . Dunn’s kinetic analysis revealed 
that charge storage was predominantly governed by surface-controlled capacitive processes, while the porous architecture and 
oxygen-containing surface functional groups promote efficient electrolyte transport and ion adsorption. The assembled symmetric 
supercapacitor delivered a maximum energy density of 11 Wh kg− 1 at a power density of approximately 200 W kg− 1 . It retained 6 Wh 
kg− 1 at approximately 1200 W kg− 1 , and maintained 76.5% capacitance retention after 10,000 charge–discharge cycles. The device 
also successfully powered five red LEDs for 13 s after a 10 s charging period, demonstrating its practical energy-storage capability. 
These findings indicate that Eucalyptus seed-derived AC is a sustainable and promising electrode material for supercapacitor 
applications. 
1 Introduction 
Recently, electric vehicles (EVs) have garnered significant atten- 
tion due to their potential to enhance energy efficiency and 
reduce environmental impact. At the core of EV technology lies 
the electrochemical energy storage system, with the lithium- 
ion batteries (LIBs) currently dominating the landscape owing 
to their high operating voltage, superior energy density, and 
capability to deliver extended driving ranges [ 1 ]. Despite these 
advantages, LIBs are hindered by several intrinsic limitations, 
such as reduced storage capacity at low temperatures, rela- 
tively short cycle life, and high production costs. To overcome 
these challenges, supercapacitors have emerged as a promising 
alternative energy storage technology, offering exceptional cycle 
stability, ultrafast charge–discharge rates, and broad operational 
temperature adaptability [ 2 ]. Supercapacitors are classified into 
pseudocapacitors and electrochemical double-layer capacitors 
(EDLCs) based on their underlying charge storage mecha- 
nisms. Pseudocapacitors store charge through fast and reversible 
Faradaic redox reactions occurring at the electrode-electrolyte 
interface. In contrast, EDLCs operate via a non-Faradaic method 
wherein charge storage is facilitated by the electrostatic adsorp- 
tion and desorption of ions at the interface between the electrode 
material and electrolyte [ 3–7 ]. Among the two capacitors, EDLCs

Advancements in energy storage and conversion technologies 
critically depend on the development of high-performance, 
cost-effective materials sourced from renewable and abundant 
resources [ 11 ]. Carbon materials, owing to their excellent thermal 
stability, tunable surface chemistry, high electrical conductivity, 
and adjustable porosity, have emerged as key candidates for 
addressing challenges in environmental remediation and energy 
storage applications. However, conventional synthesis routes 
for functionalized carbon structures such as porous carbon, 
carbon nanospheres, carbon nanotubes, carbon quantum dots, 
and graphene oxide are typically energy-intensive, environmen- 
tally detrimental, and reliant on nonrenewable feedstocks like 
coal and petroleum derivatives [ 12 ]. While effective, techniques 
such as chemical vapor deposition and graphite arc discharge 
incur high production costs and contribute to environmental 
pollution. Although laser etching has recently shown potential, 
its dependence on fossil-based precursors limits its scalability 
[ 13 ]. Therefore, the development of novel, sustainable, and scal- 
able synthesis strategies is imperative. One promising approach 
involves utilizing bio-waste, an abundant and renewable resource 
generated daily, to fabricate eco-friendly carbon materials tailored 
for electrochemical energy systems [ 14 ]. 
EDLCs primarily utilize carbon-based materials to facilitate 
charge storage through electrostatic accumulation at the elec- 
trode and electrolyte interface. Among many carbon-based 
materials, biomass-derived activated carbon (AC) stands out 
as a highly competitive candidate for supercapacitor electrode 
applications, owing to its hierarchical porous architecture, 
adjustable specific surface area, favorable electrical conductivity, 
and excellent electrochemical performance with outstanding 
cyclic stability. Moreover, biomass as a precursor offers significant 
advantages, as it is a renewable, sustainable, and environmentally 
benign resource, enabling a cost-effective and scalable approach 
for fabricating high-performance carbon electrodes. Therefore, 
the development of carbon materials derived from biowaste is 
encouraged by a sustainable, simple, and economical method for 
synthesizing AC [ 15 ]. 
Several biomass-derived carbon materials have been investigated 
for supercapacitor applications, exhibiting promising electro- 
chemical performances attributed to their hierarchical porosity 
and high surface areas. Carbon derived from a rose branch 
exhibited a high specific surface area of 1803 m2 g− 1 and delivered 
a specific capacitance of 175 F g− 1 at 0.2 A g− 1 in 6 M KOH 
electrolyte [ 16 ]. Similarly, AC synthesized from waste palm 
achieved a surface area of 1755 m2 g− 1 and a capacitance of 
164 F g− 1 at 0.5 A g− 1 using a PVA/LiCl gel electrolyte [ 17 ]. 
Cyperus pangorei-based carbon, with a moderate surface area of 
711.2 m2 g− 1 , delivered 194.6 F g− 1 at 0.5 A g− 1 in 1 M Na2 SO4 
electrolyte [ 18 ]. Eucalyptus bark and Eucalyptus globulus seed- 
derived carbons demonstrated high surface areas of 1721 and 
2388.38 m2 g− 1 , respectively, exhibiting capacitances of 155 F g− 1 
in 1 M NaClO4 and 150 F g− 1 in 1 M KOH at 1 A g− 1 [ 19, 20 ]. 
Additionally, carbon from Eucalyptus wood (surface area: 1817 m2 
g− 1 ) achieved an impressive capacitance of 230 F g− 1 in 2 M NaCl 
at 1 A g− 1 [ 21 ]. Decayed tomato-derived carbon, although having 
a lower surface area of 880.33 m2 g− 1 , showed a capacitance of 
110 F g− 1 in PVA/H2 SO4 gel electrolyte [ 22 ]. Java kapok tree shell- 
based carbon (surface area: 1260 m2 g− 1 ) delivered 169 F g− 1 at 1 
A g− 1 in 6 M KOH [ 23 ]. Other notable examples include pecan 
nutshell (731 m2 g− 1 , 130 F g− 1 ) [ 24 ], switchgrass (599 m2 g− 1 , 
50 F g− 1 ) [ 25 ], walnut shells (724 m2 g− 1 , 187 F g− 1 ) [ 26 ], and 
carrot slices (180 m2 g− 1 , 90 F g− 1 ) [ 27 ], all tested in 6 M KOH 
electrolyte. Despite these advances, the exploration of seed-based 
biomass, particularly from fast-growing and abundantly available 
plant species, remains relatively limited. Eucalyptus is a widely 
cultivated, fast-growing plant species, and its seeds are largely 
underutilized agricultural by-products. The intrinsic organic 
composition of Eucalyptus seeds, rich in carbonaceous content, 
makes them a promising precursor for porous carbon materials. 
However, to date, there are very few systematic studies reporting 
the conversion of Eucalyptus seeds into AC and evaluating their 
performance as supercapacitor electrodes. 
There are two methods for converting the precursor materials into 
AC: physical activation and chemical activation. AC is prepared 
in the physical activation method using an activation gas, such as 
CO2 , steam, or a combination of gases. The carbon precursor from 
biomass is combined with chemical reagents like H3 PO4 , KOH, 
NaOH, FeCl3 , ZnCl2 , and H2 SO4 during the chemical activation. 
KOH is chosen over other activating chemicals in the chemical 
activation to prepare porous AC due to its superior effectiveness 
and environmental friendliness [ 28 ]. AC treated with KOH is 
obtained by igniting the precursor in an inert gas atmosphere. 
It produces a larger specific surface area and pore size than AC 
precursors without KOH activation [ 29–33 ]. 
In the present study, porous AC was synthesized from discarded 
Eucalyptus globulus seeds via direct KOH-assisted activation 
without hydrothermal pretreatment. While the leaves of Euca- 
lyptus globulus are widely utilized for essential oil extraction, the 
seeds are generally discarded as agricultural waste with limited 
value-added utilization [ 34 ]. In this context, the discarded seeds 
were explored as a sustainable biomass precursor for the prepa- 
ration of porous AC. The prepared material was comprehensively 
characterized to establish the relationship between its structural, 
textural, morphological, and surface chemical properties and 
its electrochemical performance. The electrochemical behavior 
was systematically evaluated in a three-electrode configuration 
using a 2 M KOH electrolyte over a range of current densities 
and scan rates. Furthermore, the practical applicability of the 
prepared AC was assessed in a symmetric two-electrode super- 
capacitor device and demonstrated through LED illumination. 
This study provides further insight into the relationships among 
synthesis strategy, physicochemical characteristics, and elec- 
trochemical performance, thereby contributing to the rational 
design of sustainable biomass-derived porous carbon electrodes 
for electrochemical energy storage applications.

been reported for supercapacitor applications [ 19 ], the present

strategy without hydrothermal pretreatment. It thoroughly 
assesses the relationship between the structural, textural, mor- 
phological, and surface chemical characteristics of the prepared 
AC and its electrochemical performance. In addition to evalu- 
ating the intrinsic electrochemical behavior in a three-electrode

2 Materials and Experimental Section 
2.1 Materials 
Biomass Eucalyptus seeds collected from the campus, NIT Waran- 
gal, Telangana, India. Potassium hydroxide (KOH), hydrochloric 
acid (HCl), Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), N -Methyl pyrroli- 
done (NMP), and a graphite sheet with 1 mm thickness, purity 
of 99%, were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. 
2.2 Synthesis of Porous Activated Carbon Using 
Eucalyptus Seed 
Activated carbon derived from Eucalyptus seeds through chemi- 
cal activation and subsequent carbonization underwent a metic- 
ulous preparation process. Initial purification involved using 
deionized water (DI) and anhydrous ethanol to cleanse the har- 
vested Eucalyptus seeds, which were sun-dried to eliminate resid- 
ual contaminants. The desiccated seeds underwent a thermal 
treatment within a muffle furnace set at 200◦C for 2 h, yielding a 
finely powdered form through grinding with a mortar and pestle. 
The resultant powder was subjected to chemical treatment using 
potassium hydroxide (KOH) in a 1:2 ratio with the biomass, fol- 
lowed by a 24 h aging period at room temperature. Subsequently, 
as an integral step in the carbonization process, the chemically 
activated powder underwent heating in a tubular furnace at 
700◦C for 2 h. A continuous stream of nitrogen was introduced 
into the tubular furnace at a rate of 100 mL/min to maintain an 
oxygen-free environment during this thermal treatment. Upon 
completion of the heating process, the powder was extracted from 
the tubular furnace after allowing it to cool to room temperature. 
The resulting sample underwent further purification, involving 
successive washes with DI water and a 1 M HCl solution. The 
latter aimed to neutralize the pH of the filtrate. The purified 
material was then subjected to drying and grinding processes, 
producing a refined AC derived from Eucalyptus seeds (shown in 
Figure 1 ), ready for subsequent utilization in diverse applications. 
2.3 Characterizations 
The crystalline, chemical compositional, and structural infor- 
mation of the prepared sample was examined using an x-ray 
diffraction pattern (XRD; X’pert powder) with Cu Kαradiation ( λ
= 1.5418 Å ), an x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy spectrum (XPS), 
and Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; ZEISS 
EVO18), respectively. The specific area and pore size distribution 
information was obtained using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller 
(BET) and Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH; Autosorb-iQASIQ) 
techniques. FTIR spectroscopy (ALPHA-II make BRUKER) and 
Raman spectroscopy (an Olympus BX51) were also used to study 
the functional groups of AC and its D and G bands. 
2.4 Electrochemical Characterization 
The electrochemical properties of activated carbon (AC) were sys- 
tematically investigated at ambient temperature using an aqueous 
2 M KOH electrolyte, employing two and three-electrode con- 
figurations. All measurements were conducted using a MULTI 
AUTOLAB M204 electrochemical workstation. The working 
electrode was fabricated by blending AC, carbon black, and PVDF 
in a mass ratio of 8:1:1. To facilitate slurry formation, a minimal 
amount of N -methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) was introduced as a 
solvent. The resulting slurry was uniformly coated onto a 1 × 1 
cm2 area of a 1 ×2 cm2 graphite substrate using the doctor-blade 
technique and subsequently dried at 80◦C for 12 h. The active

3 Results and Discussion 
3.1 Analytical Methods 
Figure 2a depicts the x-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the 
Eucalyptus seed-derived AC. It shows prominent peaks at 23.5◦
and 43.2◦, corresponding to the (002) and (100) crystal planes, 
respectively, and suggests a turbostratic carbon structure [ 36 ]. 
A brief peak illustrates interlayer condensation at 43.5◦, which 
suggests a composite structure with both graphitized and amor- 
phous phases [ 37, 38 ]. The broad peaks matching JCPDS card no. 
26–1079 confirm the formation of a single-phase material with 
graphitic structure. This graphitic structure offers advantages 
in charge storage and supercapacitor applications by increasing 
conductivity and reducing resistance.

The investigation of nitrogen adsorption–desorption meticu-

Chemical activation resulted in the development of a hierarchical 
pore structure, leading to an enhanced specific surface area

desorption isotherm, as shown in Figure 2d , exhibits a Type IV

This behavior can be attributed to the combined effects of 
the hierarchical pore structure, oxygen-containing surface func-

investigation of the charge-storage mechanism based on Dunn’s 
kinetic analysis is presented in Section 3.3 , confirming that 
the capacitance is predominantly governed by surface-controlled 
capacitive processes with a smaller diffusion-controlled contribu- 
tion.

The morphology of the sample is characterized by using Field 
Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) studies. The 
Images from FESEM show that the AC made from Eucalyptus 
seeds has a characteristically porous shape, which is necessary for 
effective ion transport in supercapacitor applications. Figure 3a–
c shows microstructural features at different magnifications. 
A highly porous network can be seen at lower magnification, 
as shown in Figure 3a , where the surface seems rough with 
interconnecting carbon particles. A dense distribution of micro- 
and mesopores is visible in the texture as magnification rises, 
which can be clearly seen in Figure 3b,c . This is essential for 
attaining a high surface area and promoting electrolyte ion trans- 
port [ 42 ]. Notably, the microstructure has a layered architecture 
with irregular voids and sheets stacked randomly, increasing the 
specific surface area and offering many electroactive sites. 
Energy-dispersive x-ray Analysis (EDAX) was performed to deter- 
mine the elemental composition of the prepared AC. The EDAX 
spectrum shown in Figure 3d confirms the presence of carbon 
(81.39 wt%), oxygen (13.90 wt%), and potassium (4.21 wt%). The 
high carbon content indicates the successful carbonization of 
the Eucalyptus seed biomass precursor, while the oxygen content 
confirms the presence of surface functional groups that improve 
the wettability of the carbon surface and contribute to pseu- 
docapacitive charge storage [ 43 ]. A small amount of potassium 
was also detected, which is attributed to trace potassium species 
remaining after the KOH activation process. Following activation, 
the samples were thoroughly washed with dilute hydrochloric 
acid and DI until a nearly neutral pH was attained to remove 
soluble potassium compounds and residual impurities. There- 
fore, the remaining potassium is likely present as strongly bound 
species within the carbon framework rather than as free residual 
KOH. Considering its relatively low content, residual potassium 
is unlikely to play a significant role in the electrochemical 
performance. Instead, the good capacitive behavior is primarily 
governed by the hierarchical porous structure together with the 
oxygen-containing surface functional groups, which facilitate 
electrolyte accessibility and enhance charge storage.

successful formation of the carbon framework, while the oxygen- 
and nitrogen-containing species originate from the biomass

3.2 Electrochemical Measurements 
The AC has undergone an extensive evaluation for aqueous 
electrolyte-based supercapacitor applications using three and 
two-electrode configurations. CV, GCD, and EIS techniques were 
used to evaluate the supercapacitive performance parameters of 
AC. Initially, these electrochemical tests were carried out using 
a 2 M aqueous potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution using a 
three-electrode setup. 
The CV studies are carried out in the voltage range 0 to 0.8 V 
at different scan rates. The CV analysis is shown in Figure 5a . 
As shown in Figure 5a , the recorded CV curves have distinctive 
rectangular shapes, indicating that the electrochemical double- 
layer mechanism primarily facilitates the charge storage process, 
with a small Faradaic contribution from pseudocapacitance 
caused by the oxygen and nitrogen functionalities on the AC 
surface. It has been noticed that when the scan rates increase, 
the area under the CV curve gradually grows, which is consistent 
with the diffusion dynamics of active ions inside the micro- and 
mesoporous structural framework. Higher scan rates accelerate 
the electrolyte ionic mobility to the active electrode surface area, 
enhancing current response [ 46 ].

3.3 Charge-Storage Mechanism and Kinetic 
Analysis 
Although the synthesized AC possesses a moderate BET surface 
area of 610.248 m2 g− 1 , it delivers a high specific capacitance of 266 
F g− 1 at 0.5 A g− 1 , indicating that electrochemical performance 
is not governed solely by the available surface area. Instead, the 
enhanced capacitance originates from the combined influence 
of the hierarchical pore network, oxygen-containing surface 
functionalities, and efficient charge-transfer characteristics. The 
interconnected micro- and mesoporous structure provides abun- 
dant electrochemically accessible sites while facilitating rapid 
electrolyte-ion transport, thereby improving ion accessibility 
throughout the electrode framework [ 50, 51 ]. Moreover, oxygen- 
containing surface functional groups enhance the wettability of 
the carbon surface, promoting improved electrolyte penetration 
and efficient ion adsorption at the electrode/electrolyte inter- 
face [ 52 ]. The EIS results further reveal a low charge-transfer 
resistance, indicating rapid electron transport and effective 
utilization of the electrochemically active surface during the 
charge–discharge process [ 53 ]. Therefore, the improved electro- 
chemical performance of the prepared AC can be attributed to 
the combined influence of these structural and electrochemical 
characteristics rather than to the specific surface area alone [ 54 ]. 
To obtain further insight into the charge-storage mechanism, 
Dunn’s kinetic analysis was carried out using the following

3.4 Symmetric Device 
The AC, carbon black, and PVDF are mixed in an 8:1:1 mass 
ratio and prepared as a slurry using NMP as a solvent. The 
prepared mixer is coated in equal amounts onto both electrodes 
in a symmetrical two-electrode setup with graphite sheet as 
the current collector to assess the material’s practicality for 
supercapacitor applications. The fabricated symmetric device 
was electrochemically tested in 2 M KOH aqueous electrolyte 
throughout a potential range of 0.0 to 0.8 V. Figure 6a shows 
the CV curves of the device at different scan rates. The nearly 
rectangular CV curves indicate the ideal capacitive behavior 
of the electrode. Additionally, the current density continuously 
increased as the scan rates gradually rose from 5 to 100 mV s− 1 , 
demonstrating the good rate capacity of the material. Figure 6b 
illustrates the GCD curves of the device at various current 
densities. The slight non-linearity observed in the GCD curves 
indicates pseudocapacitive contributions and internal resistance, 
which enhance overall charge-storage beyond ideal EDLC behav- 
ior. The symmetric triangular GCD curves of the device suggest 
that the charge–discharge operations are exceptionally reversible.

the symmetric capacitor is calculated to be 120 F g− 1 at a current 
density of 0.5 A g− 1 . The capacitance behavior of the device at dif-

4 Conclusion 
Activated carbon was successfully synthesized from Eucalyptus 
seeds through a simple KOH activation process, providing a 
sustainable and cost-effective approach for the preparation of 
biomass-derived carbon electrode materials. The synthesized 
carbon exhibited a hierarchical porous structure with a specific 
surface area of 610.248 m2 g− 1 and a defect-rich carbon framework 
( ID / IG = 0.9). Electrochemical evaluation demonstrated specific 
capacitances of 266 F g− 1 in a three-electrode configuration and 
120 F g− 1 in a symmetric two-electrode device at 0.5 A g− 1 . 
Dunn’s kinetic analysis indicated that the charge-storage process 
was predominantly governed by surface-controlled capacitive 
behavior, while the porous structure and oxygen-containing 
surface functional groups contributed to improved electrolyte 
accessibility and charge storage. The assembled symmetric super- 
capacitor delivered a maximum energy density of 11 Wh kg− 1 at a 
power density of approximately 200 W kg− 1 , retained 6 Wh kg− 1 
at approximately 1200 W kg− 1 , and maintained 76.5% capacitance 
retention with a Coulombic efficiency of 88.98% after 10,000 
charge–discharge cycles. The successful operation of five red 
LEDs following a short charging period further demonstrates the 
practical applicability of the fabricated device. Overall, these find-
