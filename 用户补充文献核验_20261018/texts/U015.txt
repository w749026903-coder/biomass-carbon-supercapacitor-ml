Abstract
Biomass-derived porous carbons are promising candidates for supercapacitor electrodes due
to their high surface area, tunable pore structures, and superior conductivity. In this study,
peanut shells were utilized as a sustainable precursor to synthesize hierarchical porous carbon
via H3PO4 activation. The resulting materials feature a well-defined micro-mesoporous architec-
ture, achieving a specific capacitance of 221.9 F g −1 at 0.5 A g −1 in a three-electrode configuration.
Furthermore, the symmetric supercapacitor demonstrated exceptional long-term durability, with
99.3% capacitance retention over 20 000 cycles at 10 A g −1. These findings offer a scalable and effi-
cient strategy for converting agricultural waste into high-performance energy storage materials.
1. Introduction
Supercapacitors and ion batteries are among the most widely deployed energy storage devices. Among
these, supercapacitors are extensively used in various fields due to their high power density, long cycle
life, and fast charge-discharge rates compared to other energy storage technologies [ 1–4]. Developing
low-cost, high-performance electrode materials is crucial for improving the energy storage capability of
supercapacitors. Porous carbon materials have been extensively studied due to their large specific sur-
face area, rich pore structure, excellent conductivity, and low cost [ 5–8]. However, traditional activated
carbon production relies on petrochemical-based feedstocks such as coal and petroleum [ 9], which are
non-renewable resources. Moreover, without appropriate pretreatment, these materials can cause severe
water and soil pollution during production. Biomass carbon offers extensive availability, straightfor-
ward preparation, and renewable, pollution-free production. It substantially reduces resource wastage
and significantly lowers the costs associated with processing biomass waste [ 10–12]. Some agricultural
and industrial wastes have been utilized as precursors for preparing carbon electrodes for supercapaci-
tors, demonstrating excellent electrochemical performance. For instance, Li et al utilized wheat straw and
carried out Fe 3+/H2O2 Fenton pre-treatment combined with carbonization activation to obtain a porous
carbon material with a specific surface area of up to 3440 m 2 g−1. At a current density of 0.1 A g −1, the
specific capacitance reached 425.2 F g −1, and at an ultra-high current density of 100 A g −1, it still main-
tained an excellent rate performance of 286.1 F g −1 [13]. Y ang et al used wood chips as the raw mate-
rial, subjected them to ZnCl 2 hydrothermal treatment, and directly carbonized them to obtain a carbon
material rich in mesopores, which was used as the cathode of a zinc-ion hybrid supercapacitor. The spe-
cific capacitance reached 263 F g −1, and after 30 000 cycles, the capacity showed almost no degradation
[14]. Moreover, other biomass such as rice straw [ 15] and jering pod [ 16] have also been extensively
studied and confirmed to have excellent electrochemical energy storage potential. Consequently, biomass
is regarded as a highly promising precursor for the production of functional carbon. Moreover, porous
biomass carbon materials exhibit advantages such as high specific surface area, unique structure, excel-
lent electrical conductivity, and high mechanical strength, making them outstanding electrode materials
for supercapacitors [ 17–20]. Consequently, the application of biomass carbon in supercapacitors repre-
sents a current research focus.

Among diverse biomass sources, peanut shells represent a widely distributed and critically impor-
tant agricultural byproduct. Their substantial production volume and distinctive structural characteristics
have drawn significant research attention. Annual cultivation and harvesting of peanuts for edible oil
production generate approximately 6 million tons of peanut shells, which are currently discarded entirely
as waste [ 21]. This results in substantial resource wastage and environmental pollution. By converting
them into supercapacitor electrode materials, the dual goals of resource utilization of solid waste and
reduction of pollutants were achieved. Currently, among various activation methods, chemical activa-
tion yields superior results. The selection of activators (such as KOH [ 22, 23], NaOH [ 24, 25], H 3PO4
[26, 27], ZnCl 2 [28, 29], and FeCl 3 [30, 31]), along with activation conditions (activator ratio, activa-
tion temperature, activation time, etc.) significantly influence the pore structure and surface chemistry
of porous carbon [ 32, 33]. By rationally selecting activators, pore structures can be effectively enriched,
substantially enhancing the electrochemical performance of supercapacitors. However, current research
lacks systematic understanding of activator efficacy for specific biomasses and optimal activation condi-
tions. This limitation hinders the method’s broader application.
Despite the growing interest in peanut-shell-derived porous carbons for supercapacitors, previous
studies remain limited in scope and depth. Sandeep and Ravindra employed H 3PO4-assisted hydrother-
mal carbonization, yet the resulting carbon exhibited a low specific surface area of only 17.8 m 2 g−1
and a modest energy density of 4.08 Wh kg −1 derived from three-electrode GCD curves without two-
electrode device validation; moreover, the material was prepared under a single set of conditions, with-
out systematic investigation of activation parameters [ 34]. Cheng et al prepared N,P co-doped peanut
shell carbon using NH 4H2PO4 as a single-source dopant, achieving a specific capacitance of 184 F g −1
at 1 A g −1. The activation parameters were systematically varied for the N,P co-doped material, but the
individual effects of nitrogen and phosphorus on the carbon material were not distinguished from each
other [ 35]. Liang et al compared H 3PO4, ZnCl 2, and KOH as activators for peanut shell carbon; how-
ever, their investigation focused primarily on identifying KOH-activated carbon as the superior per-
former, while the H 3PO4-activated counterpart was prepared under a single fixed condition and not
evaluated in a two-electrode device, leaving its full potential largely unexplored [ 36]. Y an et al utilized
KOH-activated peanut shell carbon for capacitive deionization, achieving an ultrahigh surface area of
3032 m 2 g−1, yet the study focused on desalination rather than supercapacitor energy storage and did
not report energy or power density metrics; furthermore, the activation parameters were limited to KOH
ratio without investigating temperature or time effects [ 37].
Notably, none of these prior investigations systematically disentangled the individual and coupled
influences of activation ratio, temperature, and time on the structural evolution and electrochemical
response of H 3PO4-activated peanut-shell carbons. For the first time in this biomass system, we eluci-
date the intricate interplay among graphitization degree, oxygen-containing functional groups, and hier-
archical pore architecture, and further establish their collective correlation with the electrochemical per-
formance. This integrated understanding goes beyond routine parameter optimization, unraveling the
multidimensional structure-property-electrochemistry relationships in phosphorus-doped peanut-shell-
derived carbons.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials and reagents
Peanut shells were obtained from Liaoning Province. H 3PO4, PTFE (60 wt%), HCl, and BP 2000 were
obtained from Aladdin Reagent Company. Deionized water was used throughout all experiments.
2.2. Synthesis of carbon materials
Figure 1 depicts the fabrication procedure for PPCs. Collected peanut shells were thoroughly rinsed and
dried. The shells were then ground and impregnated with H 3PO4 solutions at varying mass ratios (1:0.5,
1:1, 1:1.5), followed by overnight drying at 60 ◦C. The carbonization and activation were performed in
a single step within a tube furnace under N 2 atmosphere. Samples were heated to the target tempera-
tures (400 ◦C, 500 ◦C, 600 ◦C) at a rate of 5 ◦C min −1 and maintained for specific durations (0.5 h,
1 h, 2 h). The resulting products were washed with dilute HCl and deionized water until reaching a neu-
tral pH, then dried at 60 ◦C for 12 h. The prepared samples are designated as PPC- X–Y–Zh, where X, Y,
and Z represent the activation temperature, precursor/H 3PO4 weight ratio, and activation time, respec-
tively. Experimental variables are summarized in table 1.

2.3. Material characterization
Microstructural analysis of the generated samples was conducted using a scanning electron microscope
(SEM, ZEISS SUPRA 55) at scanning magnifications of 1k and 20k. The crystallographic planes of the
samples were characterized by x-ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical Empyrean X) with a diffraction angle
range of 10 ◦–80◦. The material structure and graphitization degree were further investigated using a
Raman spectrometer (Raman, Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution), with a wavenumber range of 50–4000.
The elemental composition and surface functional groups of the samples were analyzed by x-ray pho-
toelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi). The specific surface area and pore size
distribution were calculated using the BET (micromeritics ASAP2420) model.
2.4. Electrochemical measurements
Electrochemical characterization was conducted using a three-electrode configuration on a CHI 660E
electrochemical workstation. The washed and dried nickel foam sheets were weighed. A slurry was pre-
pared by grinding porous carbon material BP 2000 and a concentrated dispersion of polytetrafluoroethy-
lene in an 8:1:1 ratio. The resulting black slurry was uniformly coated onto one side of pretreated nickel
foam and dried for 10 h in a vacuum oven at 60 ◦C. Finally, the dried nickel foam was pressed for
1 min at 10 MPa to obtain the electrode. After drying, it was weighed again to obtain the mass. The
active material mass was calculated by multiplying the difference between the two weights by 0.8, keep-
ing the loaded effective mass in the range of 3–5 mg. In a three-electrode system employing Hg/HgO as
the reference electrode and platinum mesh as the counter electrode, cyclic voltammetry (CV), constant-
current charge-discharge, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements were conducted on

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Material characterization
The surface morphology of PPC-500-Y-1 h was further observed by scanning electron microscopy
(SEM). The sample without the addition of an activator, PPC-500-0-1 h (figures 2(a) and (b)), retains
the original three-dimensional structure of the peanut shell. However, the surface is relatively smooth,
with almost no pore structure. Figures 2(c)–(h) shows the SEM images of the samples treated with
H3PO4. The obtained samples retain the original creased and wrinkled structure of the peanut shell, but
exhibit a significantly rougher surface. As the dosage of H 3PO4 increases, the roughness of the sample
surface increases significantly. Numerous large pores have formed along the wrinkles, embedded with
abundant micropores and mesopores, constituting an ordered honeycomb-like layered porous structure.
This indicates that H 3PO4 significantly influences the morphology of the prepared precursor.
Figure 3(a) displays the XRD patterns of the eight distinct carbon preparations. The diffraction peaks
at 23 ◦ and 43.5 ◦ correspond to the (002) and (100) crystal planes, respectively. The (002) plane corre-
sponds to the interlayer spacing of graphitic carbon. The weaker diffraction peak at the (100) plane indi-
cates the presence of graphitized crystallographic planes [ 39]. Notably, the intensity of the XRD peak
depends not only on the stacking order of the carbon layers but also strongly on the types of atoms
involved in diffraction. According to X-ray diffraction theory, the atomic scattering factor ( f ) is equal
to the atomic number ( Z) at zero scattering angle, and for any fixed scattering angle, its value increases
with increasing atomic number [ 40]. Phosphorus has an atomic number of 15, which is considerably
higher than that of carbon ( Z = 6). Consequently, the scattering power of phosphorus atoms is signif-
icantly stronger than that of carbon atoms. When phosphorus atoms are doped into the carbon frame-
work through H 3PO4 activation and partially replace carbon atoms, the average scattering factor of the
unit cell increases, leading to an enhancement of the (002) diffraction peak intensity.
Figure 3(b) displays the Raman spectra of the PPCs. The D band peak at 1350 cm −1 and the G
band peak at 1590 cm −1, respectively represent the concentrations of disordered carbon and graphitized
carbon [ 41, 42]. The ratio of peak intensities of the D band to the G band ( ID/IG) is widely used to
assess the crystallinity or defect density of carbon materials. A higher ID/IG value indicates more defect
presence, while a lower ratio suggests a higher degree of graphitization. The ID/IG ratio first increases

Pore structure and pore size distribution are key factors influencing the electrical conductivity of
porous carbon materials. Isothermal curves represent the surface properties of porous carbon materials
and their interaction with adsorbed gases. Isotherm types can be categorized as Type I, Type II, Type
III, Type IV , and Type V isotherms. Isotherms can be categorized into the low relative pressure region
(0 < P/P0< 0.4), the medium relative pressure region (0.4 < P/P0 < 0.8), and the high relative pressure
region ( P/P0 > 0.8), corresponding respectively to the presence of micropores, mesopores, and macrop-
ores. Microporosity enhances the specific surface area and electrical conductivity of porous carbon mate-
rials, while mesopores provide pathways for ion transport whilst reducing resistance. Macropores offer a
buffer layer for ions, shortening their migration distance.
Figures 4((a) and (b)) shows the N 2 adsorption/desorption isotherms, revealing that the obtained
carbon materials exhibit distinct combinations of Type I and Type IV isotherms according to the IUPAC
classification. In other words, when the relative pressure P/P0 ranges between 0.0 and 0.1, the sharp
increase in adsorption at low pressures indicates a microporous structure (Type I isotherm). The Type
IV isotherm with visible hysteresis loops within the relative pressure P/P0 range of 0.4–1 indicates the
presence of mesopores [ 43, 44]. Figures 4((c) and (d)) shows the corresponding pore size distribution
curve derived from the pore size distribution data, further confirming that PPCs exhibit a hierarchical
micro/meso-porous structure. The pore size distribution curve clearly indicates that H 3PO4 treatment
primarily generates a substantial number of micropores and mesopores, producing two distinct pore
diameters: micropores ranging from 0.7 to 2.0 nm and small mesopores between 3.0 and 4.0 nm.

This change indicates that appropriately increasing the amount of the activator can significantly etch
the carbon framework, generating a large number of micropores and mesopores, thereby significantly
increasing the specific surface area and pore volume. However, when the ratio is further increased to 1.5,

Based on the above analysis, the specific surface area and pore structure of the PPC material strongly
depend on the activator ratio, carbonization temperature, and activation time. The optimal preparation
conditions are 500 ◦C, 1 h, and an precursor/H 3PO4 acid ratio of 1:1. At this point, the specific surface
area is as high as 1184.88 m 2 g−1, the total pore volume is 0.67 cm 3 g−1, and the average pore diameter
is 2.25 nm. This hierarchical structure with abundant mesopores and connected micropores is conducive
to the rapid transmission of ions, and therefore, this sample also exhibits the highest specific capacitance.
Low or high ratios, temperatures, and times will lead to a decrease in the specific surface area, an unrea-
sonable pore size distribution, and thus poor electrochemical performance.

deterioration in electrochemical performance.

4. Conclusion
Building on the above experimental findings, waste peanut shells were utilized as a sustainable precur-
sor to fabricate hierarchical porous carbon via a one-step H 3PO4 activation route. The as-obtained sam-
ple (PPC-500-1-1 h) exhibits typical amorphous carbon characteristics, a high specific surface area of
1184.88 m 2 g−1, a well-balanced micro-/mesopore structure, and abundant oxygen-containing functional
groups. Such structural merits collectively afford abundant ion adsorption sites and facilitate rapid elec-
trolyte infiltration, endowing the material with excellent electrochemical performance. Specifically, in a
three-electrode system, PPC-500-1-1 h delivers a high specific capacitance of 221.9 F g −1 at 0.5 A g −1,
and retains 156.5 F g −1 at 5 A g −1 corresponding to a capacitance retention of 70.5%, demonstrating
its superior rate capability. The low charge-transfer resistance ( Rct = 0.71 Ω) further corroborates its fast
interfacial charge transport kinetics. Accordingly, PPC-500-1-1 h emerges as a promising candidate for
high-performance supercapacitor electrodes. This synthetic approach not only achieves the dual goals
of waste biomass valorization and environmental protection by converting agricultural waste into high-
value energy storage materials with tangible economic benefits, but also provides a facile and effective
strategy to construct hierarchical porous structures in biomass-derived precursors. This versatile method
can be extended to a wide range of analogous energy storage systems.
