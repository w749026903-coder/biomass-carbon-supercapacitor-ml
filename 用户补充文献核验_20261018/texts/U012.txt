14.1 INTRODUCTION
Technological developments have the ability to address nearly any issue facing humanity [1–3]. But 
a significant problem with growing technological development is that, as manufacturing demands 
rise, tonnes of garbage are produced that surpass the environment’s capability [4,5]. Aside from 
industry, man‑made factors also produce a range of wastes, including plant‑based biowastes, munic‑
ipal organic waste, agricultural waste, etc. These wastes contain a lot of organic compounds which 
have a large surface area and extraordinarily high electronic conductivity. Also, with the increasing 
technological developments, the need for energy‑storage device arise. But the fossil fuels are in a 
limited amount. Researchers are now under increasing pressure to develop environmentally accept‑
able and renewable energy storage systems for utilising sources like wind, solar, and tidal energy 
[6]. The two main electrochemical energy storage technologies are supercapacitors and batteries 
[7–9]. Although batteries have the potential to have a high energy density, store a substantial amount 
of energy, and produce a steady output for prolonged use [10], they charge and discharge slowly and 
are unable to respond quickly. Batteries also require frequent replacements due to their short cycle 
life, and the chemical reactions they undergo may produce toxic waste [11,12]. Supercapacitors 
could successfully overcome battery energy storage systems’ drawbacks [13]. Several benefits are 
provided by them: (1) Supercapacitors can release energy in a matter of seconds thanks to their 
rapid charging and discharging characteristics, which makes them appropriate for uses like electric 
vehicle braking systems. (2) Longer longevity, as they can withstand thousands of cycles of charg‑
ing and discharging without seeing a noticeable drop in performance [14]. (3) Thanks to their high 
power density, they can meet a variety of high‑power needs by providing instantaneous energy out‑
put [15]. Supercapacitors’ rapid charging and discharging capabilities make them useful in a variety 
of applications, including consumer electronics, industrial machinery, transportation, renewable 
energy, emergency power solutions, aircraft, military applications, and larger energy storage sys‑
tems [16]. Due to this, scientists have lately begun producing supercapacitor electrodes from these 
wastes. The vast supply, renewability, natural pore architecture, increased safety, and affordability 
of biomass‑derived carbon set it apart from other carbon sources. Because these materials have 
many active sites and better conductivity, they exhibit superior specific capacitance and noteworthy 
rate performance [17]. In this book chapter, the different sources from which organic wastes are 
generated are summarised. Additionally, the types of synthetic approaches used to derive carbon 
from organic wastes are discussed with respect to application for energy storage in supercapacitors. 
The role of green electrolytes in supercapacitors is also discussed. This book chapter will help the 
reader gain knowledge about using biomass‑derived carbon for supercapacitor applications.

14.3  METHODOLOGIES INVOLVED IN THE CONVERSION OF  
ORGANIC WASTE
14.3.1 p yrolysis
Among the earliest methods of synthesis, pyrolysis can create carbon from almost anything that 
contains carbon or its compounds. The above process is flexible and uses thermal decomposition 
in a compartment with little to no oxygen to create carbon‑rich structures from precursor materi ‑
als. The oxygen‑free, high‑temperature process of pyrolysis ranges from 300°C to 1,000°C. When 
agricultural waste is pyrolysed, three by‑products are produced: syngas, biochar, and bio‑oil. In 
the field of energy storage, biochar that is created by pyrolysing biomass at a very low temperature 
often has poor conductivity and is rarely utilised. Zhang et al. utilised sodium lignin sulphonate 
as a biochar source which exhibits excellent cycle stability in 1 M H 2SO4 electrolyte, ultrahigh 
volumetric Sp.C (950 F/cm 3), and gravimetric specific capacitance (480 F/g at 1 A/g) [46].The 
most effective technique for turning biomass into charcoal is slow pyrolysis. During the slow 
pyrolysis process, biomass is heated gradually rather than rapidly, remaining at a low temperature 
for extended periods of time (from minutes to hours). During pyrolysis, a low heating rate yields 
more charcoal than a high heating rate. According to Angin et al., the biochar output decreased 
from 34.18% to 29.70% at 400 °C when the heating rate was increased from 10 °C to 50 °C/min 
[47]. Mohammed et al. employed baobab fruit shells as a low‑cost, environment‑friendly biomass 
precursor which exhibit high Sp.C of 233.48 F/g and 355.8 F/g at a current density of 1 A/g [48].  
It is possible to balance the concentration of various pyrolysis products, such as syngas, char ‑
coal, and biofuel, by using intermediate pyrolysis. A range of operational parameters, including 
the temperature of the furnace range (500 °C–600 °C) and residence duration (300–1,000 s), are 
employed to achieve intermediate pyrolysis rates [49]. Carbon nanotubes and activated carbon, 
which are produced by pyrolysis and have a high specific surface area and electrical conductivity,

14.3.2 h yDrothermal  Car Bonisation
The process of hydrothermal carbonisation, which involves the simultaneous reactions of (1) 
hydrolysis, (2) dehydration, (3) decarboxylation, (4) recondensation and polymerisation, and (5) 
aromatisation, can effectively convert feedstock into biochar (Figure 14.3). The initial reaction in 
the hydrolytic cleavage temperature process, known as hydrolysis, occurs when water reacts with 
the more major liquid waste constituents to reduce them to smaller ones. When these particles 
go through further chemical reactions, hydrochar, a solid biofuel, is produced. The hydrolysis of 
numerous biomass components, such as glucose, hemicellulose, lipids, carbohydrates, and fats, can 
be accomplished with HTC readings as low as 180°C. The lengthy process of hydrolysing cellulose 
requires higher temperature (230°C) due to its uniform and linear arrangement [53]. Even at 230°C, 
lignin, which is more difficult to hydrolyse and remains unreacted during the hydrochar‑making 
process, is essentially absent from hydrochar [54]. Smaller pieces go through additional chemical 
processes that produce water‑soluble by‑products like furans, furfurals, and acetic acid, among 
others. These intermediates undergo a variety of processes, such as dehydration, decarboxylation, 
recondensation, polymerisation, and aromatisation, to produce carbon‑rich hydrochar.
The effect of various operating conditions on the hydrothermal carbonisation of acacia wastes 
was assessed by Wilk et al. They demonstrated that raising the reaction temperature (from 180°C to 
220°C) increased the hydrochar’s properties, resulting in greater heating values from 21 to 24 MJ/
kg and a better carbon content (from about 56% to 67%) [55]. The impact of the reaction tempera‑
ture of the hydrothermal carbonisation of rice straw, which ranged from 180 °C to 240°C, was also 
assessed by Wei et al. In this instance, the carbon content rises from roughly 42% to 52% [56]. Li 
et al. examined the variation in sawdust hydrochar characteristics by altering the reaction tempera‑
ture between 200°C and 260°C. Here, the products’ greater heating value increased from about 24 
to 28 MJ/kg as a result of the carbon content rising from about 57% to 68% [57]. It is obvious that 
temperature has a significant impact on the hydrothermal carbonisation process, which converts

14.3.3 t orrefa Ction
Torrefaction is a partial pyrolysis of biomass and is of two types depending on the conditions 
employed. In wet torrefaction, temperature ranges from 200°C to 260°C in hot compressed water at 
200–700 psi with a 2 g size and a residence time of 5 min in which cooling is carried out by rapidly 
immersing in an ice bath, whereas dry torrefaction is carried out at 250 °C–300°C in an inert gas at 
atmospheric pressure with a 5 g size and a residence time of 5 min, and is cooled indirectly. Its three 
main products are non‑condensable gaseous products, acidic aqueous products with a yellowish 
hue, and dark‑coloured solid products. Since torrefaction is often carried out at a low heating rate, 
a larger yield of solid product is produced [61]. The slow pyrolysis and hydrothermal carbonisation 
processes are preferred for the production of wood biochar over the rapid pyrolysis, gasification, 
and/or torrefaction processes. Nevertheless, slow pyrolysis is recommended over hydrothermal car‑
bonisation for the production of alkaline biochar. Compared to biochar made by gasification or 
rapid pyrolysis, slow pyrolysis biochar has a higher aromatic concentration [62]. Most research has 
focused on slow pyrolysis and hydrothermal carbonisation processes to increase surface areas and 
surface functional groups.
14.3.4 i onothermal  Car Bonisation
Ionothermal carbonisation is a one‑stage process used to create porous carbons from biomass by 
using ionic liquids. Ionic liquids are renowned for their exceptional thermal stability, low melting 
point, and resistance to chemicals. Ionic liquids are regarded as green solvents primarily because of 
their special characteristics, which include low vapour pressure, non‑flammability, thermal stabil ‑
ity, and non‑volatility [63]. Owing to these characteristics, ionic liquids have drawn a lot of attention 
as porogenic agents, solvents, and templates in the biochar manufacturing process [64]. Zhang et al. 
reported the formation of porous heteroatom‑doped carbon materials with high yield by ionother ‑
mal method [65].
Liu et al. used Jujun grass as a N‑containing precursor, and the optimised electrode showed a 
remarkable Sp.C of 336 F/g at 1 A/g in 6 M KOH. It even maintained a capacitance of 222 F/g at 
10 A/g, which is 66% of the starting capacitance [66]. Lin et al. reported ionothermal carbons which 
exhibits a high Sp.C of 245 F/g at a current density of 1 A/g [67]. Production of carbon by different

14.4  CHALLENGES AND PERSPECTIVES OF REALISATION FOR  
SUPERCAPACITOR
Supercapacitors, or ultracapacitors, are potential energy storage devices for future generations. 
They are widely used in hybrid electric vehicles and other power systems, delivering electrical 
energy quickly and with a long shelf life. However, they face challenges such as technical issues,

consistency testing, and the need for industrial standards. This chapter addresses the challenges and 
future development opportunities for supercapacitors, providing guidance and new directions for 
their advancement and commercialisation.
In this context, researchers are working on developing energy storage techniques, such as super‑
capacitors, that efficiently fill the void between traditional capacitors and rechargeable ion batteries. 
These devices provide a better lifespan, good power density, fast charging and discharging, low 
cost, simple maintenance, and no environmental impact. The study of electrochemical double‑layer 
capacitors (EDLCs) began in the 1950s; however, it was not until 1978 that the technology, which is 
now referred to as supercapacitors, became commercially accessible for computer memory backup 
power [68]. However, EDLCs have low internal resistance and are capable of storing and delivering 
energy at higher power levels than rechargeable batteries. Double‑layer capacitors are made up of 
two carbon electrodes separated by a porous membrane and immersed in an electrolyte that facili ‑
tates ionic flow while preventing electrical short circuits. A current collector is attached to an elec‑
trode to reduce internal resistance. Charge is stored in polarised electrolytes, creating a double layer 
of anode and cathode charges at the interface between the electrode and the electrolyte. However, 
with the increasing safe utilisation of supercapacitors, they have been researched and commer ‑
cialised since 1879, with companies like Maxwell, NEC, Panasonic, Tokin, and Russian Economd 
dominating the global market. Double‑layer capacitance was first proposed by Helmholtz in 1879, 
but it was only in recent decades that it began to be used for energy storage. Patents were transferred 
to NEC in 1979, and various types of supercapacitors have been introduced since then (Figure 14.5).
There are four challenges for supercapacitors:
 i. Supercapacitors currently have a gap in energy densities between them and batteries, with 
research focusing on improving energy density. Innovative electrolyte and electrode active 
materials that exhibit better supercapacitor performance are essential. Enhancing energy 
densities can be achieved by increasing the electrode surface area or voltage window. More 
research is being conducted to develop new materials and organic electrolytes [69,70].
 ii. Supercapacitors provide immediate throughput and high energy; however, they may repre‑
sent an obstacle in military applications, notably as electrical power sources for satellites 
and spacecraft. Reliable design studies are critical for addressing load compassionate load 
variation, outside conditions, and system stability [71].

 iii. Supercapacitors have a low rated voltage of less than 2.7 V, necessitating several series con‑

 iv. Supercapacitors are rapidly advancing energy storage devices at various stages of develop‑

electrodes can be created through ex situ or in situ growth on flexible substrates, improving electro‑

manufacturing processes, excellent electrochemical properties, and flexibility, integrating active 
materials, substrates, and electrolytes [74,75].

14.5.6 t ype of  ele Ctrolyte
Electrolytes are often divided into three families: organic, aqueous, and non‑aqueous. Each has 
unique advantages and disadvantages. Aqueous electrolytes have high ionic conductivity and capac‑
itance, but their narrow decomposition voltage limits their working potential [101]. Ionic liquids 
and organic electrolytes have low conductivity, flammability, and cost. Environmentally friendly 
aqueous electrolytes are more suitable for electrochemical supercapacitors (ESs). Techniques to 
enhance ES performance include evaluating advanced electrolytes, understanding ES properties, 
and analysing electrolyte effects on ES performance. Aqueous electrolytes are employed in elec‑
trolytic supercapacitor devices because they have limited working potential windows, which makes 
them less economically appealing. Most literature uses aqueous electrolytes due to their ease of 
management and low cost. Aqueous electrolytes interact easily with electrode surfaces with small 
pore sizes and atomic radius. The number of hydrated and bare cations, anions, and charge carrier 
mobility all influence the electrolyte selection (Figure 14.10) [102].
Use of acidic electrolytes: Hybrid supercapacitors have been developed to extend the operating 
potential windows, thereby achieving higher energy densities in ESs that use aqueous electrolytes. 
Symmetric ESs have a finite cell potential limit, while asymmetric ESs, which use various elec‑
trode materials, offer a wider operating potential window. Various types of asymmetric ESs, such 
as carbon//PbO 2 and carbon//RuO 2, have been tested in strong acidic electrolytes, each showing 
distinct properties. Notably, polyaniline–carbon electrodes have demonstrated an energy density of 
up to 11.46 Wh/kg [103]. Liquid electrolytes like H2SO4 provide high thermal stability, potentially 
enhancing the energy density and stability of supercapacitors. RuO2/carbon nano‑onion symmetric 
ESs have an energy density of 20 Wh/kg and deliver excellent capacitance performance when sulph‑
uric acid is used as the electrolyte [104]. For EDLCs, the specific capacitance in H 2SO4 electrolyte 
is significantly larger than that in neutral electrolytes due to the higher ionic conductivity of the 
former. The mobility of the electrode materials is influenced by the conductivity of the electrolyte. 
For carbon electrode materials, the reported Sp.C ranges from 100 to 300 F/g [105].

Use of basic electrolyte : Acidic electrolytes are insufficient for valuable testing of metal

Use of neutral electrolytes : Neutral electrolytes in ESs have lower specific capacitance than 
H2SO4 or KOH electrolytes, and their equivalent series resistance increases. They have a larger 
operating potential voltage due to their wider electrolyte stable potential window due to lower con‑
centration of H+ and OH− compared to acid and alkaline electrolytes [109]. Na2SO4 electrolytes at a 
high potential window (1.6 V) at 0.5 M concentration, show excellent retention capacity after 10,000 
charging–discharging cycles. Neutral electrolytes are utilised in asymmetric supercapacitors with 
high working voltage to increase the energy density of electrode material. Neutral electrolytes like 
Li2SO4 or LiCl are introduced with Lithium metal as a negative electrode combined with a suitable 
positive electrode [110]. This method improves the working potential voltage (4.0 V) and energy 
density with rusting‑related issues. However, further advancements are needed for supercapacitors 
to enhance their energy density, power density, and lifespan.
14.6 ROLE OF GREEN ELECTROLYTES IN SUPERCAPACITORS
The global increase in energy demand due to urbanisation and population growth has led to a rapid 
depletion of energy resources and rising environmental threats, prompting a shift towards renewable 
energy to meet the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals, particularly in addressing greenhouse gas 
emissions. Supercapacitors are employed for a variety of applications, including electric cars, puls‑
ing methods, electronic gadgets, and energy and power quality control. However, managing material 
waste from electrochemical devices remains a concern, with food and green waste accounting for 
44% of global waste. Aqueous gel‑based polymer electrolytes, also known as hydrogel electrolytes, 
consist of a polymer matrix, electrolytic salt, and plasticiser, offering cost‑effectiveness, good ionic 
conductivity, a large potential window, and being eco‑friendly [112]. However, polyethylene oxide, 
polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and percetic acid are used as a polymer matrix and water as the plasticiser, 
and neutral, acidic and base electrolyte consist to develop a polymer matrix electrolyte. PVA is used 
frequently due to its facile synthesis, good hydrophilicity, better chemical and mechanical stability, 
transparent film formation, and negligible toxicity. Hydrolysis decreases the presence of acetate 
groups in polymers like PVA, which are linear with smaller hydroxyl groups. These chains form 
hydrogen bonds, forming a crystal lattice. Hydrogen bonding occurs between water and polymer 
chains, and PVA ’s–OH groups enhance electrolyte ionic conductivity [113].
Lokhande and his group designed an asymmetric supercapacitor device using prepared β‑MnO2 
nanospheres and O–SnS nanoflowers on stainless steel, which showed a specific capacitance of 122 
F/g at 5 mV/s scan rate with retention capacitance of 95.3% after 5,000 charging–discharging cycles 
at 10 mA) under PVA–LiClO4 gel electrolyte. The large capacitance and high potential window of 
0–1.6 V offer high energy and power density of 29.8 and 1.3 kW/kg, respectively. To check the real 
applicability of the fabricated device, they checked the LEDs light illumination properties, which 
was excellent (Figure 14.11; Table 14.1) [114].
