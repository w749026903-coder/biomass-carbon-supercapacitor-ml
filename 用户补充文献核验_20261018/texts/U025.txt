Unveiling the energy storage potential of valorized 
unripe plantain peel-derived activated carbon
Ayesha Samrin · Mohammed Rehaan Chandan · Igor Levchenko · Katia Alexander  · 
Mandhakini Mohandas
Received: 27 February 2026 / Accepted: 25 August 2026
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
The damage from excess waste generation on the environment has made it necessary to re-think how we 
approach product development and the waste management lifecycle. Within this framework, conversion of 
biowaste into energy storage materials has emerged as an effective means of reducing the environmental 
impact of food production. In this work, activated car-bon with superior supercapacitive properties is produced 
directly from unripe plantain peel by optimizing the use of two different activating agents KOH and ZnCl 2 dur-
ing pyrolysis. Activated carbon Z7 which was pyrolyzed at 700 °C using ZnCl 2 as the activating agent had a 
higher sur-face area of 1636 m 2/g and higher electrochemical performance when compared to activated carbon 
produced under all other experimental conditions. The specific capacitance obtained for Z7 in a two-electrode 
Swagelok set-up was 167 F g −1 at 0.5 A/g and an excellent energy density of 23 Wh/kg at a power density of 
174 W/kg at 0.5 A/g current was obtained. Further-more, a capacitance retention of 79% was achieved after 
4500 cycles at a current density of 5 A/g with a coulombic efficiency of 99%.
Keywords Unripe plantain peels · KOH and ZnCl 2 activation · Porosity · Specific capacitance
Introduction
Driven by the growing population and its increasing use of energy-intensive technologies, the global consump -
tion of energy continues to increase, at a significant cost to the environment. To slow down the depletion of fos-
sil fuels, and reduce associated emissions, there have been significant global efforts to transition to clean forms 
of energy, with a particularly strong focus on renewable energy sources, such as solar or wind. However, their 
intermittent nature as well as potential asynchronicity between peak generation and peak demand necessitates the 
development of efficient systems for energy storage, such as batteries, flywheels, supercapacitors. Supercapaci -
tor is an efficient energy storage system due to its high-power density and high stability. The energy is stored 
in a supercapacitor by two mechanisms, namely (i) Electric double layer (EDLC) and (ii) Faradaic redox reac -
tions. For a clean energy storage system, the electric double layer capacitor (EDLC) materials are highly suitable 
as they are potentially sustainable, have a longer life cycle, chemically stable and are also economical [ 6, 36].

Among promising EDLC materials, activated carbon is one of the most readily available materials with good 
supercapacitive properties [14, 18]. Although activated carbon can be produced from a variety of sources, the use 
of biowaste as the carbon feedstock offers a better choice for the environment [ 22, 38] with an activating agent 
such as KOH, NaOH, NaHCO 3, ZnCl2, HPO3, etc. typically used to chemically activate the carbon in order to 
enhance its properties [4, 28, 33]. Amongst possible biowaste feedstocks, peel from bananas has received notable 
attention due to the substantial volume of waste (almost 32% of the fruit) that is produced during consumption. 
The research has been particularly active in India, as it is the largest producer of bananas globally, according to 
the data from the National Horticulture Board, Ministry of Agriculture, Govt. of India ( https://nhb.gov.in) [34]. 
As banana peel waste contains a high amount of nitrogen and phosphorous, its disposal presents a notable envi -
ronmental hazard, whereas its prolonged decomposition of almost two years contributes to higher greenhouse 
gas emissions [1, 35].
Most of the studies conducted to date on banana peel-derived EDLC materials have focused on the reforming 
of waste from ripe bananas. For example, Tripathy et al., synthesized activated carbons with the highest specific 
capacitance value of 227 F g−1 at 1 A/g in a three-electrode setup with a capacitance retention of 97% after 5000 
cycles. The energy density for the activated carbon was 7 Wh/kg at a power density of 250 W/kg [40]. In another 
example, Lv et al., synthesized a hierarchical porous carbon foam with a specific capacitance of 206 F g−1 at 1 A/g 
and a capacitance retention of 88% [26], whereas Liu et al., prepared an n-doped activated carbon with a specific 
capacitance of 185.8 F g−1 at 5 mV/s and 210.6 F g −1 at 0.5 A/g [25]. Kandasamy et al., investigated the use of 
sulfuric acid to activate the carbon produced from the peel from a triploid cultivar of banana Musa paradisiaca, 
yielding samples with a high specific capacitance of 306.2 F g−1 at 30 mV/s (Kandasamy et al., 2016).
However, in southern parts of India, namely Kerala and Tamil Nadu, unripe plantains are widely used to 
prepare many culinary products, thus yielding a large amount of green plantain peel. While the use of the lat -
ter to prepare activated carbon have been studied for application in oil adsorption [ 41], lead(II) and 3,7-Bis 
(dimethylamino)-phenothiazin-5-ium chloride adsorption [15], reduction of carbon monoxide from biogas [ 11], 
anthracene bioadsorption [2], as a feedstock for ethanol production [46], removal of hexachlorocyclohexane iso-
mers from wastewater [9], and heavy metal adsorption [42], unripe peel-derived EDLC materials have received 
limited attention. Tadesse et al., synthesized activated carbon from raw banana peels using a two-step process 
of carbonization and chemical activation using KOH. They obtained a surface area of 565 m 2/g and a specific 
capacitance of 0.821 F g−1 [37]. Though the surface area is high the specific capacitance values were low which 
could be due to poor pore development or pore accessibility achieved in a two-step synthesis process. The self-
doping of heteroatoms and retention of biomass has been reported to be higher in a single-step process than a 
two-step process as there is biomass loss and also heteroatoms tend to etch out during the activation process [24].
Unripe plantain peels were selected as the biowaste precursor in this work as it has many advantages such as 
high availability, large yield and good scalability when compared to previously reported biomass materials. The 
unripe plantain peels have more rigid and stable lignocellulosic structures containing cellulose, hemicellulose 
and lignin which yields higher carbon content during thermal treatment. Furthermore, it has lower sugar content 
and less volatile content than ripe plantain peels reducing tar formation and structural collapse during thermal 
treatment. These rigid structures have low microbial degradation and are highly suitable for pore development 
and increased char formation required for easy handling and scalable production. Additionally, the plantain peels 
have nitrogen content naturally present paving way to self-doping of nitrogen [3].
This work aimed to optimize the synthesis process to obtain activated carbon with a high surface area and 
higher specific capacitance value from unripe plantain peel in a single step. To do that, a single step carboniza -
tion with activation by either KOH or ZnCl2 was chosen, and the surface and electrochemical properties of thus-
produced materials were analyzed for energy applications. The impregnation ratio was selected as 2:1, where 2 is 
the mass of activating agent and 1 is the mass of the carbon source based on its performance in our previous work 
[33]. Such pyrolysis strategy based on chemical activation enables the direct transformation of unripe plantain 
peel, a structurally complex agricultural biowaste, into high-performance nanostructured carbon, where the use

Experimental section
The unripe plantain peel was collected from a local savory shop in Chennai, India. The peel was cut into small 
pieces and washed thoroughly in water first and then in ethanol and acetone (Fig. 1). The washed peel was drained 
well and dried in a hot air oven at 100 °C for 48 h until completely crisp dry. The resulting dried material was then 
used as a carbon feedstock. Potassium hydroxide and Zinc chloride were used as activating agents and purchased 
from SRL chemicals, Chennai.
In each experiment, 20 mg potassium hydroxide was mixed in 50 ml distilled water until the salt dissolved 
completely. Then, 10 mg of dried plantain peel (2:1 impregnation ratio, where 2 is the mass of activating agent 
and 1 is the mass of the carbon source) was added into the activating agent solution. The dried peel was then 
allowed to fully soak in solution under sonication for 2 h, at which point the soaked peel was transferred into a 
hot air oven and kept at 110 °C overnight until completely dry. Once dried, the peel was transferred to an alumina 
boat and subjected to pyrolysis at 700 °C/800°C for 2 h under nitrogen atmosphere. Thus-obtained activated

Results and discussion
FESEM, HRTEM and selective area electron diffraction (SAED)
The FESEM images of K7, K8, Z7 and Z8 in Fig. 2a show a porous structure on all the surfaces, and Fig. 2b, 
c illustrate the 3D visualization of the surface. From the images it is noted that the porosity of the sample Z7 
was much higher which was also confirmed by the Brunauer-Emmelt-Teller analysis. The HRTEM image of Z7 
(Fig. 2d–f) shows an amorphous structure with the turbostratic arrangement of carbon structure due to the pres -
ence of heteroatoms oxygen and nitrogen. From the high-resolution images, it is concluded that the carbon struc-
ture is highly porous, with very small pore diameters as confirmed from the BET/BJH analysis. From Fig. 2g, the 
interplanar spacing (d) was calculated to be 0.206 nm for the (101), and is similar to the value calculated from 
the XRD (0.206 nm). The SAED patterns show a typical amorphous structure for the activated carbon without 
any crystalline particles as shown in Fig. 2h. Additional FESEM and HRTEM images (Figs. S1 and S2) could 
be found in the Supplementary Material (SM) available. Thus, the surface morphology was studied by FESEM, 
HRTEM and SAED reveals the amorphous nature of the carbon structures in Z7.
Furthermore, elemental mapping was analyzed on sample Z7 to investigate the distribution of key elements 
within the carbon matrix, as shown in Fig. 3. The images reveal the predominant presence of carbon, along with 
smaller quantities of heteroatoms such as oxygen and nitrogen. Notably, these heteroatoms are uniformly dis -
persed throughout the carbon structure, indicating successful self-doping. This uniform distribution contributes 
to the formation of a conductive network, which enhances charge transfer and supports efficient electrochemical 
performance. These observations are in strong agreement with the XPS analysis, further confirming the com -
positional homogeneity and the role of heteroatom incorporation in improving the functional properties of the 
material [12, 44].

Brunauer-Emmet-Teller (BET) analysis
The nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms of K7, K8, Z7 and Z8 seen in Fig. 4a, bottom panel suggest Type 
IV isotherms predominant in mesoporous structures. A sharp uptake at the low relative pressure (P/P o< 0.1), 
signifies the presence of micropores due to micropore filling of nitrogen in the carbon structures. All samples 
contain micropores with significant number of micropores in Z7. The mid-to-high relative pressure region (P/
Po= 0.4–0.9), resembles H4 hysteresis loop in all samples according to IUPAC classification. This is due to the 
capillary condensation and evaporation taking place in mesopores of the carbon structures. The overall nitrogen 
adsorption capacity is in the order Z7 > Z8> K8 > K7.
The pore size distribution of the K7, K8, Z7 and Z8 are shown in Fig. 4b, bottom panel. This is further con -
firmed by BJH data which confirms the presence of micro- and mesopores in all samples. The plots of K7, K8 
and Z8 show an almost uniform pore volume distribution which is much lower than that of Z7 which may be due 
to pore closures at higher pyrolysis temperature (800 °C). Z7 has the highest differential pore volume with domi-
nant mesopores centred around 2 nm to 5 nm along with micropores. The presence of interconnected micro- and 
mesoporous bimodal structures significantly enhances ion diffusion and electrolyte accessibility. This improved 
transport pathway reduces resistance and promotes faster charge–discharge cycles, directly contributing to supe-
rior electrochemical performance and overall energy storage efficiency.
From the plot it is noted that most pores range between 2 nm and 4 nm in Z7, which also contributes to higher 
pore volume contributed by mesopores and micropores. It is noted that the active surface area of the activated 
carbons was much higher in zinc chloride activated carbons (Z7 and Z8) when compared to that of the potassium 
hydroxide activated carbons (K7 and K8). The increase in pore volume also contributes to an increase in electro-
chemical charge storage. From the electrochemical analysis it is noted that the specific capacitance increases with 
the increase in pore volume (K7 < K8 < Z8 < Z7).

with the increase in pore volume, thus, in our case Z7 has higher surface area and pore volume forming higher 
number of efficient mesoporous structures which would enhance electrochemical performance [19].

Potassium hydroxide and zinc chloride activation mechanisms
The unripe plantain peels mainly contain lignocellulose [ 29]. The unripe plantain peels undergo KOH chemical 
activation at 700 °C and is schematically shown in Fig. 6a. Initially the KOH reacts with the lignocellulose to 
dehydrate the lignocellulosic structure at 400 °C. As the temperature increases to 700 °C, KOH reacts with the 
carbon atoms to form potassium bicarbonate and potassium oxide along with the release of hydrogen, carbon 
monoxide and carbon dioxide which creates cracks/pores in the structure [30].

The cyclic voltammetry analysis was performed on the three-electrode system for all the samples as shown in 
Fig. 7b. The CV graphs resemble quasi-rectangular curves due to the presence of heteroatoms such as oxygen and 
nitrogen as confirmed from the XPS and FESEM elemental mapping analysis. These heteroatoms considerably 
contribute to the electrochemical charge storage process. Also, from the XPS data, Z7 has a C/O ratio of 100.9 
and K7 has a C/O of 50.2. The C/O ratio can be crucial in the electrochemical performance of the activated car -
bons. A higher C/O ratio means higher carbonization and improved graphitization which directly improves con-
ductivity making ion diffusion faster and easier in the porous structure. Although higher oxygen content can cause 
diffusion resistance, an optimum oxygen content can increase wettability and improve the ion interactions at the 
electrolyte-electrode interface. Thus, the enhanced electrochemical performance in Z7 is attributed to higher C/O 
ratios which is much higher than K7 [43]. Equation (1) was used to calculate the specific capacitance values from 
the CV curves. The specific capacitance values obtained from the CV curves at 10 mV/s for K7, K8, Z7 and Z8 
are 95 F g−1, 135 F g−1, 415 F g−1, and 256 F g−1 and at 100 mV/s for K7, K8, Z7 and Z8 are 36.5 F g −1, 80.3 F 
g−1, 123.3 F g−1, and 100.9 F g−1 respectively.

surface area, optimized porous structure, favorable surface functionalities and increased conductivity. Z7 has the

lower series resistance and charge transfer resistance than other samples in EIS analysis indicating higher ionic 
conductivity. This enhanced performance of the ZnCl2 activated carbon at 700 °C is due to the selective removal 
of hydrogen and oxygen atoms forming highly porous carbons with sufficient defect sites and electrochemically 
active structure for charge storage when compared to the potassium hydroxide activated carbons.

The study shows that activating agents and pyrolysis conditions significantly impact the electrochemical per -

retained 79% capacitance after 4500 cycles. This single-step pyrolysis method demonstrates the strong potential 
of agro-waste as a sustainable, high-performance energy storage material.

Compared to prior studies on activated carbons derived from similar biowastes, this work achieved signifi -

tions and heteroatom doping in tailoring the structure and performance of biomass-derived activated carbons, 
setting the stage for the detailed conclusions drawn from these findings.
Conclusions
The nitrogen self-doped porous activated carbons were successfully synthesized using KOH and ZnCl 2 activat-
ing agents at different temperatures. The Z7 had higher specific surface area, higher porosity and higher charge 
storage capacity than K7. The sample Z7 which was activated at 700 °C showed excellent electrochemical per-
formance and had a high specific surface area of 1636 m 2/g, which is the highest among all the samples. This 
highly porous active surface area of Z7 attributes to higher electrochemical performance when compared to other 
samples fabricated in this study. The oxygen and nitrogen atoms also contributed to the pseudocapacitance in the 
carbon. Though the amount of nitrogen atoms present in K7 was higher than that in Z7, the presence of pyridinic 
N and pyrrolic N atoms in Z7 have enhanced the wettability and C/O ratio required for efficient charge storage, 
potentially further contributing to the pseduocapacitive electrochemical conductivity of Z7. From the previously 
reported work for activated carbon from unripe plantain peels, this work achieved a higher surface area and much 
better electrochemical performance with a much-simplified single step synthesis procedure for activated carbon. 
The activated carbon had abundant micropores and mesopores which enhanced the charge storage in the material 
which gave a specific capacitance of 167 F g−1 at 0.5 A/g in a two-electrode Swagelok set-up. The device gave an 
excellent energy density of 23 Wh/kg and a power density of 174 W/kg at 0.5 A/g current. A capacitance retention 
of 79% was achieved after 4500 cycles at a current density of 5 A/g with a coulombic efficiency of 99%. Thus, the 
synergistic action of high specific surface area and optimum nitrogen doping paved way for higher electrochemi-
cal performance in Z7. These results suggest that Z7 can also be used in many other applications such as water 
treatment, oil adsorption etc.
