1.1 Introduction 
The growing population and infrastructure along with the industrial revolution in 
the modern world tremendously raised the energy demand, increasing fossil fuel 
consumption [1]. The extensive utilization of petroleum resources leads to irreversible 
exhaustion and greenhouse gas emissions [2,3]. The energy scarcity and detrimental 
effect of fossil fuels on the environment stimulated the necessity of renewable energy 
from many sources such as solar, wind, and hydropower. However, the intermittent 
availability of these resources affects the continuous energy supply, which demands a 
solution for storing the energy in the form of electricity [4]. 
Conventional capacitors, batteries, and supercapacitors (SCs) are the energy storage 
solutions commonly used for electric and electronic applications. The electrochemical 
energy storage and conversion in all three systems follow the same thermodynamic 
and kinetic formulations of electrochemical reactions, in which the charge storage 
and release occur between the electrode-electrolyte boundary [5,6]. However, the 
mechanism of energy storage is different; thus, the SC shows energy density and power 
density higher than that of the conventional capacitor and battery respectively [7]. 
Fig. 1.1 shows the Ragone plot which displays the comparative analysis of various 
energy storage devices in terms of specific power and specific energy [8]. In the case of 
a conventional capacitor, the charge is stored between two conducting plates separated 
by a dielectric medium, and the SC shows exceptional performance over a capacitor 
and there is no dielectric material in it [9]. Using porous electrodes with high surface 
area along with their thin and lightweight design, offers SCs a significant commercial 
advantage over conventional capacitors. A capacitor can store only micro to milli 
Farads of capacity, whereas SCs can accumulate 100 to 1000 Farads of charge while 
maintaining the power [10]. SCs are preferable for applications in which a power boost 
is required, and significant energy can also be consumed at a faster rate than batteries 
[11]. The Faradaic redox reactions in the layered electrodes in a battery provide huge 
capacitance and hence energy density than that of SCs, but the charge-discharge rate 
is very low. The widespread commercial-level application of batteries is limited due to 
the high cost and long charging time [12].

1.2 Supercapacitors (SCs) 
SCs are also called as electrochemical capacitors (EC), ultra-capacitors, electric 
double-layer capacitors (EDLC), and gold capacitors [17]. Scientists in various 
countries such as the United States, Japan, France, Russia, South Korea, Switzerland, 
etc. initially started the research on SCs but now it is extended to all over the 
world. The commercial value of SCs is ratified on an industrial scale, hence many 
companies like Maxwell, Panasonic, NESS, NEC, and EPCOS are actively engaged 
in the development of SCs. The capacity, power, and cost of SCs are distinct for each 
country and currently, the SCs from the United States, Europe, China, Japan, Korea, 
and Russia are dominated in the market [18]. The application range of SCs is extending 
continuously which in turn prompts the researchers to improve the characteristics of 
the device as appropriate for high power and energy requirements. 
1.3 Greener aspects of SC vs battery 
The twelve principles of ‘Green Chemistry’ described in 1998 by Anastas et al. 
account for environment-friendly, economical, ecologically benign, and sustainable 
techniques while working with chemical processes [19]. The advanced technology of 
energy storage should fulfill the criteria regarding both efficiency and environmental 
concern since electronic waste becoming one of the biggest burdens of modern society 
in the race of contemporary life and growing technology [20]. The dependency on 
energy storage systems for industrialization is actually beyond the limit [21]. Still, 
the environmental impact caused by energy storage devices is much lower than that 
from fossil fuel consumption [22]. The metal ion batteries with high energy density 
are well established as energy storage devices while the environmental concern is 
not much considered in their commercialization [23]. According to the reports, more 
than ten billion batteries are being abandoned annually by global civilization including 
industries [24]. The short life span of 200–1000 cycles for batteries is the major reason 
for the high level of waste production, and insufficient power density limits their 
applicability. In addition, the manufacturing of batteries and their recycling process 
involves the use of toxic chemicals, stringent conditions, and/or tedious procedures. 
The lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), widely used metal ion batteries in electric and 
electronic devices for the past few decades, mainly contain graphite, lithium, and 
cobalt as the raw materials. The anode material in LIBs is mainly graphite which 
is available from either natural or synthetic routes. The recovery of graphite from 
the anode of LIBs using toxic solvents and the expensive routes make hurdles to 
sustainability. Cobalt mining is another tedious part of LIBs due to the wide use 
of lithium cobalt oxide as the cathode material [25]. Moreover, the battery contains 
several heavy metals such as Mn, Ni, Cu, Fe, and Al in high concentrations which are 
incombustible after disposal leading to detrimental effects. The environmental harm 
caused by the flammable electrolytes, halogen-containing binders, and other device 
components with toxic metals in batteries is also gaining attention from the scientific

1.5 The basic principles of SC 
The working principle of SCs is based on the storage of energy through the electric 
double-layer formation at the electrode-electrolyte interface [35]. When an electrode 
is immersed in an electrolyte solution, an electric double layer (EDL) region is formed, 
that can be represented as a capacitor in an electric circuit, and the EDL has a significant 
impact on the energy storage properties [36]. The electrochemistry of the double-layer 
formation at the surface of the electrode via the interaction of electrolyte ions has been 
proposed by several concepts and models [37,38]. An inclusive theory for the EDL 
formation was proposed by Grahame in 1947, called the electric double layer capacitor 
(EDLC) [39]. The adsorption/desorption of ions on the electrode-electrolyte interface 
is responsible for the charging and discharging of electrical energy in a SC [40].T h e 
major components of a SC are current collectors, electrodes, electrolyte, and separator. 
The current collector is the conducting substrate on which the electrode (anode or 
cathode) material is loaded and it works as the efficient channel for transferring the 
electrical signals from the electrode to the external power source. In a SC assembly, 
the positive and negative electrodes are connected with the electrolyte ions embedded 
on the separator between them. The structure and components of a SC are schematically 
presented in Fig. 1.3. 
The function of the separator is the same as the role of the separator in battery 
and dielectric in the conventional capacitor, while the thickness is in the range of the 
Angstrom to micro unit for SCs [41,42]. The separator prevents the short circuit of 
positive and negative electrodes while acting as a pathway for electrolyte ions [14].

applicability in high energy demands. 
1.6 Classification of SC 
From the discussion on the basic principle of SCs, it is clear that the electric double-
layer (EDLC) formation is quite simple, but this is not the only storage mechanism. 
There is another possible electrochemical reaction at the electrode-electrolyte interface 
mainly depending on the electrode material, which is also necessary to take into consid-
eration. The precise outlook of the charge storage mechanism in SCs classified them 
into three major categories namely, EDLCs, pseudocapacitors, and hybrid SCs, and 
more subclasses are also included, depending on the electrode material, combination 
of electrodes and electrolyte, etc [49]. The classification of SCs based on the energy 
storage mechanism is depicted in Fig. 1.4. 
1.6.1 EDLC 
The EDLC involves two conducting electrodes with a lower distance of separation and 
electrolyte. The charge storage mechanism in EDLCs follows the theoretical principle 
of SCs where the charge is stored on the surface of the electrodes via EDLC formation 
at the electrode-electrolyte interface without any irreversible reaction [50]. When a 
voltage is applied from the external power, the electric field generated in the electrolyte 
causes the movement of ions into the oppositely charged electrodes, thus forming EDLs 
which are similar to two capacitors connected in series [51]. The lack of chemical 
reactions made EDLCs withstand over millions of cycles than batteries and the electron 
transfer from the electrodes is quite quick, thus can offer great power density [52].The 
electrode material with a high surface area and suitable porosity is a major necessity 
for high-performance EDLCs since they can effectively utilize the entire surface and 
offer easy ion diffusion [53,54]. The high conductivity of the electrode material is also

1.6.2 Pseudocapacitors 
The second category of charge storage mechanism in SCs is referred to as pseu-
docapacitance, where the electron transfer reaction between the electrolyte ions and 
electrodes is important. Here, the energy is stored beyond EDLC via redox reactions 
and intercalation/de-intercalation of ions between the electrode and electrolyte, and 
hence more energy is transferred which results in higher capacitance and energy density 
than EDLCs [55]. When the active electrode with Faradaic behaviour is immersed in 
an electrolyte solution under the action of an external potential, the electrolyte ions 
will diffuse even into the immediate bulk of the electrode surface, thus, storing more 
energy than EDLCs [56]. However, the power density and durability of the SC are 
necessary to maintain as the pseudocapacitors usually compromise these features. This 
is because the redox reaction is comparatively slower than the electrostatic interaction 
and consequently, the power density of the device will be less [57]. To maintain 
the power density of the SC while using Faradaic materials, the introduction of an 
additional EDLC counterpart into the device is highly preferred [58]. The different 
energy storage mechanism in three major categories of SCs is shown in Fig. 1.6.

battery and SCs materials in terms of energy and power densities. The limitations 
in acquiring an energy density similar to that of battery for EDLCs necessitated the 
development of hybrid SCs to produce the combined result of high capacitance and

1.6.6 Supercapattery 
The battery-SC hybrid device is referred to as supercapattery, which can be represented 
as the next generation devices that could be able to meet the ultimate position in 
the Ragone plot. Here, the asymmetric configuration of a capacitive electrode and 
battery-type electrode is used which surprisingly boosts the energy density [77]. 
This hybrid device originated with the combination of a capacitive electrode with a 
lithium-ion storage electrode which is named a lithium-ion capacitor and later in 2007, 
the name supercapattery (supercapacitor + battery) was proposed. The efficiency of 
supercapattery close to metal ion batteries stimulates the fast and intense research on 
this category even using metal ions beyond lithium. The energy density of a metal-
ion battery is 120–200 Wh/Kg and the power density of a SC is in the range of 2 to 
5 kW/Kg or even more, hence the combination of these two parameters in a single 
device is the future target [59,77]. Here, the battery-type electrode can store the energy 
through redox reactions in the bulk of the electrode whereas the SC electrode exhibits 
an electric double-layer formation mechanism at the electrode/electrolyte interface and 
the combination could meet the energy and power outputs as exactly necessary for the 
future electronics and electric vehicles [78]. The ability of supercapattery to enhance 
not only the energy density of SC but also the cycle life of the battery enables its 
commercial value as an energy storage device. The capacity of a battery-type electrode 
is higher than that of the capacitive electrodes by several orders of magnitude which 
is the major factor responsible for the elevation in energy density of supercapattery. 
The working voltage window also has a significant effect on the energy density. Using 
a suitable battery-type electrode that works in a different potential window with a 
capacitive electrode can broaden the overall voltage of the full device. Thus, a large 
number of combinations are possible for the development of supercapattery where 
battery-type electrodes of lithium and sodium ions are extensively investigated [79]. 
Lithium-ion supercapattery can be based on either aqueous or organic electrolytes and 
lithium compounds as anode and carbon-based materials such as activated carbon as 
cathodes are widely used. Organic electrolytes for Li-ion supercapattery can be LiPF6 
or LiBF4 and aqueous electrolytes are usually from lithium salt solution with neutral pH 
like Li2SO4,LiNO3,etc [80–82]. The similarity in the physical and chemical properties 
of lithium and sodium along with the infinite abundance of sodium resources and

1.7.1 Graphene 
Graphene is the wonder material that played a crucial role as a SC electrode material 
in the energy sector owing to its high conductivity, large specific surface area, thermal 
stability, high electric charge mobility, high mechanical strength, etc [85]. The pristine 
graphene is highly conducting due to the extented aromatic π-conjugation over the 
two-dimensional (2D) hexagonal lattice, thus can easily form the electric double layer 
in a suitable electrolyte with fast electron transfer [86]. Even though the possibility 
for any other chemical reaction while using pristine graphene electrodes is almost 
negligible, the EDLC mechanism itself displayed a theoretical capacitance of 550 F/g 
for graphene due to the large theoretical surface area of 2630 m2/g [87]. Still, the 
tendency of graphene monolayers to undergo irreversible restacking due to the van der 
Waals forces had a detrimental effect on the SC performance of graphene electrodes 
due to the reduced surface area accessible for the electrolyte ions [88]. During the 
fabrication of SC electrodes, the addition of conducting additives like carbon black or 
carbon nanotubes (CNTs) can prevent the restacking of graphene layers to some extent. 
However, pristine graphene without any functionalities reaches a capacitance value of 
100–200 F/g only, which is much less than the theoretical capacitance [89]. The specific 
capacitance of graphene can be improved via constructing other graphene derivatives 
such as zero-dimensional graphene quantum dots (GQDs), one-dimensional graphene 
nanoribbons, 2D graphene with other nanoparticles, 3D graphene foams, and holey 
graphene nanostructures [90–92]. Graphene is commonly produced via Hummers’ 
method, which is not recommended in a green context due to the corrosive chemicals, 
drastic evolution of toxic/hazardous gases, and stringent reaction conditions. The other 
methods for graphene production such as chemical vapor deposition (CVD), unzipping 
of CNTs, and silicon carbide heating require high pressure and temperature, and the 
cost of production is also high; thus, the commercialization is not wide [93]. The yield 
of electrode material is highly significant for the upgradation of SCs and researchers are 
doing intense work on the production of high-quality graphene via green methods such 
as ball milling of graphite, intercalation method, liquid phase exfoliation, reduction of 
graphite oxide via green reducing agents, etc [94]. Liangyu et al. proposed a green and 
one-step method for the large-scale production of graphene doped with atomic nickel, 
nitrogen, and oxygen using the ball milling technique (Fig. 1.8). An asymmetric SC 
with the tri-doped graphene as cathode and activated carbon as anode delivered an 
energy density of 25.6 Wh/Kg at a power density of 400 W/Kg with a life span of 
87.5% capacitance retention over 10000 cycles [95]. 
The 3D graphene with interconnected porous networks is another interesting cat-
egory for researchers as it delivers high energy density without compromising the 
lifespan [96]. Huan et al. reported a scalable method for the preparation of O/N-
doped graphene with a hierarchical porous 3D structure using camphor leaves as the 
carbon precursor with a specific capacitance of 397 F/g at a current density of 1 
A/g [97]. Sandhya et al. reported a green and single-step method of synthesis of N-
S doped graphene nanosheet with 3D structure as SC electrode via carbonization of 
tender palm shoot biomass (Fig. 1.9). A flexible solid-state SC using the 3D graphene 
with PVA/H2SO4 gel electrolyte exhibited an energy density of 12.6 Wh/Kg at a

1.8 Electrolytes in SCs: investigation in the viewpoint of 
a green chemist 
Electrolytes are vital constituents that have a great impact on the performance of SC 
in terms of both energy and power density. The efficiency of an electrolyte can be 
explained using characteristics such as ionic conductivity, electrochemical stability 
window (ESW), temperature stability, chemical stability, environmental friendliness, 
safety, and cost [156]. The energy density of SC mainly depends on the charge stored 
(Q) and working voltage (V), as already discussed, where both of these parameters can 
be altered through variation in the electrolyte. The charge and hence the capacitance 
depend on the diffusion of ions into the pores of the electrode material. Here, the size of 
electrolyte ions can determine the extent of ions diffusion into the surface and/or bulk 
of the electrode as we discussed in the previous section. The working voltage of the 
device is limited to the decomposition voltage of the electrolyte, hence the electrolyte 
for SC should be chosen with a wide ESW. The compatibility of the electrolyte with 
the electrode can also affect the ESW. Furthermore, the stability of electrolytes is 
necessary to work at various temperature ranges. The lower ESR value of SC is an 
attractive feature for energy storage devices which is mainly governed by electrode 
and electrolyte interaction. An electrolyte with a lower value of ESR will provide 
strong ionic conductivity and hence good power density. The other factors such as 
the mobility of ions, amount of charge carriers, and valency of mobile ions are also 
key in determining the conductivity of ions. The ionic conductivity can be described 
based on the effect of solvation where both the dielectric constant and viscosity of 
the solvent must be considered. The dissociation of salt into constituent anions and 
cations were enhanced by a higher dielectric constant of the solvent by reducing the 
ion recombination, thus increasing the ion conductivity [157]. However, the viscosity 
of solvent imparts an adverse effect on the mobility of ions, i.e. higher viscosity results 
in lower mobility. From the literature study, the viscosity and dielectric constant of a

methylpyrrolidinium tetrafuoroborate (MEPYBF4), and tetramethylene-pyrrolidinium 
tetrafuoroborate (TMPYBF4) dissolved in organic solvents are used as electrolytes 
in SCs. Propylene carbonate (PC) and acetonitrile (ACN) are the commonly used 
organic solvents, and the mixture of solvents in various combinations is also used to 
get the desired properties [161]. According to the literature studies, the most studied 
organic electrolytes for pseudocapacitors contain lithium owing to the smaller size 
of Li+ appropriate for the insertion/de-insertion of ions into the electrode surface 
during charge-discharge processes [162]. The larger size of ions in organic electrolytes

1.8.2 Ionic liquids 
Ionic liquids are another promising alternative for electrolytes in SC devices. The salts 
used in ionic liquids are a combination of organic cations and organic/inorganic anions 
with a melting point of less than 100°C. The physicochemical properties of ionic liquids 
can be altered with a variety in the quantity and combination of cations and anions. The 
constituent cation and anion of ionic liquids are the major determining factors of ESW 
and the range of working temperature of the electrolyte. Thus, the research on how 
the properties of ionic liquids vary according to the combination of different ions can 
determine the SC performance. Ionic liquids possess various attractive features such 
as high thermal and electrochemical stability, non-flammability, high voltage window 
(∼3 V), solubility, and high conductivity [163]. The commonly investigated ionic 
liquids are based on imidazolium, pyrrolidinium, ammonium, and sulphonium, etc., 
as cations and tetrafluoroborate (BF4), hexafluorophosphate (PF6), and dicyanamide 
(DCA) as anions. The high viscosity is the major limitation associated with ionic 
liquids which restricts the commercialization of SCs using ionic liquid electrolytes 
[164]. Mixing ionic liquids with organic solvents becoming an efficient strategy to 
improve the conductivity as well as to reduce the viscosity of ionic liquids [156]. Since 
the size of ions in ionic liquids is larger than in aqueous electrolytes, the optimization 
of the porosity of electrode material is necessary to ensure efficient ion diffusion. 
The wide potential window offered by organic electrolytes and ionic liquids can 
enhance the energy density, though fluorine-containing salts create a serious threat 
to the environment. The commonly used salts of BF4 are highly carcinogenic and 
create volatile fluorocarbons that harm the environment as well as human health. The 
widely used organic solvent in the case of organic electrolyte is acetonitrile which 
is also highly flammable creating safety concerns. The decomposition of acetonitrile 
generates hazardous and toxic cyanides and together ionic liquids also possess toxicity 
towards the environment and living organisms [165]. The use of organic electrolyte 
or ionic liquids additionally demands highly sophisticated instrumentation and exper-
imental setup for the fabrication which is expensive and not easy to handle even in 
the laboratory. The cost of the organic solvents and ionic liquids also impart difficulty 
in the commercialization of SC in an economic approach. The alternate electrolytes 
should be affordable, safe, easily available, easy to handle, and disposable without any 
negative impact on the environment, but with a promising voltage window.

1.8.3 Aqueous electrolytes 
Aqueous electrolytes are the most attractive part of SC devices in the perspective of 
green chemistry owing to their environmentally benign nature, ease of availability, low-
cost, and easy handling in both laboratory and industrial scale applications. This cat-
egory of electrolytes has higher conductivity than organic and ionic liquids especially 
due to the small size of the constituent ions of electrolytes and this will lower the ESR 
value enhancing the power density. According to the thermodynamic equilibria, the 
ESW of water is low (1.23 V), and above which the water undergoes decomposition via 
hydrogen or oxygen evolution reactions. Hence the voltage for a symmetrical EDLC or 
pseudocapacitor using aqueous electrolyte is limited to 1.23 V [166]. This is the major 
limitation that restricts the use of aqueous SCs in real sense applications especially in 
higher voltage needs. There is a large number of electrolytes studied in water such 
as KOH, NaOH, H2SO4,N a2SO4,K 2SO4,L i S O4, MgSO4, KCl, NaCl, LiCl, HCl, 
KNO3, and LiNO3 etc., where H2SO4, KOH and NaSO4 are the commonly employed 
acidic, alkaline and neutral electrolytes respectively [157]. The ionic conductivity and 
mobility of H+ and OH− ions are higher due to the hydrogen bond interaction which 
led to the higher conductivity for both acidic and alkaline electrolytes compared to 
the neutral one. However, the corrosive nature of both acidic and alkaline electrolytes 
results in a poor cycle life of the SC and they are not much recommended for higher 
voltage applications. The less corrosive nature and comparatively wide ESW due to 
lower concentration of H+ and OH− ions in neutral electrolytes offer a safer usage 
than acidic and alkaline electrolytes [167]. The superior performance of aqueous 
SCs in terms of high voltage and good energy density can be achieved through the 
higher surface area electrodes with suitable electrolytes in an asymmetric electrode 
configuration, where the voltage of a device can reach even up to 2.4 V [168].T h e 
leakage issue of liquid electrolytes also limits the widespread use of the SC which 
can overcome by gel polymer electrolytes or solid electrolytes. The development of a 
suitable greener electrolyte could enhance the sustainability of SC and the asymmetric 
hybrid or supercapattery design elevates the energy density. Aqueous electrolytes are 
greener and safer in this context, whereas the low value of ESW limits the superior 
performance than battery. The extensive investigation on SC devices using aqueous

electrolytes is the limited voltage window (<1.23 V) due to the presence of aqueous

1.9 Development of greener SC devices 
The development of SCs as a clean and green energy storage device with much attention 
to compatibility, and applicability is necessary for a sustainable life. The materials 
synthesized in green methodologies, the use of greener chemicals/electrolytes, bio-
degradable components, and facile device fabrication are efficient approaches for the 
growth of SCs without environmental concerns. Improving the energy density of 
SCs up to the level of batteries without compromising the specific power and cycle 
life in a sustainable approach is the recent trend in research on SCs. The economic

sustainable society. The performance of SCs is greatly influenced by the active material 
constituting the electrode, and the electrolyte should be chosen according to the type 
and morphology of the electrode. Thus, commercial viability of green SCs can be 
achieved via appropriate synchronization of the charge storage mechanism in the 
device and suitable electrode-electrolyte combinations. 
Another component of the SC is the separator which ensures the ionic conductivity 
between the electrodes. The commonly used separators are made up of binder materials 
like polytetrafluoroethylene which is an F-based binder that causes the generation of 
fluorocarbon and the disposal of these materials also creates serious environmental 
issues. The easily available, green, and low-cost porous membranes with good electro-
chemical stability, ionic mobility, and safe disposal are ideal alternatives for separators 
of greener SCs. The separator based on cellulose acetate, filter paper (Whatmann), and 
nylon is also used in SC, which helps attain excellent electrochemical performance 
[184]. 
The current collectors and sealing materials in SCs are made up of incombustible 
aluminium, steel, and rarely copper which are non-degradable in the land after the 
disposal of SC [165]. Recently, carbon-based current collectors have emerged as an 
alternative to metallic current collectors in SCs which are free from any environmental 
impact. Carbon-based materials possess eco-friendliness and cost-effectiveness appro-
priate for sustainable commercial SCs. The thickness of carbon current collectors is 
much smaller and they are flexible which opens up a great opportunity to fabricate 
flexible SCs [185]. Three types of current collectors are developed from carbon-based 
materials which are CNTs-aligned paper-like structures, carbon fiber-based mats, and 
finally graphite foil or sheet. Among the three types, graphite current collectors exhibit 
excellent conductivity and capacitance performance over others [26]. Moreover, the 
flexibility of the graphite foil possesses additional features beneficial for flexible SCs. 
Other substrates made up of carbon such as carbon foams, and carbon cloth are also 
extensively used in SC applications as current collectors since they are green materials.

green SCs with good energy density while maintaining high power density and cycle 
life. The electrode, electrolyte, separator, and current collector must be based on bio-
energetic materials or prepared with cost-effective and environmentally harmonium 
features for ensuring SCs as green energy storage devices for the sustainability and 
economic well-being of society.

1.11 Challenges and prospects in green SCs 
The major challenge associated with the development of green SCs is the energy den-
sity gap between conventional SC and metal ion batteries. Metal ion batteries dominate 
in electric and electronic devices and have an energy density of 30 to 200 Wh/Kg which 
is still a challenge for SC. Lithium-ion batteries used in mobile phones possess an 
energy density of 150 to 200 Wh/Kg whereas the SC with similar dimensions can 
exhibit an energy density of only 5 to 10 Wh/Kg. The complete potential of SC has 
not yet been explored due to insufficient research and investigation on the electrode 
and electrolyte materials. From the perspective of green chemistry, bio-derived elec-
trode materials with high surface area, and eco-friendly electrolytes are necessary to 
develop and enhance the energy density of green SCs. On behalf of the discussion on 
challenges in various electrolytes, an electrolyte that acquires all the criteria of high 
ESW, good ionic conductivity, negligible leakage, low viscosity, cost-effectiveness, 
and environmentally benign nature is a challenge to be investigated. Thus, electrolyte 
modification in a rational way to address the existing issues is required. Despite all the 
above factors, aqueous electrolytes are the ideal candidate for sustainable development, 
and the use of redox additives or saturated electrolytes called water in salts is the 
efficient approach for overcoming the ESW limitation of aqueous electrolytes and 
for improving energy density. The precise outlook on the aging mechanism of SC is 
another challenge aroused in the practical applicability of SCs. The aging of SC can be 
due to the decomposition of electrolytes, damage to the outer case, and phase change 
or degradation of the electrode material. The decomposition of electrolytes through 
some chemical reactions causes the generation of pressure inside the device, which 
will limit the long-term use of the SC. The practical use of SC is not much viable 
when compared with the experimental performance, mainly due to the occurrence of 
self-discharge which is the sudden fall of voltage after the SC is fully charged. The 
redistribution of charge through the multiscale pores of the electrode surface can cause 
self-discharge. In addition, the impurities present in the electrode or electrolyte along 
with the wide temperature range of operation also influence the self-discharge process. 
Even though the development of SC and its practical use without self-discharge is 
unattainable, reducing the discharge rate to a minimum extent is a necessary part of 
the research. During practical use, higher voltage is applied to the entire device, thus it 
is necessary to check whether each device in the series connection shows the desired 
voltage. The cost of SC due to lack of extensive research is the significant factor, that 
limits commercialization in an economic route since the batteries are still available 
at an affordable cost when compared with SCs. The electrode material, electrolyte, 
and fabrication techniques constitute the cost of a SC; hence the research on efficient 
electrodes and electrolytes should consider the concern about environmental impact 
and economic cost. The development of green SCs using eco-friendly components 
additionally makes SC fabrication free from inert environments and sophisticated set-
up, which is highly advantageous for commercialization. 
Supercapattery and their flexible varieties are emerging technologies for future 
sustainability since they can meet the energy and power requirements near metal ion

batteries offering additional compatibility. However, novel electrode materials satis-
fying the mechanism of supercapattery are necessary to develop in a green and facile 
method. Flexible SCs are established in the field of miniaturized gadgets owing to the 
thin, lightweight, and compact structure of the device. The recently emerged category 
called interdigitated SCs is used as micro-SCs, which can easily be integrated into 
various portable, flexible, and wearable devices. Metal ion batteries store higher energy 
density while their use in wearable devices harms the human body due to their toxic 
electrolytes. Moreover, the massive structure of batteries hurdles their miniaturization 
and transportation. Interdigitated SCs have a planar structure where the electrodes are 
separated in a single plane with polymer gel electrolyte and the device configuration is 
free from an additional separator. The advantages of gel electrolytes over conventional 
electrolytes provide good energy storage properties to the interdigitated SCs. The series 
or parallel connection of these devices could develop in planar configuration with good 
compatibility; the device exhibits all the advantages of SCs such as high-power density, 
fast charge-discharge rate, and long lifespan. A tremendous research scope exists for 
commercializing micro-SCs with novel electrodes having better capacitance because 
they can make remarkable effects in portable devices in various fields such as sensors, 
communication chips, and processors. 
Thus, future research should focus on the development of novel electrodes and 
electrolytes with proper knowledge about the mechanism of the electrode-electrolyte 
interaction. The materials and methodology should be based on green strategies with 
strong concern about the cost of production. Apart from the innovation of novel 
materials, the design and fabrication of the device also required constant research and 
engineering to reduce self-discharge and electrolyte leakage. The use of compatible 
materials and components is recommended for green SCs to deliver a high energy 
and power density with long durability and this approach could eliminate the harmful 
impact on the environment by reducing electronic waste. 
1.12 Conclusions 
In conclusion, the urgent need to move away from fossil fuel reliance highlights 
the critical role of developing efficient energy storage technologies. SCs offer a 
viable alternative, known for their rapid energy storage and discharge capabilities that 
can outperform conventional battery systems. The focus on creating environmentally 
friendly SCs, which incorporate sustainable electrode materials and green electrolytes, 
is crucial in promoting both ecological responsibility and economic feasibility. Despite 
their clear advantages, SCs face challenges regarding energy density when compared 
to batteries, which calls for ongoing research and development. Future efforts should 
aim to improve the energy storage capabilities of SCs while maintaining their eco-
friendly characteristics, and cycle life enhancing their viability as independent energy 
sources or in combination with batteries. As research progresses, SCs have the potential 
to significantly impact energy storage solutions for electric and electronic devices, 
ultimately contributing to a more sustainable and resilient energy future.
