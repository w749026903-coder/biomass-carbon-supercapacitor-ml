ABSTRACT
Biomass and biowaste resources can be used to create self-  doped carbon with a distinctive microstructure. Using an economical 
and environmentally friendly method to create heteroatom- doped carbon electrode materials with excellent electrochemical per-
formance has attracted much attention in the energy storage industry. A novel facile two- step, low- cost, and eco- friendly synthesis 
method for Colocasia esculenta peels has been developed to manufacture activated carbon (CEPAC) and used as an electrode 
material for supercapacitor application. The CEPAC 1:1 displayed a high specific surface area of 910 m2/g with oxygen- heteroatom 
polar sites in the carbon network. A specific capacitance of 525.3 F/g was recorded in the three- electrode system using a 3 M KOH 
solution. The assembled symmetric cell delivered an impressive specific capacitance of 98.7 F/g at 1 A/g while maintaining 98.4% 
of the initially recorded capacitance after 10 000 charge– discharge cycles. These results present a promising low-  cost and simple 
processing route for synthesizing electrode materials with superior surface properties for high- performance supercapacitors.
1   |   Introduction
Considering the recent fast-  growing demand for energy supply 
to meet the sustainable development goals, fast-  depleting fossil 
fuel energy sources pose a major concern. Industrialization and 
civilization are the critical aspects of sustainable development 
that need an enormous energy supply. Fossil fuel energy re -
serves are linearly declining because of the relatively long time 
required for mineralization [1]. With this, there is a need to find 
other alternative energy sources to augment the existing fossil 
fuel. Renewable energy technologies have been in the system for 
a while to complement fossil resources [ 2]. These new energy 
supply technologies are clean and environmentally friendly and 
may be affordable. To outwit the present energy demand crisis in 
the world, energy conversion and storage systems are required 
to harness clean and environmentally stable renewable energy 
sources. Among the fast-  growing and promising energy stor -
age and conversion systems that have attracted, the attention of 
modern researchers are supercapacitors [ 3– 5], fuel cells [ 6, 7], 
rechargeable batteries [8], and metal– air batteries [9, 10].
One of the significant drivers of the new energy storage and 
conversion systems is their pristine electrode materials, 
such as carbon-  based hybrids [ 11], especially carbon-  based

composites, graphene [ 12], carbon nanotubes [ 13], and so on. 
The traditional precursor materials usually adopted as elec -
trodes are inherently costly and produce relatively low yields. 
Constant optimization of the energy storage precursor mate -
rials has led to a renewed interest in the utilization of car -
bon from biomass sources that are environmentally friendly 
[14, 15 ]. These alternative carbon precursors use green syn -
thesis approaches such as hydrothermal [ 16, 17 ], pyrolysis 
[18], and electrospinning [ 19].
Recent evidence suggests that biomass-  derived carbon precur -
sors are the key to harvesting renewable energy in enormous 
quantities, which represents a major milestone in the domain of 
energy technology [20]. Biomass- derived carbon electrodes pos -
sess a wide range of advantages over their counterparts obtained 
from petroleum coke such as heteroatoms self-  doping onto the 
precursor substrate, large specific surface area (SSA), low cost 
of precursor production, and high specific pore volume [ 21– 23]. 
Hence, biomass-  derived carbon electrodes may offer a solu -
tion to the drawback of low energy density of supercapacitors 
[24, 25 ]. Electrode materials synthesized from biomass waste 
reportedly offer high specific capacitance due to the large SSA, 
sufficient defect sites, and micro/mesopores [26]. Also, there are 
abundant oxygen-  containing functional groups that enhance 
electrode conductivity. Biomass-  derived carbon is reported to 
contain heteroatoms such as potassium, phosphorus, nitrogen, 
and oxygen [26, 27].
Although extensive research has been carried out on waste 
biomass materials, some waste biomass materials remain un -
explored. Due to this, large quantities of biomass are rendered 
annually as waste which even becomes an environmental pol -
lutant. It is, therefore, logical to convert the ever-  increasing 
natural biomass wastes into electrode materials for energy 
storage applications. The carbon derived from biomass sources 
possesses unprecedented properties and has found applications 
in wearable electronics [ 28, 29] and fast energy storage systems 
[30, 31]. More recently, researchers have shown keen interest in 
plant biomass sources as these are in high abundance [ 14, 32]. 
Plant biomass that have been studied over the years includes 
coconut shells [33], basswood [ 3], leaves [34], stems [35], starch 
[36, 37], bean shell [38], peanut shell [39], miscanthus grass [40], 
lotus leaves [41], and cauliflower [42].
Supercapacitors (SCs) are one of the outstanding sought-  after 
essential energy storage devices that are considered by the re -
search and development (R&D) community for fast energy stor -
age in most of the recent electronic devices such as wearable 
electronics and cell phones [43]. Research on the supercapacitor 
subject has been mostly restricted to the electrode materials and 
their derivatives. Due to this, the electrode materials usually 
define the purpose of the supercapacitors, which can either be 
electrical double-  layer capacitors (EDLCs) or pseudocapaci -
tors, the two principal charge storage mechanisms presented 
by the different types of electrode materials. There has been a 
lot of interesting ongoing research seeking to improve the elec -
trode properties to maximize the performance of the electrode 
materials.
Biowaste-  based carbons have been utilized as promising 
EDLC electrodes over the years. The synthesis methods that 
have been adopted for activated carbon production are usu -
ally expensive and require advanced instrumentation. It is 
necessary to adopt a simple, cheap, and eco-  friendly approach 
to produce carbons with superior properties. In this work, a 
novel facile two-  stage activation protocol was employed to 
synthesize activated carbon (AC) from biowaste precursor 
(Colocasia esculenta  peels [CEPs]) using KOH as the activa -
tion agent. The AC derived from this precursor (CEPAC) was 
employed as an electroactive material in EDLC energy storage 
applications. This precursor is abundant locally and contains 
inherent heteroatoms, which may be beneficial for electro -
chemical energy storage applications; hence, exploiting its 
benefits is necessary. The use of the precursor not only cre -
ates electroactive material but also helps to revive the useful -
ness of the biowaste and creates a circular economy for the 
local people. Two different activation ratios (carbon:KOH; 1:1 
and 1:0.5) were used. The as-  synthesized AC was tested as an 
electrode material in 3 and 6 M KOH solutions of three-  and 
two-  electrode systems, respectively. The CEPAC 1:1 displayed 
a high SSA of 910 m2/g with numerous mesopores. The as -
sembled cell delivered a high specific capacitance with stable 
capacitance retention during cycling. These results present a 
promising low-  cost, facile, and environmentally friendly pro -
cessing route for synthesizing electrode materials with supe -
rior surface properties for high-  performance supercapacitors.
2   |   Materials and Experimental Method
2.1   |   Materials
The Colocasia esculenta  corm samples were obtained from the 
Ejisu market in the Ashanti region of Ghana. Analytical grades 
of chemicals were used in this study. A nickel foam current col -
lector of dimensions (300 × 300 mm2 area and 1.2 mm thickness) 
and density of 0.45 g/cm3 with corresponding 95% porosity was 
purchased from Merck & Co, Kenilworth, New Jersey. The car -
bon cloth (15 mm diameter and 0.2 mm thickness) was purchase 
from Fisher Scientific. Potassium hydroxide (KOH, 95% pu -
rity) and hydrochloric acid (HCl, 35.5% purity) were purchased 
from Danniemma Medichem Venture Gh Limited. The polyvi -
nylidene fluoride (PVDF) was procured from Zhejiang Fluorine 
Chemical New Co, China. Dimethyl formamide (DMF, 99% pu -
rity) was purchased from VWR Chemicals, France.
2.2   |   Experimental Methods
2.2.1   |   Sample Preparation and Precarbonization
The fresh C. esculenta  corm samples were initially washed with 
tap water in a bowl to get rid of mud and debris. The samples 
were subsequently air-  dried for 24 h, after which the corms 
were peeled and chopped into smaller pieces using a knife. The 
CEPs were sun-  dried until there was no significant change in 
sample weight, followed by oven drying at 105°C for 24 h. After 
progressively drying, the samples became crispy and were eas -
ily crushed using mortar and pestle. Fifty grams of the crushed 
samples were loaded into a crucible and precarbonized at a tem-
perature of 250°C for 3 h in a muffle furnace at a heating rate of 
5°C/min.

2.2.2   |   Synthesis of AC From CEPs
For the activation of the precarbonized CEPs, potassium hy -
droxide was utilized as the activation agent. Two activation 
ratios of 1:1 and 1:0.5 (CEP:KOH) were used to synthesize the 
ACs. For the 1:1 ratio, 5 g (2.5 g for the 1:0.5 ratio) of pulverized 
KOH was mixed with 5 g of the precarbonized CEP and homog -
enized in an evaporation dish. The homogenized mixture of the 
precarbonized CEP and KOH was transferred into a porcelain 
crucible and then covered with a lid. The sample in the porcelain 
crucible was embedded in a bigger crucible; this is a slight mod -
ification of the work reported by Yeboah [ 44]. Prior to this, the 
bigger crucible was initially filled with green sand to the brim 
to prevent easy access to oxygen molecules, which can signifi -
cantly reduce the yield. This was done to create an inert con -
dition for the activation process. The sample was activated in a 
muffle furnace at a temperature of 750°C for 2 h at a heating rate 
of 5°C/min. After the activation process, the samples were thor -
oughly washed with 0.1 M HCl to ensure a neutral pH. After acid 
washing, the mixture was filtered using Whatman filter paper 
(Grade 1, 240 mm ∅), and the residue was oven-  dried at 105°C 
for 24 h to obtain the AC. The CEPs-  derived ACs were denoted 
as CEPAC 1:1 and CEPAC 1:0.5. Figure  1 shows the facile two- 
stage schematic preparation process for the CEPAC samples.
2.2.3   |   Fabrication of Electrode Materials 
and Supercapacitor Cell
The working electrodes for the electrochemical analysis were 
fabricated using a binder-  to- AC ratio of 1:9. For the electrode 
preparation, 10 wt.% of PVDF was mixed with 90 wt.% of the AC 
in a petri dish. Four drops of N, N-  dimethyl formamide were 
added to the mixture and ground to form a slurry which was 
then coated on a nickel foam current collector of dimensions 
(1 × 4 cm). The mass of the active material deposited on the cur -
rent collector was approximately 3 mg. The working electrode 
was oven-  dried for 24 h at a temperature of 105°C. After 24 h 
of drying, the electrodes were then cooled to room temperature 
before the electrochemical studies in a three-  electrode setup. 
A symmetric two-  electrode SC cell was also assembled in coin 
cells (2032 CR stainless steel) using carbon clothes as the current 
collector and 6 M KOH electrolyte solution.
2.2.4   |   Electrochemical Measurement
The electrochemical performance of the synthesized acti -
vated carbon electrodes was investigated using a Corrtest 
Electrochemical Workstation (Model CS350) in the three- 
electrode setup and Parstat MC Workstation (AMETEK, 
Princeton Applied Research) for the two-  electrode system. 
Electrochemical tests such as cyclic voltammetry (CV), elec -
trochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and galvanostatic 
charge–  discharge (GCD) were performed in a three-  electrode 
system using Ag/AgCl reference electrode and graphite as the 
counter electrode. The working electrode used in the three- 
electrode system for the electrochemical analysis was the 
fabricated AC electrodes. All the electrochemical tests were 
performed in 3 and 6 M KOH electrolytes, respectively, at a po -
tential window of 1.2 V (−1.2–  0 V) at room temperature (25°C). 
The CV tests were carried out at scan rates ranging from 5 to 
100 mV/s, while the current density used for the GCD investi -
gation ranged from 1 to 3 A/g. The EIS test was performed at 
5 mV amplitude and in a frequency range of 10 MHz to 100 kHz.

densities ( P) of the various electrode materials from the GCD

2.3   |   Material Characterization
The surface morphology of the synthesized AC was investigated 
with a Tescan Solaris FIB-  SEM (Tescan Orsay Holding, Brno, 
Czech Republic) using ultra-  high- resolution scan mode with 
both secondary (SE) backscattered (BSE) detectors. The textural 
properties and SSA of the AC materials were studied using nitro-
gen adsorption techniques with surface and pore size analyzer 
Quantachrome Instruments NOVAtouch LX2. Phase composi -
tion identification and crystallinity of the synthesized AC mate -
rial was carried out using the Bruker D8 Advance Diffractometer 
(Bruker, MA, USA) with Cu Kα1 radiation ( λ = 1.54 Å) attach-

als were collected at room temperature using a DXR2xi Raman 
Imaging Microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) with 
a 532- nm line Ar- ion laser excitation source.
3   |   Results and Discussion
3.1   |   Structural Characterization
Microstructural images of CEPAC 1:0.5 and CEPAC 1:1 are 
presented in Figure  2a,f, respectively. Although both CEPAC 
samples were prepared from the same source/precursor, the 
difference in the porous network is likely due to the activation 
protocols used.
CEPAC 1:0.5 exhibited uniformly distributed macropores as can 
be seen from Figure  2a. On the contrary, CEPAC 1:1 showed a 
cluster of mesopores interconnected with a continuous carbon 
structure as shown in Figure  2f. The morphology of CEPAC 1:1 
suggests that using equal portions of the precursor and KOH for 
activation offers electrodes with desired mesoporous structures, 
unlike in the case of CEPAC 1:0.5. During the activation pro -
cess, small and low-  volume pores are activated which increases 
the surface area of the carbonaceous materials available for ad -
sorption. It is clear from the SEM images that small and low- 
volume pores were effectively activated with the activation ratio 
of 1:1. Aside from the morphological differences, the presence of 
carbon and impurity elements in the CEPAC samples are shown 
in the energy-  dispersive x-  ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental 
maps in Figure 2c–  e,h,i.
Using equal amounts of precursor and KOH, no traces of im -
purity elements such as Si were found in CEPAC 1:1. In addi -
tion to the main constituent carbon (C), only traces of oxygen 
were noticed in the elemental maps as provided in Figure  2g– i. 
However, substantial amounts of Si and O were found in CEPAC 
1:0.5 as presented in Figure  2b– e. Phase composition analysis 
via x- ray diffraction provided results which agreed well with the 
EDS maps. The diffraction pattern presented in Figure  3a for 
CEPAC 1:1 showed the characteristic peaks of AC for reflections 
from (002) and (101) planes at peaks position 24° and 42°, re -
spectively. In Figure  3b, the presence of quartz (SiO 2) is seen in 
addition to the main graphitic carbon phase. These diffraction 
patterns confirm the results provided in the EDS maps and sug -
gest a possible removal of impurities using an equal amount of 
precursor and KOH.
The highly sensitive Raman scattering revealed the typical 
peaks of graphitic carbon as shown in Figure  3c,d. Raman shifts 
at 1347 and 1583 cm−1 which correspond to the D (“disorder”) 
and G (“graphite”) of highly amorphous graphite carbon [ 45] 
were observed for both CEPAC samples. The R - values for both 
CEPAC samples were estimated using the ratio of the peak in -
tensity of the D-  band (ID) to the G-  band (IG). This dimension -
less parameter is widely used to distinguish between carbon 
types (diamond or graphitic) as well as a basis for understand -
ing the structure of the carbonaceous material (crystalline or 
amorphous). According to existing literature, a high R-  value 
symbolizes a high disorder and, thus, a lower crystallinity. The 
estimated ID/IG ratios of the CEPAC samples are provided in 
Figure  3c,d and suggest somewhat amorphous carbonaceous 
material. The higher values obtained in this work also suggest 
more disorderliness/defects in the prepared samples [ 46– 50], 
which is beneficial for energy storage capabilities.
At a temperature of 77 K, nitrogen adsorption was assessed em -
ploying a high-  speed surface area analyzer, the Quantachrome 
Instruments NOVAtouch LX2. Prior to the measurements, the 
samples were subjected to degassing within a dynamic vacuum 
for a minimum of 120 min at 180°C, with a gradual tempera -
ture increase of 10°C per minute. The SSA was derived from the 
nitrogen adsorption data within the range of relative pressure 
(P/P0) from 0.05 to 0.30, utilizing the Brunauer–  Emmett– Teller 
(BET) methodology. To determine the distribution of pore sizes 
within the CEPAC 1:1 and CEPAC 1:0.5 samples, the Barrett– 
Joyner– Halenda (BJH) approach was applied to the relative 
pressure range (P/P0) from 0.35 to 0.98. Notably, the analysis ex-
clusively considered the desorption phase of the isotherm.
For the CEPAC 1:1 and CEPAC 1:0.5 samples, Figure 4a,d shows 
the BET isotherm plot in linear coordinates, and Figure  4b,e 
shows the corresponding pore size distribution of the same. 
The CEPAC 1:0.5 displayed a remarkable SSA of roughly 
265 m2/g, whereas the estimated SSA of the CEPAC 1:1 was 
around 910 m2/g. The findings show that the CEPAC 1:1 had 
a pore radius (Dv[r]) of 1.8 nm, a pore surface area of 71 m2/g, 
and a pore volume of 0.08 cc/g. The CEPAC 1:0.5 exhibited a 
slightly lower pore radius (Dv[r]) of 1.9 nm, a slightly larger pore 
surface area of 102 m2/g, and a slightly greater pore volume of 
0.21 cc/g. These numbers show that the SSA of the CEPAC 1:1 
was significantly higher than that of the CEPAC 1:0.5. These 
results can be attributed to the KOH activation in higher quan -
tities in CEPAC 1:1, thereby, creating a larger surface area. The 
high SSA can contribute to an enhanced specific capacitance, 
rate capabilities, energy density, and cycling stability of the su -
percapacitor device. The obtained pore volume could also fa -
cilitate ionic movement, leading to improved electrochemical 
performance. The adsorption–  desorption N 2 isotherm of the 
CEPAC 1:1 sample exhibited a Type IV isotherm, indicating a 
mesoporous structure as shown in Figure  4c. Conversely, the 
CEPAC 1:0.5 sample (see Figure  4f) is indicative of a Type III 
behavior, suggesting a macroporous structure characterized by 
weak interactions.

3.2   |   Electrochemical Performance of the EDLC 
Electrode
Based on the structural characterization, the as-  synthesized AC 
materials possessed high SSA and numerous mesopores, making 
it a suitable electrode material for supercapacitor applications. A 
three- electrode system was designed in 3 M KOH and 6 M KOH 
electrolyte solutions and used to investigate the electrochemical 
capacitive performance of the carbon materials. Analysis of CV, 
GCD, EIS, and cyclic stability tests were conducted to ascertain 
the electrochemical performance of the samples.
3.2.1   |   CV Analysis
The electrochemical performance of the electrode materials in 
the three-  electrode system was analyzed and the results dis -
cussed. Figure  5a,b shows the CV curves of the as-  synthesized 
CEPAC 1:1 in 3 M KOH and 6 M KOH, respectively, at differ -
ent scan rates and a wide potential window of 1.2 V, with a 
quasi- rectangular or isosceles shape which is a feature of 
EDLCs. The CV comparative analyses displayed higher vol -
umetric current, resulting in a higher specific capacitance at 
lower electrolyte concentration (3 M KOH). This result can 
be attributed to the direct effect of the potassium ions (K +) at 
the AC interface since there is enough time for ion migration. 
Such behaviors are evident, especially with decreasing scan 
rate (at 5 mV/s). The presence of oxygen heteroatom, which 
serves as some form of pseudocapacitive element [ 46, 51], also 
contributed to the improved performance as a result of higher 
redox reaction as shown in Figure  5c. Using a scan rate of 
5 mV/s, a specific capacitance of 472 and 307 F/g was recorded 
in 3 M KOH and 6 M KOH electrolyte solution, respectively. 
As the concentration of the electrolyte increases, there exists 
much competition of the ions during migration into the porous 
interiors of the AC, thereby decreasing the volumetric current 
(hence, lowering specific capacitance), as shown in Figure  5d. 
The lower capacitance recorded in higher electrolyte concen -
trations can also be attributed to the disruption of the active 
sites of the AC by the highly concentrated potassium ions. 
These results are shown in Table  1. To understand the effect 
of the activator on the sample during activation, the quantity 
of KOH was reduced by 50%. The sample was labeled CEPAC 
1:0.5. Figure  5e,f shows the CV results of the electrode materi -
als tested in 3 and 6 M KOH, respectively, at different scan rates 
(using −1.2–  0 V potential window). From the figures, similar

3.2.2   |   Galvanostatic Charge–  Discharge Analysis
For the maximum operating potential window of the EDLC, the 
electrode materials (CEPAC 1:1) were charged and discharged 
at different current densities (1– 3 A/g) in the two electrolyte 
solutions (3 and 6 M KOH) as shown in Figure  6a,b. It is evident 
that there are no deviations from the linearity of the charging 
regime with no visible IR drop during discharging. These linear 
and symmetric profiles of the GCD curves are typical of EDLCs. 
This gives rise to high columbic efficiency of the electrode mate-
rials. The specific capacitance increases as the current densities 
decrease. This is because enough time is allowed for ions to 
migrate into the active sites of the porous electrode materials 
at lower current densities. The recorded specific capacitance 
obtained from the GCD shows higher values at low electrolyte 
concentration (3 M KOH) than the 6 M KOH solution as listed 
in Table  2. A very high specific capacitance of 525.3 F/g was re-
corded when the electrode was tested in a 3 M KOH solution, 
whereas 253.8 F/g was recorded in 6 M KOH, all obtained at 
1 A/g current density. These results can also be attributed to the 
competition of the ions at high concentrations, limiting their 
migration into active sites of the porous electrode. During the 
stability tests, at 3000 charge–  discharge cycles, the electrode in 
3 M KOH solution recorded 96.6% capacitance retention, while 
the electrode in 6 M KOH solution recorded a 97.8% capacitance 
retention (see Figure  6c). This can be attributed to the slow deg -
radation of the few ions that migrated into the active sites of the 
electrode in the high- concentration electrolyte (6 M KOH). After

3.2.3   |   EIS Analysis
The Nyquist plot of the as- synthesized electrode materials tested in 
both solutions is presented in Figure  6g. The results describe the 
interfacial kinetics between the electrode and electrolyte. The plots 
show a semicircle and almost vertical line in the high-  and low- 
frequency regions, respectively. Table 3 shows the EIS equivalent 
circuit analysis results obtained from fitting using Figure 6h. The 
equivalent circuit analysis results revealed circuit components, in-
cluding electrical double- layer capacitance (Cdl), charger transfer 
resistance (Rct), solution resistance of the bulk electrolyte (Rs), and 
Warburg frequency parameter (Wo). The presence of the Warburg 
frequency parameter indicates that the electrode reaction kinetics 
were present in the supercapacitor system studied. From Table 3, it 
can be observed that the electrochemical performances of the two 
different AC samples, denoted as CEPAC 1:1 and CEPAC 1:0.5, 
were different when tested in 3 and 6 M KOH electrolytes, respec-
tively. Comparatively, it can be observed that CEPAC 1:1 in 3 M 
KOH exhibited relatively higher electrical properties than CEPAC 
1:0.5 samples in 3 and 6 M KOH, respectively.
CEPAC 1:1 in 3 M KOH showed circuit properties such as C dl 
of 984.62 μF, Rs of 0.830 Ω, and R ct of 0.368 Ω. Due to the rela -
tively low solution and charge transfer resistances observed in 
the 3 M KOH electrolyte, it is more likely that efficient charge 
conduction or diffusion probably occurred in the electrolyte. 
Also, CEPAC 1:1 shows Wo of 1.481 and 1.018 Ω/s ½ in 3 and 6 M 
KOH electrolytes, respectively. The Warburg frequency parame-
ter was favored in the 3 M KOH electrolyte as shown in Table 3 .
Again, it can be observed from Table  3 that there was an ap -
preciable change in C dl values of CEPAC 1:0.5 from 513.14 to 
196.02 μF for the 3 and 6 M KOH electrolyte medium, respec -
tively. This significant change can be attributed to the relatively 
high solution resistance of 13.872 Ω observed in the 6 M KOH 
electrolyte. Moreover, comparing the CEPAC 1:0.5 samples in 
both 3 and 6 M KOH, similar trends were observed with electro -
lytes having lower concentrations.
The results presented in Table  3 revealed possible circuit com -
ponents that can be associated with an ideal supercapacitor 
system. It was observed that electrolyte composition and elec -
trode material greatly influenced the electrical properties of 
the circuit elements of the supercapacitor system studied. One 
of the most significant factors that measure how well a super -
capacitor cell can store charges is its electrical double capac -
itance value. From Table  3, it can be seen that the activation 
ratio plays a vital role in the electrical double-  layer storage 
mechanism. CEPAC 1:1 sample exhibited the highest electri -
cal double- layer capacitance ( Cdl) as compared to CEPAC 1:0.5. 
Looking at the electrical double-  layer capacitance values, sev -
eral inferences could be drawn. It can be seen that CEPAC 
1:1 sample was more porous, indicating that using KOH of the 
same amount as the carbon material creates more mesopores 
for accommodating more charges, which is in agreement with 
the SEM and BET results. Another point worthy to note is the 
concentration of the electrolyte used. Usually, the activation 
agent creates pores that are similar to their ionic sizes, and 
since the activation agent is the same as the electrolyte, ionic 
diffusion could occur with ease.
However, increasing electrolyte concentration leads to a reduc -
tion in the Cdl values, and this might be due to the disruption 
of the pore networks by the highly concentrated electrolyte.

4   |   Conclusion
By using a simple, low-  cost, and environmentally friendly two- 
stage facile activation process, a sustainable biowaste-  derived 
carbon with numerous mesoporous textures was synthesized 
from CEPs. The as-  synthesized AC was used as an electrode 
material for electrochemical supercapacitor application. A 
specific capacitance of 525.3 F/g was recorded at 1 A/g in the 
three- electrode system using 3 M KOH solution, and the elec -
trode maintained 96.6% of this capacitance after 3000 charging 
and discharging. The assembled symmetric SC cell delivered 
an impressive specific capacitance, energy, and power densities 
of 98.7 F/g, 13.7 Wh/kg, and 500.5 W/kg, respectively, at 1 A/g. 
The device, after 10 000 charge–  discharge cycles, maintained 
98.4% of the initial recorded capacitance, suggesting that the 
CEPAC 1:1 can be used for a practical supercapacitor. With a 
focus on utilizing available and reasonably low-  cost biowaste 
with superior qualities as precursors for possible energy storage 
applications, the current work aims to increase the green and 
sustainable synthesis approach.
