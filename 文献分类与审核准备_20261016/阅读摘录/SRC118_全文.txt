1. Introduction 
Tobacco (namely Nicotiana tabacum ) is a cultivated 
plant that contains high levels of nicotine, an additive 
stimulant drug [1]. Although more than 75 species of
tobacco have been identified, only the species Nico -
tiana rustica L. and Nicotiana tabacum L . are com -
mercially grown to meet human consumption [2, 3].
N. tabacum ranks as one of the most prominent
species in the plant realm for its chemical and bio -
logical importance, encompassing approximately 
2500 identified metabolites to date, and a consider-
able amount is grown globally [4].
Official statistics indicate that more than 5.78 mil-
lion tons of tobacco were produced worldwide in 
2022 [5]. Tobacco items consist of cigarettes, cigars,
heated tobacco products, e -cigarettes, conventional
oral tobacco, and nicotine pouches. Cigarettes are 
the most prevalent with about 500 factories manu -
facturing 6 trillion cigarettes globally. Tobacco sales 
in the world are dominated by mostly cigarettes ac -
counting for 78.7% [6].
The health risks of smoking cigarettes are well
known with implication in at least 25% of heart dis -
ease deaths worldwide [7, 8]. In the Global North,

Abstract. Cigarette butts (CB s) are among the most abundant forms of anthropogenic litter worldwide and constitute a per-
sistent source of environmental contamination due to their slow degradation and the release of toxic substances into terrestrial
and aquatic ecosystems. In this review, we critically evaluate recent advances in the valorisation of CB  waste into value-
added functional materials. Particular attention is given to conversion strategies includ-ing chemical activation, electrospin -
ning, hydrothermal processing and nanocomposite fabrication. These approaches enable the production of activated carbons,
cellulose-derived na-nomaterials and metal-doped composites with promising applications in water purification, energy stor-
age, vector control and pollutant remediation. W e discuss the underlying mechanisms, performance, scalability and sustain -
ability of these technologies, and highlight current challenges, research gaps and future opportunities of large-scale imple -
mentation. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that CB s can be re-envisioned as valuable secondary resources that support
circular economy and sustainable material development strategies.

heart disease, while cardiovascular conditions claim 
a life every 33 s. Tobacco consumption affects not
only physical wellbeing but also results in shorter
life spans, lower productivity, and higher healthcare 
expenses. More than 15 billion cigarettes are con -

costs related to tobacco reaching approximately

The disposal of cigarette butts (CB s) is a global
issue. CB s are physically small and do not easily de -
grade, which poses significant toxicity risks to a 
wide range of organisms, including aquatic species,
insects, and both domestic and wild animals when it
is disposed in the environment. This toxicity arises 
from the presence and environmental release of nu -
merous hazardous compounds such as nicotine,
polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH s), phenolic 
derivatives etc. [10 , 11 ]. Collecting such small frag -
ments is difficult and costly, with urban expenses 
sometimes reaching millions of dollars [11 ]. B ecause 
of the sheer volume of cigarette use, the resulting 
waste dispersion, removal costs, and the hazardous 
substances found in persistent butts, it is crucial to 
prioritize solutions for this problem [12 , 13 ].
Despite the toxic effects of CB  waste on the envi-
ronment, CB s can be recycled into valuable prod -
ucts. Various researchers have examined treatment
methods for CB s to minimize their ecological foot-
print and repurpose the waste into secondary raw 
materials or commercially available goods.
Several review articles report on CB  pollution and 
their toxicity to the environment. Early reviews re -
ported on their toxicity and environmental distribu -
tion, highlighting the release of heavy metals and mi-
croplastics to the aquatic and terrestrial environment.
Marinello et al. [14 ] emphasized recycling methods 
of CB s and cellulose acetate recovery, while Kurmus 
and Mohajerani [15 ] reported on the toxicity of CB s 
and their valorization strategies. Again, B attista [16 ]
reviewed the waste valorization of CB s for biorefin -
ery technologies and Morales-Vera et al. [17 ] ex -
plored the biodegradation of cellulose acetate by mi-
crobial approaches.
Although reported reviews on the impact of CB s on 
the environment and valorization techniques have 
made an important contribution, the rapid expansion 
of research in this area requires more thorough and 
up-to-date assessments. To address this gap, we pro -
vide a comprehensive evaluation of cigarette butt
prevalence, chemical composition, pollutant release 
into aquatic systems, environmental impacts, and 
emerging valorization strategies. This includes as -
sessing the types and concentrations of toxic ele -
ments (i.e . cadmium, arsenic, lead, chromium etc. )
leached from CB s [18 , 19 ]. Accordingly, in this re -
view, we examine the environmental distribution of
cigarette butts, their compositional characteristics,
their filter structure, and the nature of contaminants 
released into water systems. In addition, we explore 
recent case studies, and the potential reuse and socio-
economic implications of CB  pollution. Collectively,
this study provides an integrated understanding of
CB -related pollution and its broader environmental
implications.
2. Methodology 
We performed a detailed investigation of existing lit-
erature via Google Scholar and Scopus and included 
articles up until 2025 to analyse the state of knowl-
edge regarding the impact and valorisation of ciga -
rette butt waste. Our search strategy was using key -
words such as “cigarette butts”, “cigarette filter” and 
“cigarette butt waste uses”. Only research articles,
book chapters, case studies and reviews were includ -
ed in the analysis, whereas in-press documents and 
retracted studies were omitted. We picked studies ac -
cording to set criteria to guarantee consistency and 
relevance. The search was restricted to articles with 
titles that included these keywords, and plural vari-
ations were also taken into account. To remove bias,
we established a predetermined set of criteria to 
guarantee that only articles containing quantitative 
data on the toxicity and uses of CB s were incorpo -
rated. Screening, a multi-stage assessment proce -
dure, was conducted for each finding identified in 
the literature.

shredded tobacco leaves encased in a fine paper
wrap. The cigarettes produced commercially today 
are made up of several key components (Figure 1).

There are various stages in tobacco production.
The first process involves tobacco cultivation and

combining the leaves through blending [20 ]. B y-

into the cigarette blend. To make smoke more ap -
pealing, additives are sometimes added and these 
include humectants such as propylene glycol or
glycerol. The function of these humectant compo -
nents is to maintain the moisture and flexibility of
the cigarette, and delay the drying out of the to -
bacco. Common additives, such as licorice and 
cocoa solids, are used to soften and conceal the 
harsh taste of the smoke [20 ].
•W rapping paper encloses the tobacco and is pri-
marily made from cellulose.
•Filters are used for filtration of the smoke, and 
they are made up of cellulose acetate. Cellulose 
acetate is a synthetic polymer produced by the 
acetylation of cellulose in the presence of plasti-
cizers such as polyethylene glycol, leading to CA 
fibres (Figure 1) [21 ]. The rate at which CA biode -
grades is determined by the level of acetyl substi-
tution along with the surrounding environmental
circumstances [17 ].
•The yellow paper (tipping paper) is used to cover
the filter and protect the mouthpiece from saliva.
It contains tiny ventilation holes that allow fresh 
air to mix with the smoke, thereby reducing the 
concentration of harmful toxic compounds inhaled.
H owever, after smoking, the tipping paper contain -
ing cigarette filter (CF) remains in the environment
and under ambient temperatures, it can take up to 
3–5 months for biodegradation to occur [22 ].
Cigarette filters (CFs) represent a central component
of a cigarette design. Introduced in the mid-20 th cen -
tury following a 1925 patent by B oris Aivaz, filters 
were developed to lessen the intake of smoke and tar
during combustion. They also help moderate the 
harshness of the smoke and prevent tobacco frag -
ments from entering the smoker’s mouth. In addi-
tion, filters act as reservoirs that retain a range of
toxic chemical elements. H owever, when the filters 
are littered, such toxic substances can seep into the 
environment [14 ].
A report by Mohajerani et al. [23 ] states that almost
90% of filters are produced from cellulose acetate 
except in countries like H ungary, Venezuela, South 
Korea, and Japan. CF consist of about 95% white 
CA, which are continuous filament fibres fused into 
a dense network. These fibres are poorly biodegrad -
able, contributing to the longterm persistence of CB s 
in the environment.
A plasticizer called glycerol triacetate is incorporated 
into CA found in CF [24 ]. CAs are esters produced 
through two steps: the first step is the esterification 
of ethanoic acid with cellulose, and the second one 
is the hydrolysis of the ester groups (Figure 2).
The processing and physicochemical properties of
CAs are the main components that determine their
applications in various sectors [25 ]. CA is widely 
used in the production of plastics, textiles, films and 
cigarette filters. After smoking, the remaining por-
tion of the cigarette, known as CB , typically makes 
up about 30% of its original length. During smoking,

CB s are the remains after smoking a cigarette. CB s 
can be problematic due to their slow degradation and 
the release of toxic elements into the environment.
It can take several years for CB s to fully decompose 
[31 ]. B onanomi et al. [32 ] reported that after a ten-
year period, soils with a high nitrogen content facil-
itate major chemical alterations of CB s and a loss of
their mass up to 84%. In a comparative study of the 
degradation of cellulose- and plastic-based cigarette 
filters, Joly and Coulis [33 ] estimated that conven -
tional plastic cigarette filters require approximately 
7.5 years to decompose under composting condi-
tions and up to 14 years when exposed on the soil
surface. B y contrast, cellulose filters were estimated 
to degrade within 2.3 years in compost and up to 
13 years on the soil surface. Low levels of nutrients,
particularly nitrogen, can also obstruct the degrada -
tion of CB s. The effects of nitrogen on litter decom -
position dynamics have been extensively studied in 
plant litter and other organic plant-derived sub strates 
[34 ]. Nitrogen is essential for effective de composi-
tion, particularly when the carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N)
ratio of a material exceeds the critical threshold of
approximately 30:1, as nitrogen limitations can re -
strict microbial growth and activity. As a result, mi-
croorganisms often draw extra nitrogen from exter-
nal sources like the soil beneath them and 
incorporate it into the nitrogen-deficient substrate to 
support their metabolic processes [34 ].
4. Environmental impact of cigarette butts 
Each year, approximately 4.5 trillion cigarette butts 
(CB s) are released into the environment, generating 
nearly 1.2 million tonnes of waste [35 ], as compared 
to different types of waste generated in the world such 
as (i)underground coal mining producing more than 
315 million tonnes of coal mine waste rock yearly 
[36 ] and (ii)biomass waste generated from an esti-
mated 731.3 million tonnes of rice straw, 354.34 mil-
lion tonnes of wheat straw, 180.73 million tonnes of
sugarcane bagasse (180.73 million tonnes), and 
128.02 million tonnes of corn stover produced annu -
ally [37 , 38 ].
According to the International Coastal Cleanup (ICC)
programme, cigarette butts remain the most com -
monly collected form of litter during coastal clean-
up activities worldwide. Data collected from partic -
ipating countries indicate that millions of cigarette 
butts are removed annually from beaches and coastal
environments, highlighting the persistent nature of
this pollutant [14 ]. For example, cleanup campaigns 
have reported the collection of more than 3.5 million 
cigarette butts in Ghana, while substantial quantities 
were also reported in Canada and Chile. These find -
ings underscore the global scale of cigarette butt

The role of heavy metals in cigarette butts 
The concentration of heavy metals in tobacco prod -
ucts varies depending on cigarette type. H aleem et al. 
[19 ] examined multiple tobacco brands and reported 
that concentrations of Pb, Cd, Cr, and Zn ranged 
from 1.24 to 9.26 μg/g, 0 to 1.56 μg/g, 0 to 6.73 μg/g,
and 0.17 to 3.11 μg/g, respectively. Similarly,
Michael et al. [39 ] investigated heavy metal distri-
bution in Indian cigarettes across different price cat-
egories. Their study quantified Cu, Fe, Pb, Sn, Zn, H g 
and Cd in both smoked and unsmoked cigarettes, as 
well as in the corresponding cigarette butts. Smoked 
cigarette butts showed the highest concen trations of
Cd (0.52 ±0.01 μg/g), Cu (162.94 ±6.80 μg/g), Pb 
(19.86 ±2.39 μg/g) and Sn (869.05 ±19.11 μg/g).
In contrast, unsmoked cigarettes contained the 
high est levels of H g (305.34 ±8.14 μg/g) and Fe 
(105.48 ±0.39 μg/g), while unsmoked butts had the 
greatest Zn concentration (45.21 ±1.48 μg/g). Over-
all, both smoked and unsmoked cigarette butts from 
midrange and lowercost brands were associated with 
higher heavy metal content.
Further investigation is needed to determine whether
similar patterns can be observed in higher-priced cig -
arette brands. In addition, previous studies have con -
firmed the presence of heavy metals in cigarette 
butts collected from environmental settings.
For instance, Dobaradaran et al. [40 ] analysed sam -
ples from nine coastal sites along the northern Per-
sian Gulf (B ushehr region). Their findings showed 
that concentrations of Cd, Fe, As, Ni, Cu, Zn and Mn 
ranged from 0.16 to 0.67 μg/g, 79.01 to 244.97 μg/g,
0.12 to 0.48 μg/g, 1.13 to 3.27 μg/g, 4.29 to 
12.29 μg/g, 6.39 to 21.17 μg/g and 38.29 to 
123.1 μg/g, respectively.
Another study by Konar in the 1970’s focused on the 
investigation of the effect of nicotine on fish [41 ].
The authors wanted to find out whether nicotine 
could serve as an effective anaesthetic for fish within 
the industry, serving as an alternative to the highly 
toxic sodium cyanide and rotenone used during that
period. Using a 40% nicotine solution to create var-
ious dilutions, the authors observed that the fish suf-
fered from severe poisoning, gill paralysis, seizures 
and eventual death.
Another study demonstrated that leachates derived 
from cigarette butts are highly toxic to aquatic or-
ganisms. Slaughter et al. [42 ] evaluated the effects 
of smoked cigarette butts, smoked filters, and un -
smoked filters on the marine topsmelt (Atherinops 
affinis ) and the freshwater fathead minnow (Pime -
phales promelas ). The results showed acute toxicity 
at relatively low concentrations, with LC50 values 
close to one cigarette butt per litre for smoked ciga -
rette butt leachates. Smoked filters were less toxic 
than whole smoked cigarette butts, while unsmoked 
filters exhibited the lowest toxicity (Figure 5). All
control treatments maintained survival rates above 
90%, satisfying EPA test validity criteria.
Similarly, Soleimani et al. [43 ] reported significant
toxicity of CB  leachates toward freshwater cladocer-
an Daphnia magna. The 48-hour LC50 values ranged 
from 0.125 to 0.25 cigarette butts per litre for smoked 
tobacco, 1 to 2 cigarette butts per litre for smoked 
filters, and great-er than 16 CB s per litre for un -
smoked filters, further demonstrating the ecological
risks posed by cigarette butt–derived contaminants.
Jakimiuk et al. [44 ] reported on the growth of plants 
when affected by CB s. They examined the germina -
tion of model plants (Sinapis alba L. and Hordeum 
vulgare L.) when the plants were subjected to pollu -
tion by CB s. They concentrated on understanding

The littering of CB s to the environment has raised 
serious concerns as they can be involuntarily be in -
gested by humans [50 ]. Due to the small size of CB s 
and children’s natural curiosity, the ingestion of
these CB s is mostly prevalent among infants. Inges -
tion of CB s by infants can have a variety of negative 
outcomes, including choking and toxic effects from 
nicotine and presence of heavy metals. According to 
earlier research, children who are exposed to nico -
tine may experience acute symptoms such as nausea 
and vomiting, with an estimated lethal dose of
1.0 mg/kg body weight [50 , 51 ].
A fatal case of accidental ingestion of e-cigarette 
nicotine by a 15-month-old child was reported by 
Seo et al . [50 ]. The consumed dose (~3 mg/kg body 
weight) caused severe hypoxic brain damage and 
cardiopulmonary collapse, above the predicted lethal
dose for children. The child was pronounced brain 
dead after 43 days of intensive supportive care and 
continuous cardiac resuscitation. The child’s death 
was reported on the 44 th day after they developed se -
rious complications including cerebral oedema. This 
case study seriously emphasizes the serious toxicity 
of consuming nicotine in children. A fatal case was 
reported by Carmago et al. [52 ] on a one-year-old 
maltese dog following the ingestion of CB s. The an -
imal suffered from recurrent ventricular arrhythmias,
vomiting and tachypnea, which resulted in the death 
of the animal despite intensive supportive care. This 
case study also shows the severity of consuming CB s 
in animals.

5. The socio-economic impact of pollution 
by cigarette butts 
CB  pollution is harmful to the environment and pose 
a significant socio-economic impact worldwide. The 
littering of CB  waste puts municipalities and gov -
ernments in financial burdens due to the need of fre -
quent street cleaning, waste collection and drainage 
maintenance.
Removal of CB  waste is costly since CB s are physi-
cally small and are widely spread. Again, CB  litter
make places aesthetically unpleasing and can result
in negative economic activities. Furthermore, drains 
are mostly blocked by these CB s and this results in 
increased maintenance costs. As a result, CB  litter is 
continuously causing a socio-economic burden, in -
creasing financial spending, reducing the quality of
the environment and indirectly resulting in loss of
health and productivity. This emphasizes the need for
stronger waste management policies, public aware -
ness campaigns and effective valorization strategies.
6. Potential use of cigarette butts 
Despite the negative impacts of cigarette butts on the 
environment, they can be useful in different appli-
cations. CB s contain cellulose acetate that has poor
biodegradability, which can take several months to 
biodegrade, which negatively affects the environ -
ment. Conventional methods for disposing solid lit-
ter, such as landfilling or incineration, are neither
sustainable nor cost-effective for CB s. Consequent-
ly, researchers can investigate suitable techniques for
reclaiming waste CB s. Recently, various viable ap -
proaches for recycling or repurposing cigarette butts 
(CB s) have been investigated. These include their
use in construction materials like fired clay bricks 
[53 ], their conversion into mosquito insecticides, and 
the creation of highly fluorescent carbon dots [54 ]
or other carbon-based materials [24 , 55 , 56 ]. Since 
CB s primarily consist of cellulose acetate, which is 
rich in carbon atoms, they serve as an excellent pre -
cursor for carbon-based materials utilized in numer-
ous industries.
6.1. Cigarette butts and their potential use in 
energy storage 
Supercapacitors are energy storage devices that store 
energy through electrostatic charge. W ang et al. [55 ]
developed a technique to create hybrid supercapac -
itor electrodes from discarded CB s (unmodified), en -
abling the concurrent recycling of metal ions. The 
CB s were added to NH 4V O 3and further heated in a 
tubular furnace to form carbon fiber@ Vanadium ni-
tride (CF@ V N) (Figure 6a ). CF@ V N was utilized 
as an active electrode material in the construction a 
supercapacitor device (Figure 6b ). SEM micro -
graphs of the CF@ V N are presented in Figure 6c 
and Figure 6d . The nanofibers morphology is shown 
by the low magnification SEM image in Figure 6c .
Furthermore, the TEM image (Figure 6e ) further
confirms the nanoscale morphology of the CF@ V N 
hybrid electrode material, complementing the sur-
face morphological features observed by SEM. Al-
though CB s consist of cellulose acetate fibres, they 
retained their fibrous structure even after being treat-

processing. An asymmetric supercapacitor was as -

both human health and aquatic ecosystems. Among 
the available treatment approaches, chemisorption

remediation due to its operational simplicity, cost ef-
ficiency, high removal efficiency at low contaminant
concentrations, and its relatively non-toxic by-prod -
ucts. H amzah and Umar [24 ] reported the synthesis

CB  waste and evaluated their performance in remov -
ing diclofenac from water, reporting an adsorption 
capacity of 107.9 mg/g, thereby demonstrating the 
enhanced adsorption efficiency of CB -derived mate -
rials. Similarly, Pu et al. [60 ] demonstrated that CB s 
can be converted into activated carbon suitable for
uranium adsorption. The process involved hydrother-

6.3. Recyclability of cigarette butts 
The recommended materials for highways are bitu -
minous due to their unique traits, but they often suf-
fer from deterioration such as cracking and rutting 
under prolonged heavy loads and extreme heat [64 ].
This raises the technical demands on bituminous 
paving. In recent years, research has indicated that
the exceptional physical and chemical attributes of
fibres make them widely used as effective additives 
in binding agents [65 ]. The results from Tang et al. 
[66 ] suggested that adding fibres may strengthen the 
interface, with basalt fibre showing a notably signif-
icant enhancement. According to B onica et al. [67 ],
the mechanical attributes of bitumen mortar used for
roads are positively influenced by cellulose fibres,
specifically improving hot mix mortar results. Used 
cigarette filters are being employed in building ma -
terials because of their substantial volume, lightness 
and capacity to spread heat. Furthermore, Mohajerani
et al. [68 ] mixed recycled cigarette filters into clay 
bricks to balance the energy consumption involved 
in the logistics of transporting substrate bitumen.
6.4. Vector control 
Vector organisms can directly infest food through 
biting, thereby contributing to its contamination,
posing risks to human health. They can also dissem -
inate pathogens through both biological and mechan -
ical means. W hile insecticides have been widely uti-
lized to manage vectors in areas prone to dengue, the 
emergence of resistance in these species has signif-
icantly diminished their efficacy.
H ence, Dieng et al. [69 ] investigated the impacts of
cigarette filtrate on Aedes albopictus concerning 
oviposition, larval shedding and growth. They dis -
covered that elevated levels of cigarette filtrate con -
siderably inhibited oviposition, decreased larval
shedding and diminished larval viability. In 2013,
scientists evaluated the impact of CB s on Aedes ae -
gypti mosquitoes, discovering that larvae faced con -
siderable mortality [70 ]. Later 2014, researchers in -
vestigated whether the exposure of CB s could 
change biological characteristics of adult mosquitoes 
and their larvae [71 ]. Their results showed that the 
toxicity of CB  arises from its intricate chemical
makeup: nicotine interacts with nicotinic acetyl-
choline receptors, altering normal neural signalling 
through excessive stimulation, which in insects can 
lead to impaired functioning of the nervous system,
paralysis and death. B y contrast, tar, heavy metals 
and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons primarily 
cause toxicity at the cellular level by damaging 
structural components, disrupting metabolic activity 
and for certain compounds, inducing genetic damage 
associated with long-term health effects. The harm -
ful effects depend on concentration, where elevated 
levels lead to immediate larval death, while lower
levels result in sublethal consequences like slowed 
development, decreased fertility in adults and a

Despite the excellent applications of CB s in energy 
storage, vector control, adsorption and building ma -
terials, their widespread use is still hampered by sev -
eral practical and economic limitations. One of the 
primary issues associated with valorization of CB s 
is that they contain residual nicotine, heavy metals 
etc. [18 , 20 ]. This results in complications of repro -
ducible material synthesis and requires extensive 
pretreatment and purification procedures that raise 
cost and energy demand.
Moreover, the current systems of collecting and sort-
ing CB  waste are underdeveloped. Although CB s are 
common in urban areas, their scattered distribution 
results in high cost for organized collection and pro -
cessing. H ence, it is difficult to have large scale up 
synthesized materials from CB s as compared to 
other waste precursors such as marula nutshells, co -
conuts shells, palm kernel shells, walnut shell and 
linn seed shells, which can result in complicated in -
dustrial processing routes [80–83 ].
The use of CB s waste in energy storage such as su -
percapacitors remains a challenge as CB s contain 
toxic metals [15 ], which are difficult to maintain at
industrial scale level and this may limit practical ap -
plications of CB s as commercial electrode materials.
Although the use of CB s in construction materials 
seem feasible and scalable as their requirements for
pure material is low, issues arising from potential
leaching into the environment, long-term durability 
must be addressed before large scale use. Despite 
the reported potential valorization of CB s waste for
use in various applications, practical scaling remains 
difficult due to inconsistent feedstocks, widespread 
of CB s, logistics in collection, expensive processing,
and environmental toxicity arising from heavy met-
als found in CB s waste. Addressing these challenges 
will depend on optimizing processes, integrating 
waste management, and utilizing life-cycle assess -
ment strategies to ensure feasibility.
7. Conclusions 
Cigarette butts (CB s) represent a globally pervasive 
and persistent form of anthropogenic pollution, aris -
ing from the large-scale consumption of tobacco 
products and the environmental recalcitrance of cel-
lulose acetate filters. This review has highlighted the 
multifaceted environmental burden of CB s, demon -
strating their role as continuous sources of toxicants-
including nicotine, heavy metals, and polycyclic aro -
matic hydrocarbons that adversely affect aquatic 
organisms, soil chemistry, plant development, and 
broader ecosystem health. The persistence of CB s in 
diverse environments, coupled with their capacity to 
release contaminants over extended timescales,
serves as a long-term environmental impact. At the 
same time, emerging research reveals a compelling 
opportunity to reframe CB s as a secondary resource.
Advances in materials science have enabled the 
transformation of CB s derived cellulose acetate into 
functional materials for applications spanning water
purification, energy storage, adsorption technolo -
gies, and vector control. These approaches align with 
principles of circular economy and waste valorisa -
tion, offering pathways to simultaneously mitigate 
environmental pollution and generate value-added 
products. H owever, critical challenges remain, par-
ticularly regarding scalability, economic feasibility,
potential secondary contamination, and the environ -
mental footprint of processing methods.
Addressing the CB s crisis therefore requires an in -
tegrated and multidisciplinary response. Policy in -
terventions, including extended producer responsi-
bility and targeted waste collection systems, must be 
coupled with public awareness initiatives to reduce 
environmental leakage. Concurrently, research ef-
forts should prioritise lifecycle assessments, green 
processing technologies, and the safe deployment of
CB s derived materials in real-world applications.
Ultimately, the transition from pollutant to resource 
demands a paradigm shift that bridges environmen -
tal science, materials innovation, and regulatory
