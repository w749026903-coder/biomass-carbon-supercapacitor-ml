Abstract 
 
Carbon materials generated from biomass garner significant interest for supercapacitor applications 
owing to their elevated specific capacitance, affordability, environmental sustainability, and the 
availability of natural precursors. In this work, oxygen -rich porous carbons are fabricated using 
sodium thiosulfate (Na₂S₂O₃)—a less toxic activating agent compared to conventional chemicals. The 
structural and textural characteristics of the resulting carbons are strongly influenced by the synthesis 
parameters. Among the prepared samples , HTWB -0.5-900 exhibits a well -developed hierarchical 
network consisting of micro -, meso -, and macropores and possesses a high surface area of 1018.74 
m²/g, features that enhance ion transport and increase the accessibility of electrocatalytically active 
sites. When applied as a supercapacitor electrode, the HTWB -0.5-900 material, obtained at 900 °C 
using a Na₂S₂O₃/HTWB mass ratio of 0.5/1, delivers the highest specific capacitance of 175 F/g at 0.5 
A/g in 1 M KOH. Furthermore, this electrode exhibits exceptional cycling stability, maintaining its 
initial capacitance after 10,000 charge -discharge cycles, even at a current density of 10 A/g. Overall, 
the findings highlight the potential of converting hookah tobacco waste into efficient and sustainable 
electrode materials for high-performance supercapacitors. 
 
Keywords: Sodium thiosulfate activating, hookah tobacco waste, supercapacitor, biochar, chemical 
activating 
 
1. Introduction 
 
In recent years, an increasing number of researchers have concentrated on transforming biomass 
and biomass waste into high -value goods [1]. Statistics show that China, the world's largest producer 
of tobacco, produces an estimated 1.20x10 6 tons of waste tobacco annually  [2]. Originating in North 
and South America, tobacco is a plant with broad leaves that is farmed for use in  waterpipes. These 
days, it has spread around the globe, and the dried leaves are known as tobacco [3]. A common form of

tobacco known as the hookah first became popular over four hundred years ago. Over a quarter of the 
populace in the eastern Mediterranean is thought to smoke hookah  [4–6]. The name, shape, and size of 
the hookah (e.g., waterpipe, shisha, hubble -bubble, nargileh, and argileh)  vary depending on the 
location [4,5]. The chemical compositions of the smoke produced by cigarettes and hookahs are 
somewhat similar. Both of them include a variety of poisonous substances, including volatile organic 
chemicals, radionuclides, metals, volatile aldehydes, ultrafine particles, and polycyclic aromatic 
hydrocarbons (PAHs), as well as particulate matter, tar, carbon monoxide, nicotine, and volatile 
organic compounds  [7–9]. As the use of hookah increases day by day, useless waste is created. 
Therefore, it is important in practice to learn how to recover hookah tobacco waste with high value and 
high efficiency. The ideal alternative for this is to produce activated carbon from hookah tobacco  
waste. The a ctivated carbon materials have been created using a variety of biomass or biological 
wastes, including Moringa oleifera [10], pea pods [11], date palm fiber [12], mango leaves [13], Pisum 
Sativum peels [14], prickly pear seed  [15], waste cooked rice  [16], cocoa leaves [17], mandarin peels 
[18], pitaya fruit peel [19], Water Hyacinth [20]. 
It is important to note that porous activated carbon is the best type of electrode for 
supercapacitors. Porous carbon generated from biomass may acquire unique microstructures from its 
antecedents, which serve as active locations for numerous  ion transport channels and energy storage.  
The two most significant factors that would have an impact on the electrochemical properties of 
carbon materials are their high specific surface area and hierarchical porous structure  [21]. There may 
be enough active places for charge storage with a large specific surface area. The appropriate pore 
structure can enhance the area of the electrode -electrolyte interface and facilitate the ingress of 
electrolyte ions, hence augmenting capacitance  [22]. Previous research indicates that the majority of 
porous carbons were synthesized by a two -step procedure  [23–25]. Pre-carbonization was the first 
step, followed by pyrolysis or activation. There have been numerous studies done in the past that have 
favored low-temperature pyrolysis before carbonization. Hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) has a 
significant impact on the synthesis of hydrocarbons and has the ability to add oxygen -rich substituents 
to carbon-based materials [26]. Hydrothermal carbonization technology is also an economical, simple, 
and efficient  technique [27]. The preparation process is one of the most often employed 
thermochemical conversion procedures for biomass carbon -based materials, attributable to two 
advantages. The efficiency of the ensuing activation process is improved by hydrothermal 
carbonization and chemical activation, which lowers the overall cost of manufacturing. The final 
material's microstructural properties are enhanced by the two-step procedure, too.

The main factors influencing the capacitive characteristics of carbon materials are the 
appropriate pore size distribution and specific surface area.  The specific surface area of carbon 
compounds is often increased through chemical activation and physical activation. Due to its low 
temperature, low cost, high yield, and quick activation period to create  porous structures, chemical 
activation has garnered interest.  Commonly used activators are H2SO4 [28], ZnCl2 [29], KOH [30], 
NaOH [31], etc. 
However, the use of chemical activation is becoming progressively problematic because of the 
significant corrosiveness and toxicity associated with this approach. In addition to these concerns, 
chemical activation often results in low production yields, thereby constraining its potential for large -
scale industrial deployment  [32]. Consequently, extensive efforts continue to focus on developing 
alternative synthesis routes that employ inexpensive, sustainable carbon precursors together with 
benign, non-corrosive activating agents. 
Recent studies have introduced comparatively benign activating agents —such as sodium 
thiosulfate [33], potassium bicarbonate  [34], and potassium carbonate  [35]—for the preparation of 
porous carbons. In particular, Sevilla and co -workers [36,37] explored environmentally friendly routes 
for producing porous carbons from diverse biomass -derived precursors, such as sucrose,  gelatin, and 
glucose. In their approach, aqueous sodium thiosulfate served as the activating agent, while potassium 
chloride functioned as the reaction medium. This synthesis route is considered green and sustainable, 
as it relies solely on environmentally safe materials such as Na₂S₂O₃, KCl, water, and biomass. The 
resulting carbons exhibited high BET surface areas (reaching ~2600 m² /g) and delivered specific 
capacitances of approximately 200 F /g in aqueous electrolytes. A further significant finding of their 
work is that sodium thiosulfate enables the incorporation of sulfur functionalities into the carbon 
matrix through a self-doping mechanism. 
In this study, sodium thiosulfate is employed as a benign activating agent to produce porous 
carbon from hookah tobacco waste (HTW). To our knowledge, porous carbon synthesized from HTW 
using Na 2S2O3 as the pore -forming species has not been previously documented. During activation, 
Na2S2O3 decomposes at high temperatures to yield sodium sulfate, a highly reactive species that plays 
a key role in generating pores within the carbon framework. The resulting sample, HTW B-0.5-900, 
exhibits a sponge -like morphology with a large specific surface area of 1018.74 m² /g and a well -
developed hierarchical pore structure. Notably, the fabricated electrode material demonstrates 
enhanced electrochemical performance, attributed to shortened ion -diffusion paths, rapid charge 
transport through the porous network, and an expanded interfacial area accessible to electrolyte ions.

2. Experimental section 
2.1. Chemicals and reagents 
The hookah tobacco waste (HTW) used in this study was collected from the Taksim hookah cafe 
in the Osmaniye region, Türkiye. Sodium thiosulfate (Na₂S₂O₃, 99% purity), potassium chloride (KCl, 
99% purity), and hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37% purity) were purchased from Interlab (Türkiye). N-
methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) (99% purity) and poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) were obtained from 
Sigma-Aldrich. Nickel foam was purchased from Teknotip (distributor for MTI Corporation). 
Deionized water (DI) was used for all hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) treatments. All chemical 
reagents were of analytical grade and utilized as received without additional purification.  
 
2.2 Preparation of porous carbon from hookah tobacco waste (HTWB) 
Porous carbon was synthesized through a multistep procedure. Initially, hookah tobacco waste  
(HTW) was thoroughly rinsed with distilled water to remove surface contaminants, followed by drying 
at 105 °C and grinding to a particle size below 200 mesh. Subsequently, 10 g of the ground material 
and 80 mL of distilled water were loaded into a 150 mL PPL -lined stainless-steel autoclave. 
Hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) was conducted at 180 °C for 4 hours, followed by natural cooling 
of the reactor to ambient temperature. The resulting solid was filtered, washed, and dried at 110 °C for 
24 h to obtain the preliminary biochar. 
To eliminate inorganic impurities, the biochar was treated with 3.0 M HCl for 45 min under 
continuous stirring, filtered, and sequentially washed with distilled water three times (45 min each). 
The purified biochar (HTWB) was then dried again at 110 °C for 24 h. 
The method employed in this study utilizes comparatively less hazardous activating agents, 
namely sodium thiosulfate and KCl. In contrast, traditional activation processes typically rely on more 
corrosive and aggressive chemicals such as KOH or ZnCl₂ to generate porous carbon [38–40]. 
For the activation step, HTWB was blended with Na₂S₂O₃ and KCl at mass ratios of 1/0.5/3 and 
1/1/3, maintaining a total mass of 2.0 g. The mixtures were placed in a quartz boat and activated in a 
tubular furnace under a continuous N₂ flow. Thermal activation was carried out at 700, 800, and 900 
°C for 60 min with a heating rate of 10 °C min⁻¹ to produce porous carbon materials. Based on the 
Na₂S₂O₃ / biochar ratio and activation temperature, the resulting samples were designated as HTWB -
0.5-700, HTWB -0.5-800, HTWB -0.5-900, HTWB -1-700, HTWB -1-800, and HTWB -1-900. The 
synthesis of hookah tobacco waste-derived porous carbons is illustrated schematically in Fig. 1.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1 Characterization 
The physical and morphological features of the produced porous carbon materials were assessed 
using several characterization techniques. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS SUPRA 55) 
was employed to observe surface morphology, while X -ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku SmartLab) was 
utilized to evaluate phase composition and purity. The specific surface area of the hookah  tobacco 
waste-derived porous carbon was measured using a surface area and porosity analyzer (BET 
Micromeritics TriStar II) in accordance with the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) technique at 75.81 K 
and p/p₀ = 0.2782. Additionally, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (PerkinElmer/MID, Spectrum 
Two/UATR) was conducted to determine structural characteristics, including functional groups and 
chemical bonding within the porous carbon samples. 
 
3.1.1. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis of HTWB 
A thorough understanding of the microstructure is crucial for designing and optimizing porous 
carbon-based devices. In addition, the characteristics of the electric double layer are highly influenced 
by the pore architecture and the specific surface area of the carbon material [41–43]. For 
supercapacitor applications, both the distribution of pore sizes and the proportion of micropores to 
mesopores play a decisive role. According to the IUPAC classification, pores in activated carbon are 
categorized as micropores when their diameters are below 2 nm, mesopores when they range from 2 to 
50 nm, and macropores when exceeding 50 nm [44]. 
Fig. 2 (a–b) shows the N₂ adsorption –desorption isotherms obtained at 77 K, which were 
employed to determine the BET -specific surface area, pore -size distribution, and total pore volume of 
the HTWB-0.5-800, HTWB-0.5-900, and HTWB-1-800 samples. All carbon materials exhibited Type 
IV isotherms, as defined by the IUPAC classification. The steep uptake observed at low relative 
pressures (0 –0.1) reflects the presence of microporous structures, while the appearance of a minor 
hysteresis loop at higher relative pressures (0.4–0.8) confirms the coexistence of mesopores. 
As illustrated in Fig. 2b, the pore -size distribution derived from the Barrett –Joyner–Halenda 
(BJH) method based on the adsorption branch further verifies the existence of mesopores within the 
porous carbons produced from hookah tobacco waste. In addition, a portion of the macroporous 
structure appears at higher relative pressures (>0.8). The textural characteristics of the raw hookah 
tobacco waste (HTW) and the six carbon samples synthesized from it are summarized in Table 1. As 
shown, the HTW precursor exhibits limited surface area, pore volume, and overall porosity, whereas 
the activated carbon samples display considerably larger  N₂ sorption pore volumes, with HTWB -0.5-

3.1.2. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of HTWB 
 The XRD patterns of Na 2S2O3-activated HTW-derived biochars (HTWB -0.5-800, HTWB-0.5-
900, and HTWB-1-800) are presented in Fig. 3. All samples exhibit a broad diffraction hump centered 
at approximately 2θ = 23–26°, which is assigned to the (002) plane of turbostratic carbon, along with a 
weak and broad feature around 2θ = 42 –45°, corresponding to the (100)/(101) planes. The absence of 
sharp diffraction peaks confirms that the carbon materials are predominantly amorphous with a low 
degree of graphitic ordering, which is typical for chemically activated biomass -derived carbons [45–
47]. 
A clear influence of activation temperature is observed when comparing HTWB -0.5-800 and 
HTWB-0.5-900. The sample activated at 900 °C exhibits a slightly more pronounced and better -
defined (002) reflection, indicating partial rearrangement of aromatic carbon layers and enhanced 
structural ordering at elevated temperature. This behavior is attributed to increased devolatilization and 
condensation of polyaromatic domains during high-temperature activation. 
The effect of Na₂S₂O₃ impregnation ratio is evident from the comparison between  HTWB-0.5-
800 and HTWB -1-800. The higher Na 2S2O3/biochar ratio (HTWB -1-800) leads to a broader and less 
intense (002) peak, suggesting increased structural disorder and lattice distortion. This observation 
indicates that intensive chemical activation promotes pore development and disruption of carbon layer 
stacking, thereby suppressing graphitization. 
Importantly, no crystalline diffraction peaks attributable to Na 2S2O3 or its possible 
decomposition products (e.g., Na₂SO₄ or Na₂S) are detected in any sample , implying their effective 
removal during post -activation washing or their presence in an amorphous or highly dispersed state. 
Overall, the XRD results demonstrate that Na 2S2O3-assisted activation primarily produces highly 
disordered turbostratic carbon structures, while higher activation temperature enhances limited 
aromatic ordering without inducing true graphitization.

3.1.3. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of HTWB 
 
Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) images reveal distinct morphological characteristics for 
the porous carbon  samples derived from hookah tobacco waste (HTW) under varying activation 
conditions. The samples, designated HTWB -0.5-800, HTWB -0.5-900, and HTWB -1-800, were 
examined at two magnifications: 10.00 KX and 100.00 KX, to provide both macroscopic and 
microscopic insights into their porous structures (Fig. 4). 
The HTWB -0.5-800 sample  at 10.00 KX magnification exhibits a highly porous and 
interconnected network structure. Fig. 4  shows a relatively rough surface with numerous irregular 
pores, suggesting a well -developed macroporous system  [48–50]. At 100.00 KX  magnification, the 
internal structure of the HTWB -0.5-800 sample reveals a more detailed view of the pore walls and 
smaller pores within the larger framework. The surface appears to be rugged, with evidence of smaller, 
possibly mesoporous, structures contributing to the overall porosity. The pore walls show some degree 
of smoothness but retain an irregular texture [48–50]. 
The HTWB-0.5-900 sample also displays a highly porous structure  at 10.00 KX . Compared to 
HTWB-0.5-800, there might be slight variations in the pore size distribution or connectivity, indicating 
the influence of increased activation temperature on the char's morphology  (Fig. 4) . The overall 
structure appears to be a complex network of interconnected pores . At 100.00 KX, the HTWB-0.5-900 
sample's surface appears smoother with more defined pore openings compared to the 800 °C activated 
sample [48–50]. This suggests that the higher activation temperature may have led to further etching 
and refinement of the pore structure, potentially increasing the uniformity of the pore walls and 
creating a more accessible porous network.

11 
 
The HTWB-1-800 sample  presents a notably different morphology. At 10.00 KX, this sample 
appears to consist of agglomerated particles rather than the distinct interconnected network observed in 
the 0.5 ratio samples. This suggests that a higher activating agent ratio at the same temperature leads to 
the formation of a granular or particulate structure with a greater density of smaller pores or 
particles [48–50]. The 100.00 KX image of HTWB -1-800 confirms the particulate nature, showing 
numerous fine, irregularly shaped particles that are aggregated. These particles appear to have their 
own internal porosity or surface roughness, contributing to the overall surface area. The increase in the 
activating agent ratio seems to have promoted the formation of a more fragmented and granular 
material, distinct from the more continuous porous frameworks seen in the other samples. 
In summary, the SEM analysis clearly demonstrates that both the Na₂S₂O₃/biochar ratio and the 
activation temperature significantly influence the morphology of the porous carbon. Samples with a 
lower Na₂S₂O₃ ratio (0.5) tend to form interconnected porous networks, with higher activation 
temperatures (900 °C) leading to smoother pore surfaces. Conversely, a higher Na₂S₂O₃ ratio (1) at 800 
°C results in a granular, agglomerated particle morphology, indicating a different mechanism of pore 
formation and structural development.

3.1.4. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis of HTWB 
 
The FTIR spectra of the hookah tobacco waste  (HTW) and the HTW-derived porous carbons  
(HTWB-0.5-800, HTWB-0.5-900, and HTWB-1-800) are shown in Fig. 5. HTW exhibits well-defined 
absorption bands associated with lignocellulosic oxygenated functionalities, including a broad O –H 
stretching band at 3600 –3200 cm -1, a carbonyl peak around 1700 –1600 cm -1, and C –O stretching 
features in the 1250 –1000 cm -1 region [51–53]. Additional bands below 900 cm -1 correspond to 
aromatic C–H out-of-plane bending [52]. 
Upon activation with Na₂S₂O₃, all spectra undergo strong attenuation and broadening, indicating 
extensive dehydration, decarboxylation, and aromatization of the carbon matrix. Both HTWB -0.5-800 
and HTWB -1-800 retain weak shoulders in the 1600 cm -1 and 1100 –1000 cm -1 regions, reflecting 


===== PDF PAGE 14 =====
13 
 
residual C=O and C –O groups [50,54]. The slightly more pronounced curvature in the 1200 –900 cm-1 
range for HTWB -1-800 suggests partial incorporation of sulfur species (C –S or S –O vibrations), 
consistent with the higher Na₂S₂O₃ dosage [49,50,55]. 
The spectrum of HTWB -0.5-900 exhibits the flattest profile and the lowest functional group 
intensity, confirming the highest degree of carbonization and removal of heteroatoms at 900 °C  
[49,50,55]. Overall, FTIR analysis demonstrates that increasing activation temperature is the dominant 
factor driving deoxygenation and structural aromatization, whereas increasing Na₂S₂O₃ loading mainly 
influences the mid -frequency region associated with S -containing surface functionalities. These 
chemical changes correlate with the enhanced porosity and electronic properties observed for the 
thermochemically activated samples.

3.1.5 Electrochemical characterization 
The electrochemical behavior of the synthesized porous carbon electrodes was assessed at room 
temperature using a CHI 660E three -electrode configuration (CH Instruments). Measurements were 
carried out in an aqueous potassium hydroxide (KOH) electrolyte with a concentration of 1.0 M. For 
electrode fabrication, a measured quantity of polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder was dissolved in 
N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP). The working electrode was prepared by depositing a slurry composed 
of hookah tobacco waste –derived porous carbon (85 wt%) and PVDF (15 wt%) onto nickel foam. To 

===== PDF PAGE 15 =====
14 
 
minimize variability, the carbon slurry was uniformly applied to each electrode at a loading of 8 mg 
cm⁻², followed by drying in an oven at 110 °C overnight. Finally, the electrodes were compressed at 10 
MPa to obtain mechanically stable functional sheets . The Ag/AgCl electrode (Argenthal, 3 M KCl; 
0.207 V vs. SHE at 25 °C) served as the reference electrode, whereas platinum gauze with an exposed 
surface area of 4 cm² was utilized as the counter electrode. The mass and geometric area of the nickel -
foam-based working electrode were carefully controlled, as both parameters are critical for 
determining the electrode’s specific capacitance. The porous carbon slurry was applied onto an active 
surface area of about 1 cm². For galvanostatic charge –discharge (GCD) experiments, the applied 
current densities ranged from 0.5 to 15 A/g, and a potential window of −0.8 to 0.2 V was used for both 
GCD and cyclic voltammetry (CV) analyses. CV scans were recorded at sweep rates between 10 a nd 
500 mV/s. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was conducted over a frequency interval of 10² to 
0.01 Hz using a 0 V DC bias and a 5 mV rms perturbation. Long-term cycling stability was evaluated 
by repeated GCD measurements performed at a constant current density  of 0.5 mA /cm² within the 
same potential range. The specific capacitance values for the three -electrode configuration were

The electrochemical performance of the porous carbon electrodes derived from hookah tobacco 
waste (HTWB) was examined using a three-electrode configuration in 1.0 M KOH solution. The cyclic 
voltammograms of the six carbon samples recorded at 10 mV /s are presented in Fig. 6(a). Among 
these electrodes, HTWB-1-800 exhibited the largest voltammetric area, indicating the highest specific 
capacitance at this scan  rate. Nevertheless, HTWB-0.5-900 was identified as the optimal electrode, as 
its capacitance was comparable while also demonstrating superior BET surface area (1018.74 m²/g), in 
agreement with the preceding characterization results. All porous carbon samples displayed nearly 
rectangular CV profiles, confirming their favorable capacitive  characteristics for supercapacitor 
applications. Examination of the CV profiles in Fig. 6(a) shows that, except for HTWB -0.5-700 and 
HTWB-1-700, the electrodes retain a nearly rectangular voltammogram without significant distortion. 
This observation indicates that an activation temperature of 700 °C is inadequate for developing

optimal capacitive behavior. The electrodes HTWB -0.5-800, HTWB -0.5-900, and HTWB -1-800 
contain a high concentration of oxygenated surface groups, which may participate in redox processes 
within the KOH electrolyte, thereby increasing the capacitor’s leakage current. This  effect is reflected 
in the slight, asymmetric current peak appearing on the right side of their CV curves.  A nearly 
rectangular and symmetric CV profile is characteristic of ideal electric double -layer capacitance, 
reflecting the electrode’s high -rate capability and strong electrochemical reversibility. Such behavior 
indicates efficient charge transport and rapid ion diffusion for most electrodes at a scan rate of 10  
mV/s, which in turn supports the delivery of both high power and high energy densities. The rate -
dependent CV curves of HTWB -0.5-900, recorded from 10 to 500 mV /s, are presented in Fig. 6(b). 
The CVs maintain a predominantly rectangular shape at low scan rates (10 and 20 mV /s), 
demonstrating typical supercapacitor performance. As the scan rate increases, however, the curves 
progressively deviate from this rectangular form due to enhanced electrochemical polarization. 
Fig. 6(c) presents the comparative galvanostatic charge –discharge (GCD) curves of the six 
HTWB electrodes at a current density of 0.5 A /g. All samples exhibit nearly triangular GCD profiles, 
confirming efficient charge storage arising from the proper formation of electric double layers at the 
electrode–electrolyte interface. Using Eq. (1), the gravimetric specific capacitances of HTWB-0.5-700, 
HTWB-0.5-800, HTWB-0.5-900, HTWB-1-700, HTWB-1-800, and HTWB-1-900 were determined to 
be 135, 140, 175, 125, 150, and 115 F /g, respectively, at 0.5 A /g. The specific capacitance values 
follow the order HTWB-0.5-900 > HTWB-1-800 > HTWB-0.5-800 > HTWB-0.5-700 > HTWB-1-700 
> HTWB-1-900 in 1 M KOH, as illustrated in Fig. 6(c). 
The superior performance of HTWB-0.5-900—prepared with a Na2S2O3-to-HTWB mass ratio of 
0.5/1 and activated at 900 °C —can be attributed to its well -developed microporous network, which 
enhances ion adsorption and transport. Although the three samples with 0.5 Na 2S2O3 content were 
prepared using the same activating agent ratio, HTWB -0.5-900 exhibited markedly better 
electrochemical behavior than HTWB -0.5-700 and HTWB -0.5-800, owing to the formation of more 
advanced microporous features and improved surface characteristics at the higher activation 
temperature. 
Fig. 6(d) displays the GCD curves of the HTWB -0.5-900 electrode at current densities ranging 
from 1 to 15 A /g. Only a minimal IR drop is observed at the onset of the discharge curves, and the 
curves maintain a nearly symmetric triangular shape across all tested currents. The negligible IR drop 
and linear symmetry further verify the excellent reversibility and stable charge –discharge behavior of 
this material.

The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results for all six electrodes are presented in 
Fig. 6(e). Measurements were conducted over the frequency range of 0.01 Hz to 100 kHz with an 
applied amplitude of 5 mV under open-circuit conditions to evaluate the supercapacitor behavior of the 
materials. The intercept of the Nyquist plot on the real (X) axis represents the equivalent series 
resistance (Re), which includes the intrinsic resistance of the electrode material, the contact resistance 
between the electrode and current collector, and the resistance of the current collector itself. The 
diameter of the semicircle observed in the high -frequency region represents the charge -transfer 
resistance (Rct), which is influenced by the structure of the electrical double layer. 
Among all electrodes, HTWB -0.5-900 exhibits the smallest semicircle radius, indicating the 
lowest Rct. Furthermore, this sample also shows the lowest Rs value, as evidenced by the leftmost X -
axis intercept. These features collectively confirm the superior electrical conductivity and favorable 
charge-transfer properties of the HTWB-0.5-900 electrode. 
Analysis of the Nyquist plots obtained from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy confirms 
the trends observed in the CV and GCD measurements. The HTWB -0.5-900 electrode exhibits a 
Nyquist profile closely resembling that of a typical ultracapacitor, as illustrated in Fig. 6(e). When 
compared with the other samples, HTWB -0.5-900 demonstrates more ideal capacitive behavior and 
superior overall electrochemical performance. 
The cycling stability of the HTWB -0.5-900 electrode was further assessed through 
chronopotentiometry for 10,000 charge –discharge cycles at a constant current density of 10 A /g (Fig. 
6(f)). Remarkably, no discernible loss in capacitance was detected throughout the test, indicating 
excellent long-term electrochemical durability.

4. Conclusion 
 
In summary, porous carbon materials were successfully synthesized from hookah tobacco waste 
using sodium thiosulfate as the activating agent. The HTWB-0.5-900 sample exhibited a highly 
porous, complex network structure consisting of interconnected pores.  Among the prepared samples, 
HTWB-0.5-900 demonstrated the largest surface area (1018.74 m² /g), which accounts for its superior 
electrochemical behavior as a supercapacitor electrode. These findings indicate that a Na 2S2O3/HTWB 
mass ratio of 0.5/1 , combined with an activation temperature of 900 °C , yields the most promising 
material for supercapacitor applications. At 0.5 A /g, the HTWB -0.5-900 electrode delivered the 
highest specific capacitance (175 F /g) and maintained its initial capacitance over 10,000 charge –
discharge cycles even at 10 A /g, demonstrating excellent long -term cycling stability. Overall, this 
study highlights that biochars derived from renewable resources such as hookah tobacco waste can be 
effectively transformed into high-value electrode materials for energy-storage technologies.
