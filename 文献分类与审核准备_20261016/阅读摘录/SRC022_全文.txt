Keywords: Heated tobacco waste; Carbonized tobacco leaf; Metallic heating element; MnO 2 
electrodeposition; Asymmetric supercapacitor; Recycling. 
 
In this study, an integrated recycling strategy is developed to  utilize the organic and metallic 
components collected from used heated tobacco as active materials and current collectors for 
an asymmetric supercapacitor (ASC) . Residual t obacco leaves are carbonized to obtain 
carbonized tobacco leaves (CTL), while metallic heating elements (MHEs) are directly reused 
without melting, reshaping, or additional processing. The MHEs maintain their morphology , 
surface characteristics,  and electrical continuity after tobacco use . A CTL-based slurry  is 
coated onto the MHE to fabricate the CTL/MHE negative electrode, and MnO2 is subsequently 
electrodeposited for 10–30 min to prepare MnO2-CTL/MHE positive electrodes. The electrode 
prepared using 15 min deposition time (M15 -CTL/MHE) exhibits favorable Mn 4+/Mn3+ 
surface states and high electrochemical performance for use as positive electrode. The 
asymmetric supercapacitor device assembled by M15 -CTL/MHE and CTL/MHE operates 
stably over 0–2.0 V and exhibits a gravimetric specific capacitance of 39.2 F g−1 at 1 A g −1. 
The device further achieves a maximum energy density of 23.6 Wh kg−1 and a power density 
of 10.0 kW kg−1, retains 81.8% of its initial capacitance after 3000 cycles, and successfully 
illuminates a 1.8 V red light-emitting diode. These results demonstrate the direct integration of 
multiple components from a single waste product into functional energy storage device. 
 
1. Introduction 
The rapid expansion of renewable energy systems, electric vehicles, and large -scale artificial 
intelligence data centers has increased the demand for efficient energy storage systems [1–3]. 
Among the available technologies, supercapacitors have attracted considerable attention owing 
to their rapid charge–discharge capability, high power density, and long cycle life [4–6]. Their 
electrochemical response can be tuned through electric double -layer charge storage  at 
conductive carbon surfaces, Faradaic reactions at redox-active materials, or a controlled 
combination of both mechanisms [7 –9]. Nevertheless, the increasing adoption of 
supercapacitors inevitably raises the demand for carbon  precursors, transition  metal 
compounds, electrolytes, and metallic current collectors, thereby  creating additional material 
and environmental burdens  [10–12]. In this regard, converting discarded materials into

functional energy storage components has emerged as an effective strategy for reducing waste 
generation and dependence on newly manufactured raw materials [1 3–15]. Among various 
waste streams, biomass waste is particularly attractive  owing to its carbon -rich organic 
composition and potential conversion into functional carbon materials  [16–18]. Its use in 
supercapacitors can reduce reliance on landfilling and incineration while transforming 
discarded resources into sustainable energy storage materials [19]. 
Carbonization can convert biomass waste into electrically conductive carbon materials suitable 
for electric double-layer charge storage [20]. Depending on the intended application, activation 
or surface modification can further regulate the pore structure, surface wettability, and ion 
accessibility [21]. However, the fabrication of a practical supercapacitor requires more than the 
preparation of a carbon active material. The active material must be combined with a 
mechanically stable  and electrically conductive  current collector , while a complete device 
requires positive and negative electrodes with complementary electrochemical characteristics 
and an ion-conducting electrolyte [22, 23]. Numerous biomass-recycling studies have focused 
mainly on converting a single organic  fraction into an electrode material while continuing to 
rely on commercially available current collectors  [24–26]. Such approach es demonstrate the 
applicability of biomass -derived carbon but do not fully utilize the functional components 
already present in heterogeneous waste products. Therefore, a  broader recycling strategy 
should separate the physically and chemically distinct waste fractions of the original product 
and assign each fraction a suitable role within the energy storage device. 
Despite its suitability as a carbon electrode, biomass -derived carbon stores charge through a 
predominantly non-Faradaic mechanism, which can limit capacitance and energy density [27]. 
Pseudocapacitive transition metal oxides can compensate for this limitation by providing 
additional charge storage through reversible redox reactions [8, 11]. Among them, manganese 
dioxide (MnO2) is an attractive material for a positive electrode owing to its relatively low cost, 
natural abundance, environmental compatibility, and high theoretical capacitance [2 8]. 
Nonetheless, its low electronic conductivity and tendency toward particle agglomeration can 
restrict electron transport  and active -site utilization  [29]. Direct deposition of MnO 2 onto 
biomass-derived carbon can improve  contact between the two materials, shorten electron -
transport pathways, and control the oxide loading [30, 31]. Furthermore, pairing a carbon-based 
negative electrode with an MnO 2-containing positive electrode in an asymmetric 
supercapacitor enables electric double -layer and Faradaic charge -storage mechanisms to 
operate over complementary potential window s [32]. This configuration can widen the 
operating cell voltage and improve energy storage performance while retaining the rapid power

delivery associated with carbon electrodes  [33]. However, translating this complementary 
design into a practical device still requires a conductive and mechanically stable current 
collector that can be obtained from waste without extensive metal processing. 
Heated tobacco waste is a multi-component waste containing carbon-rich tobacco residues and 
an internal metallic heating element  (MHE). Unlike conventional tobacco, in which most  
components are consumed through direct combustion, heated tobacco operates by heating 
rather than combustion and therefore retain much of their solid structure after use [34–36]. The 
MHE is originally designed as a susceptor that is heated by an electromagnetic field generated 
by the device and transfers the resulting heat to the surrounding tobacco material [37, 38]. Its 
electrical conductivity and thin, flat, and dimensionally stable geometry are also important 
characteristics of electrochemical current collectors , which support active materials and 
provide continuous electron transport to the external circuit [ 39–41]. Provided that its surface 
integrity and electrical continuity remain suitable after use, the MHE can be directly reused 
without melting, reshaping, or foil fabrication.  Previous studies have converted tobacco -
derived fractions, cellulose -acetate or PLA -based filter components, and aluminum -coated 
paper obtained from heated tobacco waste [3 7, 38]. However, the direct reuse of an MHE as 
the current collector for both electrodes, together with tobacco -leaf-derived carbon from the 
same waste product, remains largely unexplored. 
In this study, a recycling strategy was developed to recover both tobacco residues and metallic 
heating elements (MHEs) from used heated tobacco and integrate them into a single 
asymmetric supercapacitor device. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assign 
these two waste-derived fractions complementary roles as a carbonaceous active material and 
a current collector, respectively, in an energy -storage device. Residual tobacco leaves were 
carbonized at 900 °C under an N2 atmosphere to obtain carbonized tobacco leaves (CTL), while 
the MHEs were separated and directly reused as current collectors. CTL was slurry-coated onto 
the MHE to fabricate the CTL/MHE negative electrode, which exhibited electric double-layer 
charge storage and delivered a specific capacitance of 143.8 F g −1 at 1 A g −1. MnO 2 was 
subsequently electrodeposited onto CTL/MHE  to prepare the positive electrodes (MnO 2-
CTL/MHE), and the optimal deposition time was determined based on the structural, surface-
chemical, and electrochemical characteristics of the resulting electrodes. The optimized MnO2-
CTL/MHE electrode delivered 175.6 F g −1 at 1 A g −1. A charge -balanced asymmetric 
supercapacitor assembled using  optimized MnO 2-CTL/MHE as the positive electrode and 
CTL/MHE as the negative electrode operated over 0–2.0 V and exhibited 39.2 F g−1 at 1 A g−1. 
The device achieved a maximum energy density of 23.6 Wh kg−1, retained 81.8% of its initial

capacitance after 3000 cycles, and  successfully illuminated a 1.8 V red light -emitting diode 
(LED). More importantly, this study demonstrates that two fractions obtained from a single 
heterogeneous waste product can be assigned complementary electrochemical functions  with 
the tobacco -derived fraction serving as the carbonaceous active framework, and the MHE 
retaining its conductive function as a current collector in a new device context. 
 
2. Experimental section 
2.1. Materials 
Used h eated tobacco ( TEREA Silver , Philip Morris Korea Inc., Seoul, South Korea) was 
collected after operation . Manganese (II) acetate tetrahydrate (Mn(Ac) 2∙4H2O), 
poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF, Mw = 534,000), poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA,  Mw = 89,000–
98,000), 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP), and Super P conductive carbon were purchased 
from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Sodium sulfate (Na 2SO4) was obtained from 
Samchun Chemical Co., Ltd. (Seoul, South Korea). Without any further purification, all 
chemicals were used as received. 
 
2.2. Fabrication of CTL/MHE as the negative electrode 
The used heated tobacco was manually separated into tobacco lea f (TL), hollow tube, filter, 
and metallic heating element (MHE). The collected TL was washed several times using acetone 
and deionized (DI) water to remove residual contaminants and subsequently dried in an oven 
at 80 °C for 12 h. The dried TL was placed in a tubular furnace and carbonized under an N2 
atmosphere at 900 °C for 2 h with a 5 °C min−1 heating rate to obtain carbonized tobacco leaf 
(CTL). To prepare the electrode slurry, CTL, PVDF binder, and Super P conducting additive 
were combined at a mass ratio of 9 : 0.5 : 0.5 . An appropriate amount of NMP was added as 
the dispersing solvent, and the mixture was  shuffled using a paste mixer  (PDM-300, 
DAEHWATECH, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea) at a rotation speed of 800 rpm and a revolution 
speed of 750 rpm for 2 h. The resulting homogeneous slurry was cast onto the MHE and dried 
in a vacuum oven  at 70 °C for 12 h.  The carbon -coated current collector was denoted as 
CTL/MHE and used as the negative electrode. 
 
2.3. Fabrication of MnO2-CTL/MHE as the positive electrode 
MnO2-CTL/MHE positive electrode was prepared by electrodeposition using a three-electrode 
configuration. Prior to the deposition, the CTL/MHE electrodes was washed several times with 
DI water. The system was then constructed using CTL/MHE as the working electrode, platinum 

===== PDF PAGE 6 =====
  
6 
 
wire as the counter electrode, Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode, and  a 0.1 M aqueous  
Mn(Ac)2 solution as the deposition electrolyte, respectively. A constant potential of 1.5 V (vs. 
Ag/AgCl) was applied for 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min to control the loading of the MnO2 layer. 
After electrodeposition, the prepared electrodes were dried in an oven at 70 °C overnight. The 
resulting samples were denoted as M#-CTL/MHE, where # represents the electrodeposition 
time in minutes. 
 
2.4. Characterization 
The electrical characteristics of the MHEs before and after use were evaluated using a 
semiconductor parameter analyzer ( B1500, Keysight Technologies, Santa Rosa, CA, USA)  
coupled with a  probe sta tion. Current–voltage measurements were conducted using both 
conventional two-probe and four-probe Kelvin configurations, and the corresponding electrical 
resistance values were obtained from the measured responses. The morphologies of the MHEs, 
CTL/MHE and MnO 2-CTL/MHE electrodes were  examined by field-emission scanning 
electron microscopy (FE-SEM, S-4300, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Elemental compositions were 
analyzed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, EX -250, HORIBA Ltd., Kyoto, 
Japan) attached to the FE -SEM system. The surface wettability of the MHEs and fabricated 
electrodes was evaluated by static water contact a ngle measurements (Navitar Zoom7000, 
AMETEK, Berwyn, PA, USA). A 15 µL droplet of DI water was deposited on electrode surface, 
and the droplet profile was recorded in a shadowgraph configuration. At 20 s after droplet 
deposition, the contact angle was determined by tangent fitting of the profile. For each sample, 
measurements were performed at least five times and the median value was reported to ensure 
reliability. The molecular structures were analyzed using Fourier -transform infrared 
spectroscopy (FT-IR, Nicolet iS10, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), while the 
carbon structure and metal oxide behavior were investigated by Raman spectroscopy (QE Pro, 
Ocean Optics Inc., Largo, FL, USA). The crystal line phases  were examined by X -ray 
diffraction (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker Co., Billerica, MA, USA). Surface chemical states were 
characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Nexsa G2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 
Waltham, MA, USA). 
 
2.5. Assembly of the asymmetric supercapacitor device 
Prior to assembly of the asymmetric supercapacitor (ASC) device , a PVA/Na2SO4 gel 
electrolyte was prepared. PVA powder (2.0 g) was added to DI water (18 mL) to obtain a 10 
wt% PVA solution and magnetically stirred at 80 °C for 3 h until a homogeneous and 

===== PDF PAGE 7 =====
  
7 
 
transparent solution was obtained. Subsequently, Na 2SO4 (2.56 g) was added to the PVA 
solution, followed by stirring for an additional 1 h. The resulting solution was  cooled to room 
temperature and allowed to stand for 12 h before use. For assembly of the device, the optimized 
MnO2-CTL/MHE electrode exhibiting the highest electrochemical performance was used as 
the positive electrode and CTL/MHE was used as the negative electrode. A negative-to-positive 
mass ratio of 1.24 between CTL/MHE and MnO2-CTL/MHE was applied to satisfy the charge 
balance condition derived from the specific capacitances measured in the three -electrode 
configuration. The PVA/Na2SO4 gel electrolyte was uniformly placed to the facing surfaces of 
the positive and negative electrodes, and the electrode surfaces were directly contacted to 
construct the ASC device . The ass embled device was maintained in a fume hood at room 
temperature for 24 h to evaporate excess water and stabilize the gel electrolyte. To reduce the 
contact resistance, the outer surface was held using polyimide tape. 
 
2.6. Electrochemical measurements 
The electrochemical properties of the individual electrodes and assembled ASC device were 
evaluated using a potentiostat (Zive SP1, WonATech, Seoul, South Korea). For three-electrode 
measurements, CTL/MHE or MnO2-CTL/MHE was used as the working electrode, a platinum 
wire as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode  in a 1.0 M 
Na2SO4 aqueous electrolyte. Cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD), 
and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted at room 
temperature. CTL/MHE was evaluated over a potential range of −1.0–0 V, whereas the MnO2-
CTL/MHE electrodes were evaluated over 0–1.0 V. CV curves were recorded at scan rates of 
10–200 mV s −1, and GCD curves were obtained at current densities of 0.5–10 A g −1. The 
gravimetric specific capacitances derived from the CV and GCD curves were calculated using

3. Results and Discussion 
3.1. Fabrication of the positive and negative electrodes 
Figure 1  illustrates the overall strategy for recycling TLs and MHEs  separated from used 
heated tobacco into positive and negative electrodes for an asymmetric supercapacitor. Figure 
1a compares the residual  solid components remaining after the use of conventional tobacco 
and heated tobacco. In a conventional tobacco, direct combustion consumes most of the 
tobacco filler and paper, leaving primarily the filter and a small quantity of ash. In contrast, the 
low-temperature heating process of a heated tobacco preserves a larger fraction of their 
components including the residual TL and MHE.  These two components were selectively 
separated and assigned different electrochemical functions.  As shown in Figure 1b, the TLs 
were carbonized to obtain CTL, which was subse quently coated onto the MHE to fabricate  
CTL/MHE negative electrode. MnO2 was then directly electrodeposited on CTL/MHE at 1.5 
V for 10–30 min to prepare MnO 2-CTL/MHE positive electrodes . During electrodeposition, 
Mn2+ species supplied by the Mn(Ac)2 precursor were electrochemically oxidized and 
deposited as MnO 2 on the conductive CTL surface  [48]. This electrode design  targets to  
combine the electric double-layer behavior of CTL with the pseudocapacitive contribution of 
MnO2, while directly integrating the organic and metallic components of the used heated 
tobacco into supercapacitor electrodes.

The structural, compositional, and surface characteristics of the MHEs were evaluated before 
and after use. The FE-SEM images show that both MHEs maintained continuous and relatively 
flat surfaces without noticeable cracking, delamination, or thermally induced deformation  
(Figure 2a and 2b). This similarity indicates that the localized heating process during tobacco 
use did not significantly damage the metallic structure.  EDS elemental mapping further 
revealed spatially distributed Fe and Cr signals for both samples, consistent with a stainless 
steel-based metallic structure (Figures S1 and S2, Table S1) [49]. The surface wettability was 
evaluated using water contact angle measurements, as shown in Figure 2c and 2d. The contact 
angles were 53.2° before use and 51.3 ° after use, demonstrating that their surface wettability  
of MHE remained nearly unchanged after use. 
The electrical integrity of the MHEs was further examined by voltage–current measurements 
(Figure S 3). Two-probe and four -probe Kelvin configurations were employed, and the 
electrical resistance was determined from the slope of the voltage–current curves in the linear 
high-current region (Figure 2e and 2f). The two-probe resistance values of the MHEs before 
and after use were 2.40 and 2.37 Ω, respectively. These values indicate not only the intrinsic 
resistance of the MHE but also the resistance generated at the interfaces between the probe tips 
and the metallic surface [ 50]. In contrast, the four -probe Kelvin configuration separates the 
voltage sensing path from the current supplying path and can minimize the contribution of 
probe contact resistance [51]. The resulting four-probe resistance values were 0.023 Ω before 
use and 0.022 Ω after use. The nearly identical values confirmed that the MHE preserved its 
continuous electron transport pathway despite its exposure to the tobacco heating process. 
Overall, t he combined FE -SEM, EDS, contact angle, and electrical resistance results 
demonstrate that the used MHE retained the structural, surface, and electrical characteristics 
required for direct use as a current collector without melting, reshaping, or reprocessing [52].

The macroscopic integrity of CTL/MHE and MnO2-CTL/MHE electrodes prepared at different 
deposition times was visually examined before detailed structural and electrochemical analysis, 
as shown in Figure S4. CTL/MHE, M10 -CTL/MHE, M15-CTL/MHE, and M20 -CTL/MHE 
exhibited relatively uniform and continuous coating layers without noticeable cracking or 
detachment. In contrast, M25 -CTL/MHE and M30 -CTL/MHE showed increasingly  light-
colored and nonuniform surfaces containing large particle-like domains. Distinct cracks and 
local delamination were particularly evident for M30-CTL/MHE. The progressive 
deterioration at long deposition times suggests that continued MnO 2 growth generated an 
excessively thick and mechanically heterogeneous layer.  Accordingly, CTL/MHE, M10 -
CTL/MHE, M15 -CTL/MHE, and M20 -CTL/MHE were selected for morphological, 
compositional, and electrochemical analysis. 
The surface morpholo gy of CTL/MHE and MnO 2-CTL/MHE was investigated by FE-SEM 
analysis (Figure 3a–d). The CTL/MHE exhibited a rough and porous carbonaceous surface  
composed of irregular flake-like particles originating from the carbonization of tobacco leaves. 
This heterogeneous texture provided numerous nucleation sites for subsequent MnO 2 growth.

After 10 min of electrodeposition, fine rounded particles were distributed over the CTL surface, 
indicating the initial nucleation and growth of Mn -containing species  [53]. Increasing the 
deposition time to 15 min enlarged the rounded domains and increased their surface coverage, 
while the particles remained sufficiently separated to preserve interparticle spaces.  After 20 
min, continued growth and merging produced larger and more densely packed agglomerates 
that covered most of the C TL surface. This morphological sequence represents a transition 
from nucleation to particle growth and finally to agglomeration as the electrodeposition time 
increased [54]. The corresponding EDS  elemental mapping  images and EDS spectra  are 
presented in Figures 3e–h and S5, while the detailed elemental compositions are summarized 
in Table S2. CTL/MHE mainly exhibited C and O signals derived from the carbonized biomass 
[55]. Following electrodeposition, Mn and O signals were distributed throughout the surfaces 
of M 10-CTL/MHE, M15 -CTL/MHE, and M20 -CTL/MHE [56]. Under identical mapping 
conditions, the apparent Mn  coverage increased with deposition time, in agreement with the 
FE-SEM observations. The C signal remained detectable for all electrodes, confirming that the 
CTL framework was retained beneath the deposited oxide layer. These results establish a direct 
correlation in which the deposition time controls MnO 2 nucleation density, particle size, 
surface coverage, and agglomeration.

The surface wettability of CTL/MHE and the MnO2-CTL/MHE electrodes was investigated, 
as shown in Figure 4a. The CTL/MHE exhibited a water contact angle of ca. 47.3°, indicating 
moderate wettability . This behavior can be attributed to the  combined effects of residual 
oxygen-containing functional groups and rough  carbonaceous surface of the CTL [ 57]. After 
MnO2 electrodeposition, the contact angles sharply decreased to 7.5°, 7.1°, and 7.2° for M10-
CTL/MHE, M15 -CTL/MHE, and M20 -CTL/MHE, respectively. The  similarly low  contact 
angles indicate that a highly hydrophilic MnO 2 surface was already established after  10 min 
and that additional deposition mainly changed the oxide morphology and loading  rather than 
the macroscopic wettability . Such improved wetting facilitates contact between the aqueous 
electrolyte and  the active materials, increasing the electrochemically active sites in charge 
storage [58]. 
Figure 4b  presents the XRD patterns of CTL/MHE and MnO 2-CTL/MHE electrodes. 
CTL/MHE exhibited a broad diffraction peak over 10–25°, which was assigned to the (002) 
plane of disordered carbon structure [59]. Also, sharp diffraction peaks were observed at 44.4°, 
64.7°, and 82.0° for CTL/MHE and all MnO2-CTL/MHE electrodes, corresponding to the (110), 
(200), and (211) planes of the Fe-rich BCC phase of the MHE substrate, respectively [60]. The 
presence of these peak indicates that the crystalline phase of the MHE was maintained during 
electrode fabrication. In contrast, no distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline 
MnO2 were observed, which was attributed to the low crystallinity and relatively small volume 
of the electrodeposited MnO2 layer compared with the highly crystalline MHE [61]. The XRD 
results confirmed the retention of the disordered CTL structure and crystalline phase of MHE 
substrate. 
The chemical structure  and vibrational characteristics were further examined by FT-IR and 
Raman spectroscopy (Figure 4c and 4d). In the FT-IR spectrum of CTL/MHE, an absorption 
band at ca. 1560 cm−1 was assigned to C=C stretching within the carbonized structure  [62]. 
Following MnO2 electrodeposition, the spectra exhibited an increased contribution near the 
lower instrumental limit around 650 cm−1, which was associated with Mn–O lattice vibrations 
[63]. The relatively weak and partially truncated appearance of this band is consistent with its 
proximity to the lower boundary of the measured wavenumber range. In the Raman spectrum, 
CTL/MHE showed the characteristic D and G bands at ca. 1350 and 1580 cm−1, corresponding 
to disordered carbon domains and sp2-hybridized graphitic structures, respectively [64]. The 
intensity ratio of the D and G bands ( ID/IG) increased from 1.10 for CTL/MHE to 1.2 0, 1.23, 
and 1.2 7 for M10-CTL/MHE, M15 -CTL/MHE, and M20 -CTL/MHE, respectively.  This 
gradual change suggests that the MnO2 electrodeposition altered the Raman contributions of

3.2. Electrochemical properties of the positive and negative electrodes 
The electrochemical properties of the CTL/MHE and MnO2-CTL/MHE electrodes were 
evaluated using a three -electrode system. To determine an optimized positive electrode, the 
CV curves of M10-CTL/MHE, M15-CTL/MHE, and M20-CTL/MHE were compared at a scan 
rate of 100 mV s−1 in the potential range of 0–1.0 V, as shown in Figure S6. Among the three 
electrodes, M15-CTL/MHE exhibited the largest enclosed CV area and the highest gravimetric 
specific capacitance of 128.6 F g −1. The lower capacitance of M10 -CTL/MHE might be 
attributed to insufficient MnO2 coverage and a smaller portion of redox-active sites. In contrast, 
extending the deposition time to 20 min increased the particle size and promoted agglomeration, 
which reduced the fraction of electrochemically accessible MnO 2 and increased the effective

electron transport distance. Based on these results, M15 -CTL/MHE was selected as the 
optimized positive electrode. 
Figure 6a compares the CV curves of CTL/MHE and M15 -CTL/MHE at a scan rate of 100 
mV s−1. The CTL/MHE exhibited capacitive behavior in the potential range of −1.0–0 V, while 
the M15-CTL/MHE operated in the potential range of 0–1.0 V. These complementary potential 
ranges support their use as negative and positive electrodes for the assembly of an asymmetric 
supercapacitor [75]. Figure 6b presents the CV curves of CTL/MHE electrode at  scan rates 
ranging from 10 to 200 mV s −1 in the potential range of −1.0–0 V. The current response 
continuously increased with increasing scan rate, while the overall quasi-rectangular capacitive 
profile was retained, indicating that charge storage in CTL/MHE mainly originated from 
electric double-layer capacitance [76]. The gravimetric specific capacitances calculated at 10, 
20, 50, 100, and 200 mV s −1 were 142.4, 131.8, 114.6, 104.7 and 93.1  F g−1, respectively. As 
the scan rate increase d from 10 to 200 mV s −1, the electrode retained 65.4% of its specific 
capacitance, demonstrating favorable rate capability as a negative electrode. The CV curves of 
M15-CTL/MHE electrode were obtained at the same scan rates in the potential range of 0–1.0 
V, as shown in Figure 6c. Compared with CTL/MHE, the positive electrode exhibited a larger

electrodeposited MnO2 [77]. The gravimetric specific capacitances at 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 
mV s −1 were computed as 176.0, 163.0, 142.7, 128.6, and 117.1 F g −1, respectively. M15 -
CTL/MHE retained 66.5% of its initial specific capacitance, indicating  that the MnO 2 layer 
increased the overall capacitance without causing a severe loss at high scan rate under the 
optimized deposition condition.

143.8, 129.1, 115.9, and 98.2 F g −1, respectively. CTL/MHE retained 63.1% of its initial

MnO2 [79]. The similar profiles maintained at different current densities indicate stable and 
reversible charge-storage behavior [80]. The gravimetric specific capacitances obtained at 0.5, 
1, 2, 5, and 10 A g −1 were 190.2, 175.6, 160.0, 141.6, and 126.4 F g −1, respectively. M15 -

3.3. Electrochemical performance of assembled asymmetric supercapacitor device 
Based on the three-electrode results, an ASC device was assembled using M15 -CTL/MHE as 
the positive electrode, CTL/MHE as the negative electrode, and PVA/Na2SO4 as the gel 
electrolyte (Figure 7a). The resulting device is denoted as M15-CTL/MHE//CTL/MHE. The 
mass ratio of the negative and positive active materials was adjusted to balance the charges 
stored in the two electrodes  [81]. The calculated mass ratios from the CV curves were 1.24, 
1.24, 1.25, 1.23, and 1.26, at scan rates of 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 mV s −1, respectively. The 
corresponding values calculated from the GCD curves were 1.22, 1.22, 1.24, 1.22, and 1.29 at 
current densities of 0.5, 1, 2, 5, and 10 A g −1, respectively. As a result, the ASC device was 
assembled by setting the optimal mass ratio of 1.24, which is mean value of the ten ratio values.

The CV curves of the M15 -CTL/MHE//CTL/MHE ASC device were examined in different 
voltage windows ranging from 1.0 to 2.0 V with an increment of 0.2 V at a scan rate of 100

enclosed CV area gradually increased without an abrupt increase in irreversible current , 
indicating the stable operation up to 2.0 V. This extended voltage window originated from the

18.3, 20.4, 22.9, and 26.8 F g −1, respectively. The ASC device w as further evaluated at scan

charge–discharge operation. Figure 7f shows the capacitance values of the device calculated 
from the CV and  GCD. Collectively, t he CV and GCD results demonstrate that the  
complementary CTL/MHE and M15 -CTL/MHE electrodes facilitated stable ASC operation 
over an extended operating voltage window of 2.0 V.

[84]. The EIS results indicate that the MHEs retained continuous electron transport pathways, 
further supporting their direct use as functional current collectors in the device.

4. Conclusion 
In summary, an integrated strategy was developed to recycle the organic and metallic 
components of used heated tobacco as functional parts of an asymmetric supercapacitor.  The 
MHEs maintained continuous and flat morphologies, comparable surface wettability, and 
nearly identical four-probe resistance values before and after use , verifying their direct reuse 
as current collectors without melting, reshaping, or additional metal processing. The residual 
tobacco leaves were carbonized at 900 °C to prepare CTL, which was slurry-coated onto the 
MHEs to fabricate the CTL/MHE negative electrode s. The CTL/MHE exhibited electric 
double-layer behavior and delivered a  gravimetric specific capacitance of 143.8 F g−1 at 1 A 
g−1. MnO2 was subsequently electrodeposited onto the CTL/MHE, and the deposition time was 
controlled to optimize the morphology and chemical states . Among the prepared electrodes, 


===== PDF PAGE 23 =====
  
23 
 
M15-CTL/MHE provided balanced MnO2 morphology and favorable Mn4+/Mn3+ surface states, 
resulting in the highest gravimetric specific capacitance of 175.6 F g −1 at 1 A g −1. An ASC 
assembled using M15 -CTL/MHE as the positive electrode and CTL/MHE as the negative 
electrode operated over a voltage window  of 0 –2.0 V and exhibited a gravimetric specific 
capacitance of 39.2 F g−1 at 1 A g −1. The device achieved a maximum energy density of 23.6 
Wh kg −1 and a maximum power density of 10.0 k W kg −1, retained 81.8% of its initial 
capacitance after 3000 cycles, and illuminated a 1.8 V red LED. Therefore, the proposed 
approach extends beyond single-component biomass conversion by assigning complementary 
electrochemical functions to the organic and metallic fractions of one waste product. This 
integration provides a practical design framework for reducing heated tobacco waste while 
developing sustainable energy storage components.
