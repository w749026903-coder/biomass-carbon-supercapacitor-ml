ABSTRACT:
 The growing demands for environmental and energy
sustainability drive the development of energy storage devices with high
energy and power densities and, consequently, potential electrode
materials. The conversion of biomass waste into sustainable electrode
materials has attracted much attention in the scientific community for
energy storage applications. In this work, porous carbon is derived from
the agricultural biowaste of the coconut tree (Cocos nucifera) husk. This
biowaste is activated through a chemical activation method using different
activating agents, such as K2
CO
3
,
 KOH, KMnO4
,
 KCl, KBr, and KI.
Among the samples, biomass-derived carbon activated using the KMnO4
activating
 agent (AC3) delivers a high specific surface area of 2333.4 m2
g
−
1with
 a pore volume of 2.625 cm3
g
−
1,
 which results in excellent
electrochemical performance, with a gravimetric capacitance of 560 F g−
1at
 2 A g−
1current
 density in 2 M KOH electrolyte solution. To
demonstrate the real-time application, symmetric aqueous (KOH) and gel [poly(vinyl alcohol)-PVA/KOH] electrolyte devices are
fabricated using the AC3@graphite sheet as positive and negative electrodes. The fabricated symmetric gel electrolyte device
achieves a high gravimetric capacitance of 276 F g−
1at
 the current density of 2 A g−
1.
 The above device manifests an energy density
of 38.3 Wh kg−
1at
 a power density of 1 kW kg−
1.
 Besides, the cycle stability retains 98% even after 5000 cycles at 10 A g−
1current
density. These superior electrochemical outcomes suggest that this AC3@graphite sheet electrode will have potential in electric
double-layer supercapacitors.■
INTRODUCTION
An
increment in the world population leads to an exponential
increase in energy demand, causing the overexploitation of
energy sources. The continuous depletion of fossil fuels and
greenhouse gas emissions urges us to exploit sustainable
renewable energy resources and stimulate the research
community to develop low-cost, environmentally friendly,
and portable energy storage devices.1
−4Among
 the different
energy storage systems, supercapacitors bridge the battery and
capacitors, having fascinating properties, such as a large specific
capacity, fast charging and discharging rate, high safety, low
cost, environmental friendliness, and reasonable specific energy
and power.5
,6Supercapacitors
 are classified into two types
based on the charge storage mechanism: electric double-layer
capacitors (EDLCs) and pseudocapacitors (PCs). In EDLCs,
charges are stored at the interface of the electrolyte and
electrode surface through electrostatic interaction between the
electrolyte ions and the surface of the electrode.7
,8Over
 recent
years, carbonaceous materials have been used as electrode
materials for EDLCs, among which biomass-derived activated
carbon (AC) has been explored more because of its unique
characteristics, such as good electrical conductivity, thermal
stability, corrosion resistive behavior, large surface area, high
carbon content, low cost, and environmental friendliness.9
,10Plenty
 of biomass-based carbon materials are available on
Earth, like rice husks, rice straws, coffee husks, walnut shells,
sunflower seed shells, coconut shells, roots, wood, leaves of
plants, peels of vegetables, etc.11
−15These
 agricultural wastes
are disposed through direct incineration in the open air,
increasing environmental pollution and carbon emissions. To
avoid this pollution, we can use this waste as biomass-derived
carbon in bulk manufacture at a low cost. Deriving carbon
from biomass is one of the waste-to-wealth techniques.
Because of their rich availability and sustainability, these
biomass-derived materials have become a research hotspot
widely used in different applications, like catalysts, electrodes,
and absorbents.16

The
 coconut tree (Cocos nucifera) is most abundant in
southern India, producing nutritious edible seeds, healthy and
refreshing drinks, oils, ropes, brooms, fibers, etc. The coconut
tree belongs to the family Arecaceae, and it contributes every
inch of its life to become more useful for humankind. Coconut
husk (CH) and coconut tree husk (CTH) are two different
sorts of biomass obtained from coconut trees (shown in
Figures S1 and S2 of the Supporting Information). Coconut
husk (coconut fiber) helps make products, such as ropes, mats,
carpets, brushes, etc. Coconut tree husk is one of the products
obtained from coconut trees that has been used for fire in
traditional kitchens; there is no other specified use that it has.
Therefore, it will be one of the attractive environmentally
friendly precursors for activated carbon electrode materials.
Producing activated carbon is a two-step process: (1)
Carbonization: The carbon-rich material is carbonized at low
temperatures. (2) Activation: Activation is performed at high
temperatures through physical or chemical activation proce-
dures. The surface is modified using air, steam, or oxygen at
high temperatures in physical activation. In chemical
activation, biomass-derived carbonized carbon is mixed with
an activating agent, such as salt, base, or acid, in an inert
atmosphere.28
In
 biomass-derived carbon, the porous nature
may occur inherently or form the porous structure easily.29
Because
 of the fast electrolyte ion diffusion, mesopores can
store more charges than micropores. By reduction of ion
scattering, micropores can improve the rate capability. High
ion adsorption will happen at the macropores; as a result, it
acts as an ion reservoir. The high performance of the
supercapacitor is due to the combined effect of all three
types of pores in the biomass-derived carbon materials.1
In
 some of the recent literature, coconut husk is used as a
biomass for energy storage applications. In this work, one of
the agricultural wastes, coconut tree husk biomass, is used as a
sustainable and environmentally friendly precursor for
preparing activated carbon for the first time. Through a
chemical activation process, various activating agents assisted
AC generated from biomass. The diffraction planes, graphitic
structure, morphology, porosity, surface elemental composi-
tion, and chemical bonding states of all AC samples are initially
analyzed. The charge storage performance of all AC samples is

EXPERIMENTAL
 SECTIONChemicals
 and Reagents. Coconut tree husks were collected
from coconut trees in Kanyakumari district in Tamil Nadu, India.
Potassium carbonate (K2
CO
3
),
 potassium hydroxide (KOH),
potassium permanganate (KMnO4
),
 potassium chloride (KCl),
potassium bromide (KBr), potassium iodide (KI), nitric acid
(KNO3
),
 and hydrochloric acid (HCl) were purchased from Merck
(P), Ltd. Poly[vinyl alcohol] (PVA) was purchased Alfa Aesar (P),
Ltd. All chemicals were used for synthesis without any further
purification.
Synthesis of Biomass-Derived Activated Carbon. The
coconut tree husks were first washed using HCl and double-distilled
(DD) water to remove the dust and inorganic impurities, then cut
into small pieces, and dried at 60 °C for 12 h to remove the water
contents. After drying, the pieces were kept inside a muffle furnace for
carbonization in an air atmosphere at 350 °C for 1 h. The carbonized
powder was purified several times using HCl, HNO3
,
 and DD water
until approaching the neutral pH. The above powder is known as
carbonized carbon (CC). The next step is called the chemical
activation process, in which activating agent K2
CO
3
and
 the
carbonized carbon material were mixed in a 2:1 weight ratio until a
homogeneous slurry was formed. The above slurry was heated inside
the tubular furnace with an Ar environment up to 800 °C at a heat
rate of 5 °C min−
1and
 maintained at 800 °C for 2 h. Then, the
sample was allowed to reach room temperature; the collected sample
was washed using HCl, HNO3
,
 and DD water. After cleaning, the
sample was dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h and denoted as
activated carbon sample (AC1). In the above synthesis procedure,
instead of K2
CO
3
activating
 agent, KOH, KMnO4
,
 KCl, KBr, and KI
activating agents were used, and the samples are denoted as AC2,
AC3, AC4, AC5, and AC6, respectively. Figure 1 represents the
synthesis procedure of activated carbon from the coconut tree husk
biomass. The possible activation mechanism that may occur between
the different activating agents and carbonized carbon under
gasification conditions (during the activation process) at various
temperatures is explained in section 1 of the Supporting Information.
Material Characterization. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern
was obtained using a Cu Kα1
radiation-equipped
 Rigaku Ultima III
powder X-ray diffractometer encountering at 40 kV for a 2θ range of
20−80°. From the XRD pattern, the diffraction planes of AC samples
were identified. Using Raman spectroscopy (LabRAM HR Evolution,
Horiba, Ltd., Japan) with a micro-Raman spectrometer (exciting
wavelength of 523 nm), the disorder and graphitization degree of each
sample were identified. Field emission scanning electron microscopy
(FE-SEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy
(HR-TEM) were used to analyze the morphology of the AC samples.
A Carl Zeiss (Hebron, KY, U.S.A.) Sigma FE-SEM apparatus with a
Gemini column (resolution of 1.5 nm) was employed to entrap the
surface morphology. Using a Bruker (Germany) Nano XFlash
detector, the energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (EDAX) and elemental
mapping of AC were carried out. A 300 kV transmission electron
microscope (Technai G2 Twin) was used to take the HR-TEM
picture of the AC3 sample. A Thermo Scientific K-Alpha X-ray
photoelectron spectroscope was used to examine the chemical bonds
on the surface of the electrode material. Quantachrome Nova 2200e
V11.05 equipment was used to quantify the specific surface area, pore
size distribution, and N2
adsorption/desorption
 isotherm of the
synthesized material using Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) and
Barrett−Joyner−Halenda (BJH) studies.
Electrochemical Measurements. Using the Biologic SP-200
electrochemical workstation, the electrochemical performances of AC
samples were investigated in three- and two-electrode configurations.
Active material (AC), binding agent [polyvinylidene fluoride
(PVDF)], and conductive material (carbon black) were mixed with
a few drops of 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) in an 8:1:1 ratio, and
the resultant mixture was sonicated around 15 min to make a uniform
slurry. The slurry of the active material was coated on the pre-cleaned
graphite sheet substrate using the drop-casting method. A 1 cm2
area
of graphite sheet was coated with the active material and used as the
working electrode after drying 60 °C for 12 h. The mass loaded on the
graphite sheet is 0.8 mg cm−
2.
 The reference and counter electrodes
were Ag/AgCl and Pt wire, respectively. For the galvanostatic
charge−discharge (GCD) study and cyclic voltammetry (CV) study,
2 M KOH was used as the electrolyte solution with a potential range
of 0.0−0.5 V. In the same three-electrode system, an electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy (EIS) study was performed in the frequency
range of 100−0.1 Hz.
Symmetric Device Fabrication. Two types of symmetric devices
were fabricated using the KOH electrolyte and PVA/KOH gel
electrolyte as the two electrolytes with the same electrode materials.

nitrogen adsorption−desorption isotherms. Before the meas-

determine the pore size distribution and specific surface area of
the sample. Figure 4a shows the N2
adsorption
−desorption
curve for the AC3 sample. The N2

activated carbon samples exhibit type IV isotherms [classi-
fications based on the International Union of Pure and Applied
Chemistry (IUPAC)] with a loop of hysteresis. The curve for
isotherms quickly rises at lower relative pressures (P/P0
<
 0.1)
depicting the presence of micropores. The hysteresis loop in

<

of a large number of mesopores; a slight upward inclination in

>

meso-, and macropores; these hierarchical pore structures
would support high-performance energy storage. Even though
all of the samples have hierarchical pore distributions, AC3
shows better electrochemical performance than the other AC
samples. The reason is that KMnO4

The charge storage performance of the six samples was studied

CONCLUSION
Porous
 carbon has been synthesized from the agricultural
biowaste of the coconut tree (C. nucifera) husk through the
chemical activation process using different activating agents,
such as K2
CO
3
,
 KOH, KMnO4
,
 KCl, KBr, and KI. The
diffraction planes, graphitic structure, morphology, and
elemental composition are studied using different approaches,
such as XRD, Raman, SEM, TEM, and EDAX, respectively.
The surface area and porosity of all AC samples are analyzed
using BET; the AC3 sample exhibits a high specific surface area
of 2333.4 m2
g
−
1with
 a pore volume of 2.625 cm3
g
−
1,
 which
results in excellent electrochemical performance. The chemical
bonding on the surface of the AC3 sample is examined using
XPS analysis. When electrochemical performance is concerned,
three-electrode studies (CV, GCD, and EIS) reveal that,
among all electrode materials, AC3 has an excellent gravimetric
capacitance of 560 F g−
1at
 2 A g−
1current
 density in 2 M
KOH electrolyte solution. The surface and diffusion
contributions during charge storage of the AC3 electrode are
studied using deconvolution of the corresponding CV curves,
suggesting the EDLC dominance of this electrode. The
aqueous (KOH) and gel (PVA/KOH) electrolyte symmetric
supercapacitor devices are fabricated using AC3 electrodes;
they have energy densities of 20.8 and 38.3 Wh kg−
1,
respectively, at the current density of 2 A g−
1.
 The gel
electrolyte symmetric device is highly efficient for practical
applications; its gravimetric capacitance retains 98% with
Coulombic efficiency of 97% even after 5000 cycles at 10 A g−
1current
 density. In addition, a small practical device is
fabricated and used to run a stopwatch. The present findings
indicate that coconut-tree-husk-biomass-derived carbon elec-
trodes with gel electrolytes could provide a potential method
for developing high-performance supercapacitors.■
