ABSTRACT
Biomass‐derived carbons are eco‐friendly and sustainable materials, making them ideal for supercapacitors due to their high
surface area, excellent conductivity, cost‐effectiveness, and environmental benefits. This review provides valuable insights into
biomass‐derived carbon and modified carbon for supercapacitors, integrating both experimental results and theoretical cal-
culations. This review begins by discussing the origins of biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors, including plant‐based,
food waste‐derived, animal‐origin, and microorganism‐generated sources. Then, this review presents strategies to improve the
performance of biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors, including heteroatom doping, surface functionalization, and hybrid
composite construction. Furthermore, this review analyzes the functions of biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors both in
its pure form and as modified materials. The review also explores composites derived from biomass‐based carbon, including
carbon/MXenes, carbon/MOFs, carbon/graphene, carbon/conductive polymers, carbon/transition metal oxides, and carbon/
hydroxides, providing a thorough investigation. Most importantly, this review offers an innovative summary and analysis of the
role of biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors through theoretical calculations, concentrating on four key aspects: energy
band structure, density of states, electron cloud density, and adsorption energy. Finally, the review concludes the future
research directions for biomass carbon‐based supercapacitors, including the discovery of novel biomass materials, tailoring
surface functional groups, fabricating high‐performance composite materials, exploring ion transfer mechanisms, and
enhancing practical applications. In summary, this review offers a thorough exploration of the sources, functions, and mech-
anisms of biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors, providing valuable insights for future research.

Introduction
Currently, there is a growing urgency among researchers to
make environment‐friendly and renewable energy storage so-
lutions for harnessing sources, such as wind, solar, and tidal
energy, prompted by rising fossil fuel consumption and envi-
ronmental degradation [ 1–3 ]. Among the key electrochemical
energy storage technologies are batteries and supercapacitors
[ 4–6 ]. Although batteries could offer high energy density,
capable of storing significant energy and providing stable output

deliver rapid responses. Additionally, batteries have a limited
cycle life, necessitating regular replacements, and their chemi-
cal processes may result in hazardous waste [8, 9]. Super-
capacitors could effectively address the limitations of battery
energy storage systems [10]. They offer several advantages: (i)
Rapid charging and discharging capabilities allow super-
capacitors to release energy within seconds, making them
suitable for applications such as the braking systems of electric
vehicles. (ii) Extended lifespan, as they can undergo thousands
of charge and discharge cycles without a significant decline in
performance [11]. (iii) High power density enables them to
deliver immediate energy output, meeting diverse high‐power
demands [12]. Their quick charge and discharge features
render supercapacitors applicable across various areas,
including transportation, renewable energy, industrial machin-
ery, consumer electronics, emergency power solutions, military
uses, aerospace, and broader energy storage systems [13].
Supercapacitor electrodes demand materials with excellent
electrical conductivity and a large specific surface area to
enhance capacitance and energy storage efficiency [14, 15].
Candidates with high electrochemical performance include
various carbon nanomaterials (such as activated carbon, carbon
nanotubes, and graphene), metal oxides (such as Fe

[17, 18], and their composites [19]. Notably, carbon nano-
materials stand out due to their numerous advantages,
including superior electrical conductivity and charge transfer
capabilities, extensive surface areas that promote high capaci-
tance, sustainable sourcing, versatile pore structures for charge
retention, and cost‐effective production methods [20–22].
Recently, biomass has emerged as a crucial resource for pro-
ducing carbon materials used in supercapacitors [23, 24]. Unlike
other carbon sources, biomass‐derived carbon offers distinct
benefits, including abundant supply, renewability, natural pore
structures, enhanced safety, and affordability. These materials
demonstrate superior specific capacitance and notable rate
performance due to their abundant active sites and improved
conductivity [25–27]. Electrochemical energy is stored in
biomass‐derived carbon through reversible adsorption and
desorption of charged species at the carbon electrode–electrolyte
interface, facilitating rapid energy transfer within seconds or
even microseconds [28]. The porosity and structure of these
materials can be modified via various activation techniques,
influencing ion diffusion rates in supercapacitors. Biomass
sources encompass a wide range of waste and byproducts from
plants, animals, and other organic matter while maintaining
sustainability and cost‐effectiveness [29].
Using biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors provides
several advantages over traditional carbon materials, particu-
larly in terms of sustainability, structural features, electro-
chemical performance, and cost‐effectiveness. (1) Sustainability
and environmental benefits. Carbon materials produced
through conventional methods often rely on nonrenewable
fossil‐based precursors and the use of hazardous chemicals,
resulting in a significant environmental impact [30]. Compared
with traditional carbon materials, biomass carbons are derived
from renewable sources such as agricultural waste, forestry
residues, and food byproducts. This contrasts with traditional
carbons, which often rely on nonrenewable fossil‐based pre-
cursors such as coal, petroleum coke, or synthetic polymers [31].
(2) Cost‐effectiveness. The synthesis processes for traditional
carbon need expensive precursors and complex equipment,
making the production process costly [32]. Compared with
traditional carbon materials, biomass feedstocks (e.g., coconut
shells, sawdust, corn cobs, and rice husks) are inexpensive or
even free, as they are often agricultural or industrial byproducts
[33]. The production of biomass carbon generally involves
simpler processes, such as pyrolysis and activation, which are
less costly than the high‐temperature graphitization or chemical
vapor deposition (CVD) techniques often required for tradi-
tional carbons [34]. (3) Rich hierarchical porous structure.
Carbon materials produced via conventional methods may lack
the hierarchical porous structure and functional groups that are
often inherent in biomass‐derived carbons. These features in
biomass‐derived carbons enhance electrochemical performance
in applications such as supercapacitors [35]. Biomass‐derived
carbons often feature naturally occurring hierarchical pore
structures (micro‐, meso‐, and macropores). These structures
promote efficient ion transport and electrolyte accessibility,
enhancing supercapacitor performance [36]. During the
carbonization process, the organic composition of biomass (e.g.,
cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin) facilitates the formation of
interconnected pores without the need for complex templating
techniques [37]. (4) Richer surface functionalities. Compared
with traditional carbon materials, Biomass carbons are more
easily doped with heteroatoms, such as nitrogen, sulfur, or
phosphorus, either through the natural composition of biomass
or post‐synthesis treatments [38]. Doping enhances the con-
ductivity, wettability, and electrochemical activity of the carbon
material [39]. In addition, biomass‐derived carbons naturally
retain oxygen‐containing groups (–OH, –COOH, and –C=O)
during carbonization, which improve electrolyte wettability and
enables pseudocapacitive charge storage via faradaic reactions
[40]. (5) Lower environmental and energy costs. Unlike tradi-
tional graphitization, which requires temperatures above
2000°C, biomass carbonization typically occurs at moderate
temperatures (500–900°C), reducing energy consumption [41].
The activation of biomass carbon often uses safer and less toxic
agents (e.g., KOH and ZnCl
2
) compared to the hazardous
chemicals sometimes used in traditional carbon processing [42].
Carbon materials derived from pure biomass store energy pri-
marily through surface adsorption and desorption, which is
evident in the triangular or triangular‐like shapes observed in
their CV curves (Figure 1A–D). Consequently, electric double‐
layer capacitors (EDLCs) are developed utilizing biomass‐
derived carbon as active electrodes [43]. Nevertheless, EDLCs
exhibit lower capacitance compared to other energy storage sys-
tems. Thus, biomass‐derived carbon is utilized as a framework,
substrate, or intercalation material for synthesizing composites
(pseudocapacitors or battery materials), facilitating the creation
of high‐capacitance devices such as pseudocapacitors and hybrid
capacitors. Pseudocapacitor materials store energy through sur-
face redox reactions, which are characterized by prominent redox
peaks in the CV curves (Figure 1B–E). In contrast, battery ma-
terials demonstrate high capacitance from deep redox reactions
within the lattice of active components indicated by sharp redox
peaks in the CV curves (Figure 1C–F) [44].

Sources of Biomass‐Derived Carbon
Biomass‐derived carbon can be obtained from various sources,
including plant‐based materials (e.g., wood and agricultural
residues) [51–53], food waste (e.g., kitchen waste and fruit peels)
[54–57], animal‐based materials (e.g., organic waste products
from animals) [58], and microorganisms (e.g., carbon derived
from algae and bacteria) [59].
2.1
|
Plant‐Derived Carbon
Plant‐derived carbon refers to carbon materials obtained from
plants through carbonization or pyrolysis [60]. Biomass sources,
such as wood, pomelo peels, and coconut shells, are frequently
utilized as carbon sources for supercapacitor electrodes [61].
These materials are favored due to their high carbon content
and importance in supercapacitors. The carbonization process
entails heating biomass in the absence of oxygen, resulting in
the decomposition of organic matter and the creation of carbon‐
rich products [62]. The characteristics of the resulting plant‐
derived carbon vary based on the source material and the spe-
cifics of the carbonization method [63].
Carbon sourced from plants presents an effective electrode ma-
terial for supercapacitors and batteries, promoting a sustainable
approach to energy storage [64, 65]. The plant‐derived carbon
materials exhibit a continuous, interconnected, and hierarchical
architecture, which enhances energy storage capabilities in
supercapacitors. Mehdi et al. [66] developed activated carbon
from date seeds through pyrolysis, subsequently activating it with
H
2
SO
4
. The resulting carbon material showcases a remarkable
specific capacitance of 487.5 F g
−1
at a current density of 1 A g
−1
,
attributed to its high conductivity and efficient ion diffusion. Sun
et al. [67] developed porous carbon from kelp by immersing it in a
NaCl solution prior to activation and carbonization. The process
involved soaking the kelp in NaCl solution, removing the solvent,
and then calcining it to obtain porous‐activated carbon. During
annealing, NaCl forms a molten state that promotes efficient
pyrolysis of the kelp, leading to the formation of a 3D‐hierarchical
porous structure with a high surface area and a well‐developed
porous texture. Furthermore, NaCl activation enhances the
graphitization of carbon, thereby improving its electrical con-
ductivity. The resulting carbon material features an extraordi-
narily high‐specific surface area of 1197 m
2

pore volume, achieving a specific capacitance of 180 A g

565.3 F g
−1
at 1 A g
−1
due to improved ion transfer. Rajesh et al.
[69] synthesized activated carbon from pinecones using a pyrol-
ysis and chemical activation technique, achieving a high surface
area, increased pore density, and rich oxygen functionalities,
with a specific capacitance of 185 F g
−1
in 1 M acidic electrolyte.
Importantly, forestry‐based and agricultural‐based plants can be
used in producing biomass‐derived carbon. Forestry‐based and
agricultural‐based biomass are renewable and widely available
resources [70, 71]. A significant portion of forestry and agri-
cultural biomass is often treated as waste, such as sawdust,
wood chips, crop residues, husks, shells, and stalks [72]. Using
these waste materials to produce carbon materials not only re-
duces the burden of waste management but also creates value‐
added products contributing to a circular economy [73]. In
addition, forestry and agricultural source‐derived biomass
inherently possess a hierarchical structure, including pores and
fibers. These natural structures are advantageous for carbon
material production, allowing the fabrication of porous and
high‐surface‐area carbons without extensive processing. For
example, Li et al. [74] developed a green and cost‐effective
method involving preoxidation and self‐activation to produce
porous carbon from discarded tobacco stems. During the syn-
thesis process, the preoxidation step promotes the formation of a
cross‐linked structure within the tobacco stems, which enables
the derived porous carbon to possess abundant micropores and
high oxygen content during the self‐activation stage. The
resulting optimized porous carbon demonstrates an impressive
specific capacitance of 320 F g
−1
at a current density of 0.5
A g
−1
, along with excellent rate capability.

Food Waste‐Derived Carbon
Food waste presents a critical global challenge, making it
essential to explore sustainable and eco‐friendly methods for its
management and utilization. Carbon derived from food waste
can be generated by subjecting items, such as fruit peels, coffee
grounds, cooking oil residues, vegetable scraps, food processing
byproducts, and seafood shells, to an oxygen‐free heating pro-
cess. This method decomposes the organic components and
transforms them into a carbon‐rich substance. The carboniza-
tion of food waste not only enhances waste management prac-
tices but also produces valuable carbon materials beneficial for
supercapacitors.
Although kitchen waste poses considerable environmental risks,
techniques, such as pyrolysis, gasification, and hydrothermal
carbonization, can convert it into functional graphene‐like

Factors in Activation Processes for Biomass‐
Derived Carbon
The activation process is a critical step in converting biomass
into high‐performance carbon materials for supercapacitors.
Various factors influence the activation effect, which directly
impacts the surface area, porosity, conductivity, and overall
electrochemical performance of the synthesized biomass‐
derived carbon material. Below is a detailed explanation of the
key factors and their impacts:
3.1
|
Activation Agent
The activation agent (chemical or physical) determines the pore
structure, surface functionality, and overall quality of the carbon
material [17]. Chemical activation agents, such as KOH [27],
ZnCl
2
[134], and H
3
PO
4
[135], promote the formation of mi-
cropores and increase surface area. For instance, KOH creates
microporous structures, whereas ZnCl
2
generates mesoporous
structures. Physical agents, such as steam or CO
2
, promote the
formation of micropores. Physical activation agents, such as
CO
2
or steam, help develop mesoporous structures and control
pore distribution. The activation agent can also introduce
functional groups, enhancing the hydrophilicity and ion acces-
sibility of carbon material. Chemical activation produces higher
surface area and better‐developed pore structures compared to
physical activation. KOH activation is particularly effective in
creating microporous carbons suitable for supercapacitors,
enhancing charge storage capacity. For example, KOH activa-
tion of coconut shells resulted in activated carbon with a high
surface area of 1567 m
2
g
−1
and superior supercapacitor per-
formance due to well‐developed micropores and mesopores
[136]. ZnCl
2
activation of corncobs created mesoporous carbon
with enhanced ion transport capabilities [134].
3.2
|
Activation Temperature
Activation temperature influences the degree of carbonization
and pore development during the activation process. Lower
temperatures (400–600°C) produce carbons with lower surface
areas and incomplete activation [137]. Moderate temperatures
(700–900°C) optimize the development of micropores,
mesopores, and graphitic structures, resulting in a balance be-
tween conductivity and ion storage capacity. Excessively high
temperatures (> 900°C) may collapse micropores and reduce the
electrochemical surface area, but enhance graphitization, which
improves conductivity. Carbon derived from Artocarpus heter-
ophyllus seed activated at 700°C exhibited a surface area of
1841 m
2
g
−1
, leading to excellent specific capacitance. Activation
above 800°C led to the collapse of micropores and reduced
supercapacitor performance for carbon derived from rice
husks [138].
3.3
|
Activation Time
The duration of the activation process determines the extent of
pore formation and carbon conversion. Short activation times
(30–60 min) result in incomplete pore formation and low sur-
face areas. Optimal activation times (1–3 h) balance pore
development and structural integrity. Excessive activation times
(> 4 h) may lead to over‐etching, structural collapse, and
decreased material yield. For example, carbon derived from
paulownia sawdust activated for 2 h under 700°C exhibited a
surface area of 2435 m
2
g
−1
. Over‐etching of carbon from
corncobs after 4 h of activation resulted in lower mechanical
strength and reduced supercapacitor performance [139].
3.4
|
Biomass Type Composition
The intrinsic composition of the biomass (lignin, cellulose, and
hemicellulose content) influences the final properties of the
activated carbon. Lignin‐rich precursors (e.g., coconut shells
and wood) produce carbons with high hardness and micropo-
rosity [140]. Cellulose‐rich precursors (e.g., corncobs and cot-
ton) favor mesoporosity and high surface area [37]. The
elemental composition (C, H, O, and N content) of the biomass
affects the conductivity and functionality of the resultant car-
bon. Biomasses with high mineral content (e.g., K, Na, and Mg)
act as natural activators during pyrolysis, facilitating pore for-
mation [141]. High mineral content improves pore development
but may require acid washing to remove residual minerals and
prevent conductivity loss. Coconut shell‐derived carbon showed
excellent micropore volume and high specific capacitance in
KOH‐activated samples [142]. Corncob‐derived carbon exhibi-
ted mesoporous structures with fast ion transport properties
suitable for high‐power density supercapacitors.
3.5
|
Mass Ratio of Activation Agent to Biomass
The mass ratio between the activation agent and biomass de-
termines the extent of pore formation and activation efficiency.
A higher mass ratio promotes the development of micropores
and increases surface area but may reduce mechanical strength.
A lower mass ratio may result in incomplete activation and
lower surface area. A KOH‐to‐biomass ratio of 3:1 was found
optimal for rice husk‐derived carbon, producing a surface area
of 755 m
2
g
−1
[143].

3.6

Pretreatment of Biomass
Pretreatment techniques, such as washing, soaking, or chemical
modification, can improve activation efficiency by removing
impurities or introducing functional groups. Washing removes
inorganic impurities that could block pores or interfere with
activation. Chemical pretreatments (e.g., NaCl soaking) can
introduce molten states that facilitate pore formation. Kelp
presoaked in NaCl produced activated carbon with a 3D hier-
archical porous structure and high specific capacitance due to
improved graphitization [67]. Acid‐washed biomass reduced ash
content and improved surface area and conductivity [144, 145].
4
|
Strategies Used in Modified Biomass‐Derived
Carbon
The electrochemical performance of biomass‐derived carbon
can be significantly enhanced through additional treatments,
such as heteroatom doping [34, 146], surface functionalization
[53], and the construction of hybrid composites [40, 147]. These
modifications are designed to increase specific capacitance,
improve charge–discharge rates, and enhance overall electro-
chemical performance, making the material more suitable for
use in supercapacitors [32, 33, 42].
4.1
|

structure modifies its electronic properties and surface chemis-

wettability in carbon electrodes used in supercapacitors is
essential to facilitate the transfer of electrolyte ions. Atomic
doping with elements, such as N, O, B, P, and S, can enhance the
wettability of carbon materials, thereby increasing the number of
active sites and improving capacitance [149]. Notably, N doping
amplifies the effectiveness of the active sites at the interface be-
tween the electrode and electrolyte, whereas P doping fosters the
formation of porous carbon with enhanced electrochemical
properties [146, 150]. The combined introduction of dual or
multiple heteroatoms is likely to significantly enhance the elec-
trochemical characteristics of carbon materials due to their syn-
ergistic effects. Ma et al. [130] developed hierarchical porous
carbon nanoarchitectonics co‐doped with N and P, achieving a

Surface Functionalization
Enhancing the surface characteristics of biochar via functional
groups or conductive coatings can boost its electron and ion
conductivity, thus improving energy storage and charge–
discharge efficiency. Utilizing physical or chemical activation
methods can generate additional micropores and mesopores in
biochar, which increases its surface area and facilitates ion
diffusion, thereby augmenting energy storage capabilities.
Depending on the intended application, further modification of
the biomass carbon surface can be executed through various
chemical treatments or coatings. Introducing functional groups,
such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, or amino groups, may enhance the
adsorption characteristics and catalytic activity of biomass car-
bon. For instance, He et al. [153] developed a hierarchical porous
carbon structure from wood waste, characterized by a high spe-
cific surface area and abundant surface functional groups,
particularly C–O bonds. During the preparation process, natural
wood was pretreated with 0.1 M NaCl and 0.55 M NaClO
2
for
delignification, effectively removing lignin, as confirmed by FTIR
spectra indicating an increase in C–O bonds. The pretreated wood
was then carbonized under a nitrogen atmosphere at 900°C for
3 h, producing biochar material enriched with surface functional
groups. The abundant C–O bonds on the surface of the biomass‐
derived carbon significantly enhance pseudocapacitance, thereby
improving its specific capacity. The resulting balsa‐based elec-
trode achieved an impressive specific capacitance of 252.1 F g
−1
and maintained a cyclic retention of 85% after 10 000 cycles,
attributed to the surface functional groups and precise micropore
management. Additionally, Zhuang et al. [154] fabricated NiCo‐
LDH on the surface of carbon quantum dots obtained from
pomelo peel, with the optimized composite demonstrating a high
specific capacitance of 1814 F g
−1
at 1 A g
−1
. Thus, incorporating
conductive functional groups into biomass‐derived carbon not
only serves as active sites for charge storage, improving electro-
chemical performance, but also enhances wettability for superior
electrolyte contact. Furthermore, Bao et al. [155] created func-
tionalized biomass porous carbon from wheat straw (FWS),
modifying the carbon by treating it with an ethylenediamine so-
lution via hydrothermal methods (Figure 8A,B). Wheat straw‐
derived porous carbon was functionalized by treating it in an
ethylenediamine (EDA) solution through a hydrothermal reac-
tion at 140°C for 10 h, yielding amino‐functionalized porous
carbon. Subsequently, the redox‐active molecule 2,6‐dia-
minoanthraquinone (DAQ) was anchored onto the amino‐
functionalized biomass porous carbon (FWS) via strong
hydrogen bonding interactions. The protophilic C=O functional
group increases interaction between the carbon and DAQ,
achieving an exceptional specific capacitance of 424.9 F g
−1
at
1 A g
−1
(Figure 8C) along with a commendable cyclic retention of
93.1% after 10 000 cycles. The active functional groups (–COOH
and –OH) present on the biomass‐derived carbon facilitate
nucleation and limit the growth of loaded active materials due to
electrostatic interactions. As shown in Figure 8D,F, the optimized
DAQ@FWS exhibits a significantly lower adsorption energy
(E

cating a stronger interaction between DAQ and FWS through
hydrogen bonding. This enhanced adhesion of DAQ molecules to
the FWS effectively mitigates the detachment of redox‐active
molecules from the carbon substrate, thereby improving the
rate performance and cycling stability of the samples. As depicted
in Figure 8E, the C=O group in DAQ serves as the protophilic site
in the redox reaction, facilitating the storage of hydrogen ions.
Furthermore, the total density of states (TDOS) of DAQ@FWS at

enhanced conductivity of DAQ@FWS (Figure 8G).

5.1
|
Functions in the Pure Biomass‐Derived
Carbon
5.1.1
|
High Surface Area for Charge Storage
Biomass‐derived carbon provides a high specific surface area
that allows the accumulation of charges at the electrode–
electrolyte interface, forming an electric double layer, which is
the primary energy storage mechanism in many porous carbon‐
based supercapacitors [160–162]. Biomass precursors often yield
materials with a combination of micropores (charge storage),
mesopores (ion diffusion), and macropores (electrolyte trans-
port) [163, 164]. The high surface area provides abundant active
sites for charge accumulation at the electrode–electrolyte
interface, which enhances the capacitance and energy density
of the supercapacitor [165]. Although biomass carbon performs
well in EDLC, the uniformity of pore distribution and specific
surface area control remain challenges. Some biomass carbons
may have a high surface area but lack accessible pores for
electrolyte ions, which can reduce capacitance [166].
Certain biomass‐derived carbons possess heteroatom functional
groups (e.g., O, N, P, or S) that facilitate redox reactions,
contributing to pseudocapacitive behavior. These redox‐active
sites enhance the total capacitance by adding a faradaic contri-
bution to the EDLC mechanism [167]. However, the stability of
these functional groups over long cycling and their influence on
self‐discharge rates require further investigation. Excessive
pseudocapacitance can lead to reduced cycling stability.
5.1.2
|
Porous Structure for Ion Transport
Biomass‐derived carbons are valued for their intrinsic ability to
produce hierarchical porous structures during activation pro-
cesses, ensuring efficient ion diffusion and electrolyte transport
[60, 129]. Mesopores act as ion diffusion channels, reducing the
resistance and improving charge/discharge rates. This hierar-
chical design reduces ion diffusion resistance and enhances the
rate performance and power density of the supercapacitor [168,
169]. However, not all biomass sources yield optimal porosity.
The synthesis process (activation methods, temperatures, and
precursors) can lead to undesirable pore size distribution. For
instance, excessive microporosity may trap ions, reducing the
performance [170].
5.1.3
|
High Electrical Conductivity for Electron
Transport
After proper activation (e.g., chemical or thermal activation) or
graphitization, biomass carbon achieves enhanced electrical
conductivity, ensuring fast electron transport between the
electrode and external circuit, which contributes to the high
power density and charge‐discharge efficiency of the super-
capacitor [171–173]. Graphitization introduces an sp
2
‐hybrid-
ized carbon structure, enhancing conductivity. Biomass‐derived
carbons with high conductivity reduce internal resistance and
improve the power density of supercapacitors [174, 175]. How-
ever, the conductivity of biomass‐derived carbon is still lower
compared to highly graphitized carbon materials such as gra-
phene. Additional treatment methods, such as chemical doping
or combining with conductive additives, may be necessary to
match performance.
5.2
|
Functions in the Biomass‐Derived Carbon
Composites
5.2.1
|
Active Materials
Biomass‐derived carbon can be integrated with conductive
polymers and other active materials, such as oxides, sulfides,
MXenes, and graphene, to create composites for use in super-
capacitors. These carbon materials, originating from biomass,
are excellent for energy storage in supercapacitors due to their
capacity to form an electric double‐layer (EDL) at their surface
[176]. The inherent highly porous structure and extensive sur-
face area of biomass carbon enhance capacitance and power
density, thereby improving the energy storage capability of
supercapacitors. Furthermore, biomass carbon exhibits good
electrical conductivity, allowing electrons to flow swiftly, which
leads to rapid charging and discharging capabilities [177].
Supercapacitors utilizing biomass‐derived carbon typically
demonstrate long cycle life, enabling them to endure numerous
charge/discharge cycles with minimal performance loss, estab-
lishing their reliability for diverse applications. For instance,
Zeng et al. [178] reported on porous carbon extracted from
kapok flower biomass waste, achieving a capacitance of
286.8 F g
−1
at 1 A g
−1
with a remarkable capacitance retention
of 97.4% after 5000 cycles. This performance is credited to its
high specific surface area, which supports efficient electrolyte

engineered carbon materials with a significant specific surface
area and increased oxygen content, attaining a specific capaci-
tance of 437 F g
−1
at 1 A g
−1
and a capacitance retention of
90.9% after 100 000 cycles. Lastly, Qin et al. [114] introduced
hierarchical porous carbon, realizing a specific capacitance of
255 F g
−1
at 0.5 A g
−1
, benefiting from its extensive surface area
for effective electrical charge storage (Figure 10D–G).

surface of the biomass carbon framework [183], utilizing
methods, such as deposition, coating, or infiltration, within its
porous structure [184]. The carbon derived from biomass acts as a

Different Types of Nanocomposites for
Supercapacitor
Biomass‐derived carbon typically exhibits EDLC properties due
to the adsorption and desorption mechanisms involved in the
charge and discharge cycles [ 39 ] (Figure 13 ). However, this re-
sults in relatively low capacitance in supercapacitors with pure
biomass‐derived carbon as active materials. To enhance the
energy density of supercapacitors, various composites incorpo-
rating carbon materials have been developed [ 156 ], such as
carbon/MXenes [ 191 ], carbon/MOFs [ 192, 193 ], and carbon/
oxides [ 194 ]. These composites facilitate faradaic redox re-
actions at the surface or within their lattice structures, which
leads to increased capacitance and characteristics of pseudoca-
pacitors or hybrid supercapacitors [ 194–196 ]. The comparison
table highlights the performance differences between pure
biomass‐derived carbon and carbon‐based composites derived
from biomass in supercapacitor applications (Table 2 ).
The combined exploration of biomass carbon with conductive
polymers, metal oxides (e.g., MnO
2
, MoO
2
, and NiO), CNTs, and
other materials is becoming increasingly common in super-
capacitor research and application (Figure 13 ) [ 197 ]. Integrating
biomass carbon with materials that possess high specific surface
areas, such as metal oxides and conductive polymers, signifi-
cantly boosts the energy density of supercapacitors. This
approach leverages the porous structure of biomass carbon
along with the favorable electrochemical properties of other
active materials. Additionally, the substantial surface area and
porosity of biomass‐derived carbon facilitate enhanced charge
adsorption [ 198 ]. The durability of biomass‐derived carbon also
contributes to improved cycle stability in supercapacitors.
Furthermore, composite materials can increase power density,
allowing for quicker charging and discharging. As a renewable
resource, biomass‐derived carbon promotes environmental
sustainability and reduces dependence on finite resources.

the electrode with the electrolyte [206], resulting in ion
adsorption and charge transfer to improve capacitance
and energy storage efficiency [56].

retention of 95% is obtained after 5000 cycles in the super-
capacitor (Figure 18E,F).
6.6
|
Biomass‐Derived Carbon/Layered Double
Hydroxides
Layered double hydroxides (LDHs) are a category of materials
with a layered structure. LDHs can store electrical charge due to
metal cations within their layered structure [233]. This property
makes them attractive for supercapacitor applications, as it
contributes to a high charge storage capacity [234]. Biomass‐
derived carbon provides a conductive network and high sur-
face area, facilitating efficient charge transfer and ion adsorp-
tion [49]. Combining biomass‐derived carbon and LDHs offers a
promising avenue for supercapacitor electrode materials [235].
Biomass‐derived carbon is often used to enhance electrical
conductivity and provide a porous structure for efficient ion
transport, whereas LDHs contribute to high charge storage ca-
pacity and pseudocapacitance. Yang et al. [236] grew NiCo‐
layered double hydroxide (NiCo‐LDH) on a fresh rose‐derived
carbon substrate. The carbon derived from biomass presents a
significant specific surface area suitable for loading active ma-
terials. The resulting composite demonstrates an impressive
specific capacitance of 606.4 F g
−1
at a current density of
0.5 A g
−1
using 6 M KOH as the electrolyte. Fang et al. [237]
fabricated Ni‐Al‐layered double hydroxide nanosheets on the
carbon fibers originating from the cotton stalk, delivering
enhanced conductivity and large active sites (Figure 19A,B). The
obtained nanocomposites exhibit a high specific capacitance of
1214.9 F g
−1
at 1 A g
−1
with 1 M KOH as the electrolyte
(Figure 19C). Qu et al. [238] prepared the nanocomposites with
Ni, Co‐LDH nanosheets, and carbon dots from fungus bran
(Figure 19D). The composites exhibit abundant accessible active
sites and favorable electrical conductivity (Figure 19E), leading
to an exceptionally high specific capacitance of 2100 F g
−1
at
1 A
−1
using 3 M KOH as the electrolyte (Figure 19F,G).
In addition, nanocomposites composed of biomass‐derived car-
bon and other transition metal compounds, such as sulfides [157,
239], phosphides, and selenides, also hold excellent prospects for
supercapacitors. Subsequently, a comparative summary of the
performance of supercapacitors incorporating biomass‐derived
carbon composites combined with other active materials is pre-
sented in Table 3. The performance metrics of biomass‐derived
carbon in Table 1 are compared to those of biomass‐derived
carbon composites in Table 3, with a primary focus on the spe-
cific capacitance at a current density of 1 A g
−1
. Notably, the
specific capacitance of biomass carbon composites can reach up
to 2000 F g
−1
, significantly surpassing the specific capacitance of
pure biomass carbon materials, which is approximately 500 F g
−1
.
This demonstrates that constructing biomass carbon‐based
composites effectively enhances the energy density of super-
capacitors. However, the cycling performance of supercapacitors
based on biomass carbon composites is slightly inferior to that of
pure biomass carbon materials. To address this, future im-
provements could incorporate surfactants during the material
preparation process to enhance the interface between biomass
carbon and the incorporated active materials, thereby improving
structural stability and cycling performance.

Mechanisms Study of Biomass‐Derived Carbon
in Supercapacitor
The performance improvement mechanisms of supercapacitors
utilizing pure and composite biomass‐derived carbon can be
analyzed through theoretical calculations, focusing on energy
band structure, density of states, electron cloud density, and
adsorption energy.
7.1
|
Energy Band Structure
Energy band structure analysis is utilized to illustrate the
mechanism of biomass‐derived carbon enhanced by doping and
compositing for supercapacitors. Energy band structures pro-
vide insights into how doping and compositing affect the ma-
terial's conductivity, bandgap, and charge transport properties
[ 251 ]. Bandgap calculation helps visualize the reduction or
modification of the bandgap, which enhances electrical con-
ductivity and charge storage efficiency. The interaction between
dopants or composite materials and carbon can be evaluated to
determine the impact on charge carrier mobility and density.
Band structure analysis reveals electronic‐level improvements,
aiding in the selection of dopants or composites to optimize
supercapacitor performance. For example, Zhang et al. [ 252 ]
fabricated Cd
2
SiO
4
@C composite for supercapacitor, where
amorphous carbon is prepared from planted gourd leaves. The
Cd
2
SiO
4
@C composite exhibits super‐electrochemical perfor-
mance with a high capacitance of 607 F g
− 1
at 0.5 A g
− 1
and
98.2% retention after 10 000 cycles. Theoretical calculations of
energy band structures provide insight into the mechanism
underlying the performance enhancement of biomass carbon‐
based composites. Amorphous carbon, lacking a bandgap in
its energy band structure, demonstrates its naturally conductive
properties (Figure 20A ). The bandgap of Cd
2
SiO
4
(022) is
calculated to be approximately 0.880 eV (Figure 20C ), whereas
the introduction of amorphous carbon reduces the bandgap of
Cd
2
SiO
4
(022)@C to about 0.487 eV (Figure 20B ). This reduction
indicates improved electrical conductivity in Cd
2
SiO
4
@C.
Additionally, the pronounced band flattening in Cd
2
SiO
4
@C
signifies diminished atomic interactions and an increased
effective charge carrier density, enhancing electron transport
efficiency.

Density of State
Density of state (DOS) provides detailed insights into the dis-
tribution of electronic states near the Fermi level, revealing how
doping and compositing influence conductivity [ 128, 251 ]. By
analyzing the DOS near the Fermi level, researchers can
determine the availability of charge carriers, which directly
impact the material's electrochemical performance [ 253 ]. DOS
highlights how dopants (e.g., N, S, and P) introduce additional
states, modify the energy levels, and enhance conductivity or
pseudocapacitive behavior [ 254 ]. Changes in the DOS can
illustrate how the bandgap narrows or shifts due to compositing
or doping, facilitating better electron transport and charge
storage [ 230 ]. DOS analysis identifies which atomic orbitals
(e.g., s, p, and d) contribute to the electronic states, providing a
deeper understanding of the role of specific dopants or com-
posite components [ 230 ]. It bridges experimental results and
theoretical models by explaining the enhanced capacitance and
conductivity mechanisms through state density changes [ 251 ].
For example, the three‐dimensional N, S‐co‐doped hierarchical
porous carbons (N, S‐LPHC) derived from lignin were designed
using thiourea and KOH as active agents [ 253 ]. The optimized
N, S‐LPHC exhibits an impressive specific surface area of
1639.8 m
2
g
− 1
, resulting in a specific capacitance of 185.5 F g
− 1
at 0.5 A g
− 1
. Theoretical calculations reveal that N and S doping
in biomass‐derived carbon effectively adjusts the electronic
distribution, enhancing charge transfer efficiency during elec-
trode reactions. As shown in Figure 20D,E , the DOS for N, S‐
carbon and pure carbon indicates that the total DOS of both
carbon materials is primarily contributed by the s and p orbitals.
Nitrogen and sulfur doping significantly increase the DOS near
the Fermi level in the composite structure, facilitating rapid
electron transfer within the material.
7.3
|
Electron Cloud Density
Electron cloud density provides a clear visualization of how
electrons are distributed in the material, showing the impact of
doping and compositing on electronic properties [ 230 ]. It reveals
the nature and strength of chemical bonds between atoms in
biomass‐derived carbon and dopants or composite materials,
explaining enhanced structural stability and conductivity.
Electron cloud density highlights the regions of high electron
concentration, indicating areas where charge transfer occurs
more effectively during electrochemical processes. The analysis
shows how dopants, such as nitrogen, sulfur, or phosphorus,
redistribute electron density, introducing active sites that
enhance electrochemical performance. For composite materials,
electron cloud density helps evaluate charge redistribution at
the interfaces, providing insights into improved electron trans-
port and ion adsorption. It identifies regions with high electron
density, which are critical for adsorption, redox reactions, or
charge storage, aiding in the optimization of supercapacitor
materials. Electron cloud density analysis explains how doping
and compositing influence the conductivity, capacitance, and
overall electrochemical behavior of the material. When com-
bined with DOS and band structure analysis, it provides a
comprehensive understanding of electronic properties and
charge dynamics. For example, the possible structure of the N,
S‐carbon derived from lignin using graphite as the carbon body
is shown in Figure 20F [ 253 ]. Then, the electron orbitals and
electron cloud density of N, S‐carbon and graphite carbon are
calculated (Figure 20G ), illustrating that the electron distribu-
tion of N, S‐carbon becomes uneven and imbalanced compared
with undoped carbon, enhancing the polarity of molecular
structure. The increased electron cloud density near nitrogen
atoms is attributed to the electron‐donating effect of nitrogen,

Adsorption Energy
The adsorption energy between biomass‐derived carbon with
doping and compositing and electrolyte ions is a key metric for
understanding the improved mechanism in supercapacitors
[ 255 ]. Doping and compositing alter the surface chemistry of
biomass‐derived carbon, enhancing ion accessibility and stor-
age, which boosts the s overall capacitance [ 254, 256 ]. Adsorp-
tion energy calculations also reveal specific active sites
introduced by dopants (e.g., N, S, or P) or composite materials,
where ions preferentially adsorb, facilitating more efficient ion
exchange and charge transfer during electrochemical processes
[ 257–259 ]. For example, N doping generates electron‐rich re-
gions that enhance cation adsorption, whereas S doping in-
troduces polar sites that improve anion adsorption [ 253 ].
Biomass‐derived carbon composites incorporating materials,
such as metal oxides or conductive polymers, further adjust
adsorption energy, creating a synergistic effect between the
phases for superior ion–electrode interactions, which enhances
both double‐layer capacitance and pseudocapacitance contri-
butions [ 260, 261 ]. Adsorption energy analysis also guides the
functionalization of biomass‐derived carbon with targeted dop-
ants or composites, optimizing the surface for improved ion
interaction and overall performance [ 128 ]. For example, Lu
et al. [ 259 ] fabricated porous carbon with multiple heteroatoms
(N, O, P, and S) derived from rapeseed meals, obtaining a high
specific capacitance of 416 F g
− 1
at 1 A g
− 1
and a decent
capacitance retention of 92% after 10 000 cycles. According to
the density functional theory (DFT) calculation results, the el-
ements O, N, P, and S can significantly enhance the electron
negativity and density, improving the adsorption and diffusion
of K
þ
to attain a high capacitance. Compared with differential
charge density for a potassium atom adsorbed on a carbon ring,
doping graphene with multiple heteroatoms leads to an uneven
charge distribution on the surface, with the charge of the po-
tassium ion primarily transferred to the carbon atoms near the
heteroatoms (Figure 21A,B ). Side views further reveal that the
distance between the potassium ion and the graphene layer
decreases from 0.265 to 0.205 nm after heteroatom doping,
indicating enhanced potassium ion adsorption on heteroatom‐
doped graphene. The adsorption energies on pure and
heteroatom‐doped graphene surfaces are calculated to be − 0.734
and − 2.026 eV (Figure 21C ), suggesting that potassium ions are
more likely to adsorb on heteroatom‐doped graphene surfaces,
thereby facilitating electrochemical adsorption reactions with
high capacitance.

Conclusion and Perspectives
This review focuses on biomass‐derived carbons and their com-
posites used in supercapacitors. Initially, this review identifies
four main biomass sources for biomass‐derived carbon, including
plant‐based, food waste, animal‐derived, and microorganism‐
derived sources. Then, the review explores strategies to
enhance the electrochemical performance of biomass‐derived
carbon in supercapacitors, with a focus on three key methods:
heteroatom doping, surface functionalization, and hybrid com-
posite fabrication. Following this, the roles of biomass‐derived
carbon in both pure and composite forms within super-
capacitors are discussed. The review also examines composites
incorporating biomass‐derived carbon to improve supercapacitor
performance, highlighting various combinations with active
materials such as MXenes, MOFs, graphene, conductive poly-
mers, and transition metal oxides and hydroxides. Finally, the
mechanisms behind the performance enhancements of modified
biomass carbon are analyzed from a theoretical perspective
considering four critical factors: energy band structure, density of
states, electron cloud density, and adsorption energy. In conclu-
sion, this review provides a comprehensive examination of the
sources, functions, and mechanisms of biomass‐derived carbon,
underscoring its potential for advancing high‐performance
supercapacitor technologies.
Future research trends in biomass carbon‐based supercapacitors
are expected to concentrate on several key areas (Figure 22 ):
1. Investigating new biomass sources for carbon material
production. Researchers are actively seeking innovative
and unconventional biomass sources to broaden the

focus on utilizing plastic waste and food waste, such as
grape pomace from the winemaking industry and dairy
byproducts from whey and cheese production, to develop
high‐performance electrode materials while addressing

2. Customizing the structure and surface chemistry of
biomass‐derived carbon. A key objective is the develop-
ment of biomass‐derived carbon materials with optimized
porous structures and surface chemistry. Careful manip-
ulation of pore size distribution and the composition of
surface functional groups significantly affects the electro-
chemical performance of supercapacitors. Incorporating
additional heteroatoms, such as S, P, and B, can also

characteristics of biomass carbon by adding various func-

strategic modification elevates the material's adsorption
capabilities and conductivity.
3. Developing innovative composites using biomass‐derived
carbon. The creation of composites that blend biomass‐
derived carbon with other materials can yield synergistic
effects and improved capacitance, thereby enhancing
supercapacitor performance. Presently, composites based
on biomass‐derived carbon primarily utilize graphene and
metal oxides. MXenes and MOFs are emerging as prom-
ising materials for energy storage applications. Nonethe-
less, research on composites incorporating MXenes, MOFs,
and biomass‐derived carbon remains limited. Further
exploration in this domain is warranted to advance
supercapacitor performance.
4. Exploring electrochemical mechanisms of biomass‐derived
carbon. Understanding the electrochemical behavior of
biomass‐derived carbon in supercapacitors is critical for
performance enhancement. Key areas of focus include ion
transfer, intercalation, and structural characteristics.
Employing first‐principle calculations can elucidate the
mechanisms that enhance supercapacitor performance
through biomass‐derived carbon, particularly by assessing
the effects of various element doping on capacitance.
Furthermore, computational simulations can investigate
the influence of surface functional groups (e.g., –COOH, –
OH, and –NH
4
) on carbon materials, thereby identifying
their roles in ion transport and informing strategies for
effective surface modification.
5. Improving the industrial applicability of biomass‐derived
carbon for supercapacitors. Future research must priori-
tize enhancing the scalability and sustainability of biomass‐
derived carbon materials for industrial applications,
particularly in supercapacitors. These efforts are vital for
transitioning from laboratory‐scale innovations to practical
real‐world deployment. One effective strategy is the utili-
zation of widely available biomass sources, including agri-
cultural residues (e.g., rice husks, corn stover, and
sugarcane bagasse), forestry residues (e.g., wood chips and
bark), and food waste (e.g., fruit peels and coffee grounds).
Such resources offer a low‐cost and abundant supply of raw
materials, significantly reducing production expenses while
ensuring a consistent and reliable feedstock for large‐scale
manufacturing processes. Additionally, employing energy‐
efficient approaches, such as microwave‐assisted pyrolysis
and hydrothermal carbonization, can help lower energy
consumption and further reduce production costs, thus
enhancing the feasibility of producing biomass‐derived
carbon on an industrial scale. Replacing hazardous activa-
tion agents, such as KOH and ZnCl
2
, with eco‐friendly al-
ternatives, such as NaCl, or adopting physical activation
techniques (e.g., CO
2
or steam), can effectively mitigate
environmental impact and improve the safety of waste
management practices associated with production. These
advancements collectively serve to promote the industrial
viability and long‐term sustainability of biomass‐derived
carbon materials, positioning them as a key component of
next‐generation energy storage technologies.
In summary, future research on biomass carbon‐based super-
capacitors will focus on advancing innovative biomass mate-
rials, enhancing the surface properties of carbon materials,
developing high‐performance composite materials, uncovering
the underlying mechanisms, and enhancing the practical
application for performance optimization.
