Keywords: activated carbon, birch wood, NaOH activation, pore structure, supercapacitors,

The valorization of biomass residues into advanced carbon materials offers a promising route 
toward more sustainable supercapacitor technologies. Birchwood was evaluated as a 
sustainable precursor for activated carbon electrodes in aqueous supercapacitors. Carbon was 
produced via carbonization, grinding, and activated at 700 or 800 °C using varying quantities 
of NaOH. Structural characterization (N₂ sorption at 77 K, XRD, Raman, SEM, elemental 
analysis, XPS) indicated specific surface areas up to 2401 m 2 g-1 and a temperature‑dependent 
pore architecture: predominantly microporous at 700 °C and mixed micro/mesoporous at 
800 °C. Electrochemical performance was assessed in symmetric CR2032 coin cells with a 
phosphate buffer electrolyte, using CV and GCD. The highest specific capacitance reached 
247 F g-1, exceeding that of commercial carbon Kuraray YP-80F by up to 115% under given 
test conditions. Although more pronounced graphitic structures develop after treatment at 
800 °C and may enhance conductivity, they also coincide with reduced surface area and 
consequently do not improve capacitance. Samples activated at 700 °C generally delivered 
superior capacitance and competitive rate capability, likely due to enhanced microporosity 
which increases the accessible surface area for electric double‑layer formation. These results 
suggest birchwood is a viable high‑value feedstock for carbon-based supercapacitor electrodes.

1. Introduction
Supercapacitors have been widely researched due to their advantageous properties as energy 
storage devices - high power density, rapid charge-discharge rates, and long cycle life. 1 They 
play a key role in bridging the gap between traditional dielectric capacitors and batteries or fuel 
cells. Consequently, they are used in diverse technologies such as electric traction drives, wind, 
solar and wave energy generation systems, and microgrid applications.2,3 
However, supercapacitors have notable shortcomings limiting their potential applications. They 
generally have a lower energy density than batteries, as the charge storage is limited to surfaces 
or near the active surface area of the electrodes. 1 Carbon-based materials are considered ideal 
candidates for supercapacitor electrodes because of a combination of various beneficial 
properties and the ability to modify these properties. These include high specific surface area, 
good electrical conductivity, chemical stability, low cost, and abundant availability.4
In recent years, research on supercapacitors has increasingly focused on raising energy density 
while retaining fast charge-discharge and long cycle life. While commercial carbon‑based 
EDLCs can range from 5 to 11 Wh kg -1, many studies now target ≥10-20  Wh kg -1 without 
sacrificing >90% capacitance retention over tens of thousands of cycles.5–8 
Progress in carbon materials has been central to this shift. Beyond maximizing surface area, 
current work emphasizes hierarchical pore architectures – micropores that store charge 
combined with small mesopores that shorten ion pathways – as well as heteroatom doping to 
improve wettability and interfacial charge storage. Representative examples from existing 
studies include eucalyptus‑leaf‑derived activated carbons, which delivered 196  F g -1 when 
measured in a three‑electrode configuration by cyclic voltammetry at 5 mV s -1 in 2M KOH, 
while N‑doping increased the capacitance to 258  F g -1 under the same conditions 9. Another 
study reported porous carbons derived from a mixture of corncob and fly ash waste, achieving 
240  F g-1 at 1 A g-1 in a three‑electrode setup in 6M KOH electrolyte.10 In addition, hierarchical

Activated carbons (ACs) produced from wood and other biomass sources have shown potential 
for use in energy storage devices, particularly supercapacitors. 4,15 This can be related to their 
role as environmentally friendly and sustainable alternatives to traditional fossil fuel-based 
precursors such as coal and petroleum. 16–18 The utilization of biomass waste, e.g. agricultural 
by-products, offers a low-cost, abundant, and readily available raw material source, which 
lowers production costs and provides economic incentives.15,16,19–21 
Another abundant waste material in Europe and a notable carbon source is wood sawdust. For 
example, Sweden produces approximately 1.9 million tons of sawdust annually22 as a byproduct 
from various sawmills. Converting these waste materials into high-value activated carbons 
supports the development of diversified products and contributes to a circular bioeconomy.15,23 
During the carbonization and activation processes, biomass develops a high specific surface 
area and an abundant pore structure, making it a good candidate for electrode materials in 
supercapacitors.20,24,25 The unique structure inherent to some biomass types, such as wood, can 
facilitate efficient electrolyte transport and ion diffusion, which are important for 
electrochemical performance.26
Over the last decades, sustainability considerations have become more prominent. 
Biomass‑derived carbons made from abundant biomass sources are increasingly adopted due 
to their scalability, tunable micro/mesopore distributions relevant to aqueous electrolytes, and 
reduced life‑cycle impacts relative to fossil‑derived carbons. Recent reviews detail green

In this work, we investigate a sustainable precursor derived from birch tree (Betula spp.) 
charcoal. Birch species are widely distributed across North America, Asia, and Europe, most 
notably in the Nordic and Baltic regions. 33 Birch is one of the most consumed hard woods in 
Europe in the pulp and paper industry, in 2021, it accounted for approximately 14% of all 
consumed wood species and 50% if only hardwood is considered.34
During logging and processing in sawmills and pulp industries, birch generates significant 
waste, typically regarded as a low-value byproduct. A resulting residue type depends on the 
processing method and is typically bark, chips or shavings. In some cases, residues are further 
used, e.g., residues from milling are source for biologically active substances, fiberboards, 
different paper products and heating briquettes. 35 Composed primarily of cellulose, 
hemicellulose, and lignin, birch waste is well-suited for creating porous carbon with minimal 
impurities – an essential characteristic for high-performance supercapacitor applications. 36 
Despite the abundance of birch as a biomass precursor, its use in energy‑storage electrodes 
remains limited. While some studies have explored birch‑derived carbons in supercapacitors 
using acidic15, neutral15, or organic electrolytes36, the neutral‑electrolyte performance reported 
so far has been modest, and competitive behavior in a neutral pH buffered aqueous electrolyte 
has not yet been demonstrated.

Here, we report the structural and electrochemical properties of birchwood-derived carbons 
depending on the synthesis temperature and activation parameters. We use 0.5M K 2HPO4 / 
0.5M KH2PO4 as a model electrolyte. Beyond its compatibility with high‑surface‑area carbons, 
this buffered electrolyte provides a stable pH environment that minimizes dynamic pH shifts 
during operation.37 As a result, it provides a more predictable electrochemical environment in 
which intrinsic electrode processes can be assessed accurately. 37 The materials obtained have 
up to 2401 m 2 g-1 SBET surface area and 247 F g -1 specific capacitance, surpassing the 
commercially available alternative Kuraray YP-80F measured at the same system by as much 
as 115%. These findings highlight birch as a promising high-value resource for producing 
electrode materials for supercapacitors.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1 Synthesis of activated carbon
Commercial birch charcoal (SIA Marienbade, Latvia) was used as a precursor. Charcoal was 
first ground using a Retsch SM100 cutting mill followed by fine grinding in a Fritsch 
Pulverisette 4 planetary mill (zirconia balls and bowls) to achieve a uniform particle size 
distribution (~5 µm). For activation, the ground charcoal was impregnated with NaOH at mass 
ratios of 1:2 or 1:3 (precursor:activator) in the presence of deionized water. The sample was 
transferred into the stainless-steel reactor and placed into the preheated oven, where activation 
was carried out under argon flow (100 L h -1) at temperatures of 700 or 800 °C for 2 h. After 
cooling, the materials were thoroughly washed by sequential treatments with 10% HCl and 
deionized water until a neutral pH was achieved, followed by drying at 105 °C. The resulting 
activated carbons are referred to as BWC-X-YYY, where BWC stands for birch wood carbon, 
X denotes the NaOH to precursor mass ratio and YYY – the activation temperature.

2.2.  Structural and Compositional Characterization
The composition and structure of the obtained materials were characterized using several 
methods. Nitrogen sorptometry analysis was conducted using a Quantachrome NOVA 4200K 
instrument at 77 K to obtain volumetric and surface areas, total pore volume, and pore size 
distribution. 
Elemental (ultimate) analysis was performed using a Vario Macro Elementar CHNSO device 
to detect carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and sulfur, while oxygen content was calculated by mass 
difference. The obtained material crystalline structure was characterized by X-ray diffraction 
(XRD) using a MiniFlex600 X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Japan) with Cu Kα 
(1.5406 Å) radiation. Raman spectroscopy was performed using a TriVista CRS Confocal 
TR777 spectrometer (Spectroscopy & Imaging GmbH, Germany). Scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) images of the obtained powders were taken by Helios 5 UX (Thermo 
Fischer Scientific, USA) microscope. 
Surface composition was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using 
ThermoFisher ESCALAB Xi+ instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) equipped with a 
monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source. The binding energy scale of the instrument was calibrated 
to give a binding energy at 932.6 eV for Cu 2p3/2 line of freshly etched metallic copper. A 
charge compensation system was used, with the sample surface irradiated with a flood of 
electrons to produce near-neutral surface charge. The experimental data was referenced to C 1s 
adventitious carbon. The spectra were recorded by using an X-ray beam size 650 × 100 µm, a 
pass energy of 20 eV and step size 0.1 eV.

2.4.  Electrochemical measurements
Electrochemical tests were performed in a symmetrical setup with both electrodes having equal 
mass values. A 316 stainless steel CR2032-type coin-cell casing and Whatman® glass 
microfiber grade GF/B 1 μm pore size separator was used. The chosen electrolyte was 0.5M 
K2HPO4 / 0.5M KH 2PO4 buffer solution. In our previous work, we concluded that this 
electrolyte provided great electrochemical performance and good compatibility with wood-
derived carbon-based electrode materials.20
The cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD), chronoamperometry 
(CA), and rate capability measurements were performed using a BioLogic VMP3 potentiostat-
galvanostat workstation. Long-term cycling experiments have been performed with Neware 
BTS-4000 series tester. CV was performed at 10 mV s -1 scan rate from 0 to 1 V. Initial GCD 
measurements were done with a current density of 0.1 A  g-1 from 0 to 1 V, with the cells held 
at constant current for 2 minutes at 0 and 1 V on each cycle, rate capability (GCD) was done at 
0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0 A g-1 from 0 to 1 V, CA was performed at 1 V for 1 hour to determine 
leakage current (LC), while cyclability tests were done at 2 A  g-1. The overall measurement 
sequence was as follows: 1) CV – 3 cycles; 2) GCD – 10 cycles; 3) CA; 4) rate capability – 5 
cycles at each respective current density. Long-term cycling measurements were done 
separately for 60 000 cycles.

shown by the SEM images ( Figure 1). A broad distribution of particle sizes is evidenced, 
ranging from just a few nanometers – often clustered around larger particles – to several tens 
of micrometers. For the samples activated at 800 °C (BWC-2-800 and BWC-3-800) small 
sheet-like features that may indicate locally ordered graphitic or even graphene-like domains 
are observed.

3.2. Structural and Compositional Analysis
XRD patterns (Figure 2a) show that samples treated at 700 °C exhibit a broad peak at 20° 2θ 
characteristic of disordered carbon with no clear correlation with activator amount. Samples 
activated at 800 °C, have a distinct peak at ~26.4° assigned to the (002) reflection of graphitic 
carbon, indicating the presence of partially ordered graphitic domains with limited crystallite 
size. For some of these samples, the (002) peak is asymmetric with a broadened low-angle (left) 
wing, suggesting a combination of graphitic domains with varying interlayer spacings. This 
asymmetry indicates a heterogeneous structure consisting of both disordered and more ordered 
carbon regions, likely formed during structural reorganization under activation conditions. 
Additional peaks observed in the 2θ region of 42–45° can be assigned to the (100)/(101) 
graphitic reflections, representing in-plane ordering of sp² carbon atoms. Furthermore, 
increasing the activator ratio results in a more pronounced (002) peak, indicating enhanced 
development of graphitic domains.
Minor sharp peaks may originate from trace residual Na-containing species (e.g., NaOH, NaCl, 
Na2CO3), suggesting incomplete removal of inorganic residues during post-activation washing.

(~1350 cm-1) and G (~1580 cm-1) bands of disordered graphitic carbon. The pronounced D band

4. Conclusion
In this research, birchwood charcoal was converted into high surface area activated carbons that 
are suitable for aqueous supercapacitor electrodes. A tunable pore architecture was obtained by 
varying the NaOH-to-precursor ratio and the activation temperature, ranging from 
predominately microporous structures (at 700 °C) to mixed micro-mesoporous carbons (at 
800 °C). These structural changes affected the electrochemical behavior – samples activated at 
700 °C delivered the highest specific capacitance, reaching 247 F g -1, whereas activation at 
800 °C promoted partial graphitization and marginally higher capacitance retention. A 
comparison with the commercial Kuraray YP-80F benchmark demonstrated that the 
birch‑derived carbons provide substantially higher capacitance – an improvement of 82-115% 
under identical testing conditions. Although YP-80F exhibited the best rate capability, the birch 
carbons still achieved competitive retention, particularly for the BWC‑3‑800 material. This 
highlights the trade-off between micropore-dominated structures promoting energy storage and 
mesoporous structures favoring rapid ion transport. Combination of high surface area, favorable 
pore structure, and high capacitance demonstrates that birchwood is applicable as a sustainable 
and high-value precursor for electrochemical carbon materials.
