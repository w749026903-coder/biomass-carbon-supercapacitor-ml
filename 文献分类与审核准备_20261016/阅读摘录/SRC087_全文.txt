Abstract
Biomass-derived porous carbons are promising electrode materials for sustainable
electrochemical energy storage, yet their capacitance depends on coupled precursor and
process variables that are difficult to optimize experimentally. Here, we present a
source-aware materials-informatics framework for predicting the specific capacitance of
biomass-derived carbon electrodes directly from raw precursor descriptors and processing
conditions. A literature-curated dataset comprising elemental composition, proximate
analysis, lignocellulosic structure, and activation variables was evaluated with support
vector regression (SVR), Random Forest (RF), a deep neural network (DNN), LightGBM,
and XGBoost, together with an ensemble strategy, across seven feature combinations.
XGBoost delivered the strongest test performance, reaching R2 = 0.948 for the most
informative feature block. Beyond model ranking, the study emphasizes validation realism
by contrasting conventional random splits with source-aware evaluation, showing that
random partitions can substantially overestimate apparent generalization. Across models,
current density, activation temperature, holding time, ash content, and volatile matter
emerged as the most influential predictors. The resulting workflow provides practical
guidance for precursor screening, process-window definition, and data-driven design of
biomass-derived supercapacitor electrodes. The guidance applies within the precursor and
process regime represented by the training corpus; experimental verification is
recommended for excursions beyond it.
1 Introduction
The escalating global demand for efficient energy storage technologies, driven by the increasing
emphasis on renewable energy and the urgent need to address climate change, has positioned super-
capacitors as increasingly important devices in energy storage technologies. They are used in many
areas such as electric vehicles, renewable energy storage systems and portable electronics due to
their high power density, long cycle life and fast charge/discharge capabilities [1, 2]. One of the main
parameters determining the performance of supercapacitors is capacitance, which is directly related
to the surface area, porosity and chemical structure of the electrode materials. Traditionally, carbon-
based materials such as activated carbon have been preferred as electrodes. However, in terms of
sustainability and cost-effectiveness, the use of agricultural biomass waste as carbon electrode precur-
sors is becoming increasingly popular [3–5]. Activated carbon produced from biomass is important
both in terms of environmental benefits and contribution to the circular economy. These materials
are obtained by converting agricultural wastes such as rice husk, coconut shell, apricot kernel shell
and bamboo into high surface area carbons through carbonization and activation processes [6]. This
approach both reduces costs and offers an innovative solution in waste management. However, the
inherent variability and complex composition of biomass present challenges in consistently producing
materials with predictable performance characteristics. In addition, the porous structure and chem-
ical properties of biomass-derived activated carbons can be optimized to increase supercapacitors’

trode family for advanced supercapacitor architectures, including metal-ion hybrid configurations
that emphasise sustainability alongside electrochemical performance [9]. Factors affecting super-
capacitor performance are related to biomass-derived carbon electrodes. Factors such as material
composition, process parameters, electrolyte type, electrode design, and operating conditions affect
performance [10,11]. In addition, industrial characteristics such as moisture, volatile matter, and ash
content can also affect performance because they affect the purity and pore structure of the carbon
material. Process parameters such as activation temperature, ramp rate, and current density affect
performance by determining the pore structure and surface area of the carbon. For example, higher
activation temperatures can result in better pore development, but excessive temperatures can lead
to material degradation [ 12]. Optimizing these numerous interlinked variables through traditional
experimental approaches can be a complex, time-consuming, and resource-intensive endeavor [13].
The use of machine learning (ML) methods in materials science and especially to optimize the
performance of biomass-based carbon electrodes in supercapacitors and generally in nanomaterials
development has gained significant attention in recent years [6]. Jamaluddin et al. investigated
the impact of surface area, pore volume, and experimental parameters (electrolyte concentration,
voltage window, current density) on the capacitance of biomass-based carbon electrodes using ML
models (LR, M5-Rules, RT, MLP), reporting that the MLP model achieved the highest performance
(R=0.871, MAE=45.069 F g −1) and that surface area and pore volume had significant effects [14].
Similarly, Liao et al. predicted the specific capacitance of N-doped biochar using Random Forest
and XGBoost models, reporting strong predictive performance (test R2 values of approximately 0.95
and 0.96, respectively). They further showed that the N-6 group contributed strongly to capaci-
tance, reaching approximately 303 F g −1 under favorable pore and graphitization conditions [15].
Lu et al. estimated the energy and power density of biomass-based carbon supercapacitors using
XGBoost, LightGBM, and Deep Learning Neural Network (DNN) models, revealing that biomass
feedstock composition and activation conditions significantly influence these parameters [16]. Shri-
vas and Dubay employed Random Forest, Decision Tree, Linear Regression, and XGBoost models to
optimize the performance of activated carbon electrodes, finding that Random Forest performed best
and that surface area, nitrogen doping, and pore volume significantly affected capacitance [17]. Zhu
et al. predicted the capacitance of carbon-based supercapacitors using linear regression, Lasso, and
artificial neural network (ANN) methods [18]. Ghosh et al. combined ML with experimental valida-
tion to predict and optimize the specific capacitance of porous carbon materials (PCMs), introducing
a key eigenvalue drop-point screening approach that provides an innovative strategy for advancing
energy storage materials technology [19]. Complementary contributions operating in the surface-
area-driven regime have shown how heteroatom configuration and pore-size features can be linked to
capacitance through data-driven models, using post-synthesis descriptors such as micro- and meso-
pore surface areas [20]. These studies highlight the potential of ML to predict supercapacitor perfor-
mance and accelerate material optimization. Broader synthesis efforts have catalogued the AI model
landscape for supercapacitor performance prediction across MXenes, carbon-based materials, metal
oxides, and metal–organic frameworks, and they emphasise the role of leakage-safe validation and
harmonised electrochemical testing conditions when interpreting reported metrics [21]. Comparable
benchmarking efforts in adjacent nanomaterial domains, such as the comparative evaluation of ML
classifiers for the electrical conductivity behavior of carbon nanotube-doped polymer nanocompos-
ite films, reinforce the importance of systematic cross-model assessment when predictive descriptors
and process variables are coupled [22]. Recent contributions further show that performance gains
continue to be driven by careful control of pore architecture, porous-carbon fabrication strategy,
and device-level design choices, as illustrated by pore-engineered okra-derived activated carbons,
template-fabricated porous carbons for high-performance electrodes, wood-inspired hydrogel elec-
trolytes for flexible supercapacitors, and nitrogen-doped activated carbons derived from mangosteen-
shell biomass for high-performance supercapacitor electrodes [23–26]. Related studies also support
data-driven and optimization-focused pathways for biomass-derived carbon development, includ-
ing sustainable biochar process design and machine-learning-assisted activated-carbon optimization
from agricultural residues [27,28]. Comprehensive reviews further connect the biomass-composition–
activation–porosity–capacitance chain and highlight the emerging role of ML tools alongside cost
and life-cycle considerations for scalable biochar-derived electrode development [29].
In the literature, many studies on optimizing the supercapacitor performance of biomass-based
carbon electrodes with machine learning examine the effect of surface area, pore volume, and ex-
perimental parameters on capacitance [30, 31]. These input features often represent intermediate,
experimentally-derived characteristics of the processed carbon material. However, these studies have
generally focused on limited combinations of properties or analyzed specific biomass types. In this

To sharpen the scientific scope and practical relevance of this work, the study is guided by four
research questions:
• RQ1: Which feature groups provide robust predictive power when only raw biomass descriptors
and process parameters are available?
• RQ2: Which model family offers the best trade-off between accuracy and stability across feature
combinations?
• RQ3: Which parameters are consistently influential across model families and feature subsets?
• RQ4: How can the resulting model insights be translated into actionable guidance for sustain-
able electrode design?
These questions position the manuscript beyond a simple model comparison and frame it as
a decision-support study for early-stage material screening. More specifically, the novelty of the
present work is not claimed as a mere performance increment on an inherited dataset; rather, it lies
in combining five contribution axes that are directly relevant to source-aware materials informatics
for energy-storage electrodes: source-aware validation to expose optimistic bias, feature-block rea-
soning to distinguish which raw-material descriptors are genuinely actionable, cross-model consensus
to reduce dependence on a single algorithm narrative, deployment-oriented interpretation that links
prediction outputs to laboratory screening logic, and an explicit positioning against the reference
study in terms of external validity and design support. This framing is intended to convert a pre-
dictive benchmark into a more reusable materials-selection and process-window guidance workflow.
Operationally, this framing is now supported by the leave-one-reference-out validation pipeline, a
repeated-split stability envelope with paired significance testing, an independent SHAP check of
feature contributions and their directional signs, and a transparent statement of the compute cost
associated with each model family; together, these ingredients turn the novelty claim from a stylistic
contrast with existing benchmarks into a set of concrete artefacts that a downstream user can inspect
and reuse.

2.1 Validation and Robustness Pipeline
To ensure methodological robustness and realistic deployment relevance, we define a validation
pipeline that structures the study from data preparation to model assessment and practical in-
terpretation.
1. Data integrity and split protocol: enforce duplicate and near-duplicate checks, then report
both random split and group-aware split performance (grouped by literature reference and
biomass type).
2. Model stability analysis: evaluate metric variability with repeated runs and bootstrap
confidence intervals for R2, MAE, and RMSE.
3. Feature-group ablation: quantify what is gained by each feature block (elemental, proxi-
mate, structural, process) and report performance-cost trade-offs.
4. Out-of-distribution stress testing: hold out one biomass family or one reference source at
a time to emulate unseen material scenarios.
5. Actionable design mapping: convert model insight into practical process windows (e.g.,
AT, HT, current density ranges) for experimental prioritization.

agricultural biomass wastes used as precursors for carbon electrodes in supercapacitors and includes
herbaceous plants, fruit and nutshell residues, and agricultural straws. The preparation routes in
the source studies typically involve two primary steps: carbonization and activation. Carbonization
is performed under controlled, oxygen-limited conditions, and activation is generally carried out
using chemical activators such as H 3PO4, NaOH, ZnCl 2, and KOH. The raw spreadsheet contains
212 records in the main data table; after removing rows with incomplete feature information, 195
samples were retained for model development.
Because the compiled samples originate from studies that used different electrolytes (predom-
inantly H 3PO4, NaOH, ZnCl 2, and KOH), different voltage windows, and different measurement
protocols, cross-source consistency was addressed at three levels. First, all target values were ex-
pressed in the common unit of specific capacitance (F g−1) as reported in the source dataset, and rows
with unit ambiguity or missing feature values were dropped rather than imputed, reducing the 212
raw records to the 195 used for modelling. Second, the current density (Cond, A g −1) was explicitly
retained as a modelled input so that its variation across sources is absorbed by the predictors rather
than left as hidden noise. Third, electrolyte identity, voltage window, and cell geometry were kept as
unmodelled contextual variation: they cannot be normalised onto a common numerical scale across
sources without introducing subjective encodings, and their residual influence is precisely what the
leave-one-reference-out evaluation reported in Section 3.1 is designed to expose. This design isolates
a precursor-plus-process feature space that is genuinely available before deep electrochemical char-
acterisation, while keeping the effect of unmodelled electrochemical context transparent through the
validation protocol.
To make dataset composition transparent, the cleaned corpus is summarised along three axes
that condition every downstream model. The 195 usable rows come from 17 independent literature
publications and represent 16 distinct biomass precursors that fall into 4 higher-level biomass families
(herbaceous residues, fruit/nut residues, woody/lignified biomass, and other precursors); Figure 3
shows how samples are distributed across these families, with herbaceous residues (n=84) and other
biomass (n=47) being the most represented and fruit/nut residues and woody/lignified biomass
each contributing 32 samples. Missing values were handled by row-wise deletion of any record with
incomplete feature information, without imputation, in order to keep the fitted models faithful to
actually reported data rather than to interpolated values. Because the target is a continuous specific-
capacitance value rather than a class label, class imbalance in the categorical sense does not apply;
the relevant analogue is the shape of the target distribution, which spans roughly 80–420 F g −1
with a mean of 233.5 F g −1 and a median of 229.0 F g −1 (Figure 4). Process-variable coverage
(Figure 5) is uneven across sources, with strong concentration of the reported heating ramp rates,
holding times, and current densities near the modal experimental settings of the field, so the model
comparison must be read as conditioned on these dominant operating regions.
2.2.1 Dataset Variables and Statistical Overview The dataset encompasses variables categorized
into material composition features, process-related parameters, and the target performance metric.
Material Composition Variables. The intrinsic properties of the materials were characterized
by their elemental and proximate analysis. Elemental composition features included carbon (C, %),
nitrogen (N, %), hydrogen (H, %), and oxygen (O, %). Variables from proximate analysis were
moisture content (M, %), volatile matter (VM, %), ash content (Ash, %), and fixed carbon (FC, %).
Furthermore, the lignocellulosic composition was detailed through cellulose (Cel, %), hemicellulose
(Hem, %), and lignin (Lig, %) content.
Processing Parameters. Key experimental conditions manipulated during the material synthesis
were included as input parameters. These comprised the activation temperature (AT, °C), heating
ramp rate (RT, °C/min), holding time (HT, min), the impregnation ratio (IR), and the current
density (Cond, A g −1).
Target Parameter. The primary output variable for prediction in this study was the specific
capacitance (Cap, F g −1), which quantifies the electrochemical performance of the material.
A statistical summary of all features within the dataset is provided in Table 4.

cleaned dataset structure. The correlation landscape highlights monotonic relationships among key
process and composition variables, while the biomass-family coverage plot clarifies how samples are
distributed across precursor categories.

2.4.2 Random Forest (RF) Random Forest is an ensemble learning method based on constructing
a multitude of decision trees during training. It outputs the mean prediction (regression) of the indi-
vidual trees. RF is known for its ability to reduce variance and mitigate overfitting by incorporating
randomness in feature selection and data sampling for each tree [35, 36].
2.4.3 Deep Neural Network (DNN) A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) model, a class of feedforward
artificial neural network, was implemented for capacitance prediction, given its capability to model
complex non-linear relationships [37]. The DNN architecture was designed with an input layer
corresponding to the maximum number of input features considered in this study (up to 16 features
for the most comprehensive combination), followed by four hidden layers with 256, 128, 64, and 32
neurons, respectively. The output layer comprised a single neuron for predicting the capacitance.
The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) was utilized as the activation function for the hidden layers due
to its effectiveness in preventing vanishing gradients, while a linear activation function was used
for the output layer suitable for regression tasks. The Adam optimizer, an adaptive learning rate
optimization algorithm, was employed with a learning rate of 0.001. Dropout was incorporated
during training as a regularization technique to prevent overfitting. The model was trained for 200
epochs with an early stopping criterion based on validation loss to ensure generalization.
2.4.4 LightGBM Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), known for its efficiency and high
performance, was utilized. GridSearchCV was employed to identify the optimal hyperparameters.
The selected parameters included 500 estimators (number of decision trees), 31 leaves per tree (con-
trolling complexity), a max
depth of -1 (allowing trees to grow deeply), and a learning rate of 0.1 [38].
2.4.5 XGBoost Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) was also included, configured with hyper-
parameters similar to those used for LightGBM, determined after extensive experimentation and
tuned using GridSearchCV. These parameters were a learning rate of 0.1, max depth of 3, nestimators
of 500, and subsample ratio of 0.8 [39].
2.4.6 Ensemble Model An ensemble model was created by averaging the predictions of the Light-
GBM and XGBoost models. Ensemble methods often lead to better generalization performance by
reducing variance and model bias compared to individual models. This approach aimed to leverage
the distinct strengths of both gradient boosting models to potentially achieve improved predictive
performance, as will be discussed in the model evaluation section [40].
2.5 Model Training and Evaluation
The primary model-comparison workflow used a training (80%) and testing (20%) split. Before
training, the input features were standardised using scaling parameters fit exclusively on the train-
ing partition, which were then applied to the test partition without refitting so that no information
from the held-out samples could influence the scaler. In addition, a protocol-sensitivity analysis (re-
ported in Section 3.1) was performed to compare random-split and group-based validation behavior
under a controlled surrogate model. The models were trained on the scaled training data and subse-
quently evaluated on the scaled unseen test data to assess their generalization capability. To identify
the optimal hyperparameters for each model (excluding the DNN, which had a manually tuned ar-
chitecture as described previously), GridSearchCV was employed, utilizing k-fold cross-validation on
the training set [41]. This systematic search helps in finding the parameter combination that yields
the best model performance. Three standard performance metrics were used for evaluation: Coeffi-
cient of Determination (R 2), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE).
Following the approach in similar studies, MAE was particularly used as the primary metric to guide
the hyperparameter selection process during GridSearchCV, often due to its robustness to outliers
and direct interpretability. The three metrics are defined by equations (1)–(3):

dropout regularisation with validation-loss-based early stopping over 200 maximum epochs, so the
training loop halts as soon as the held-out validation loss stops decreasing. The final hyperparame-
ters selected for each model are reported in the preceding model-specific paragraphs.
Given that the sample-to-feature ratio is modest (195 samples with up to 17 candidate inputs),
overfitting was controlled at several complementary layers. Model complexity was regulated through
model-specific regularisation: shrinkage and column subsampling in XGBoost, leaf-wise growth con-
trol in LightGBM, tree count and depth bounds in Random Forest, kernel and ε-margin tuning in
SVR, and dropout combined with validation-loss early stopping in the DNN. The GridSearchCV
inner cross-validation loop was executed strictly within the training partition, so hyperparameter
choices never saw test-set data. Training and test metrics are reported together for every model and
every feature combination, keeping the train–test performance gap directly inspectable rather than
hidden inside a single test score. The protocol-sensitivity analysis reported in Section 3.1 further ex-
poses the residual optimism of random splitting under a controlled surrogate, providing an external
stress test that complements the within-training regularisation strategy.
All data preprocessing, model implementation, training, and evaluation were conducted using the
Python programming language (version 3.10). Core libraries utilized included Scikit-learn for general
machine learning tasks and model evaluation, Pandas for data manipulation, NumPy for numerical
operations, and specific libraries for the individual models such as TensorFlow/Keras for the DNN,
LightGBM for the LightGBM model, and XGBoost for the XGBoost model. Data visualizations
were generated using Matplotlib and Seaborn [42].

3.1 Validation-Protocol Effect on Generalization
To quantify how evaluation design affects apparent model performance, we conducted a split-protocol
sensitivity analysis using a parsimonious ridge-regression surrogate trained on the full numerical
feature set. This analysis complements the main model-comparison results by isolating the impact
of validation protocol under controlled model complexity.
Two protocols were evaluated: (i) repeated random 80/20 splits (250 repetitions) and (ii) group-
based validation using leave-one-reference-out splits, where each literature reference was held out in
turn. The cleaned dataset comprises 17 unique literature references covering 16 individual biomass
materials grouped into 4 higher-level biomass families (herbaceous residues, fruit/nut residues,
woody/lignified biomass, and other precursors), and each of the 17 references was used exactly once
as the held-out test fold while the remaining 16 references collectively formed the training fold. Fold
sizes therefore vary by source, ranging from 2 to 32 samples with a median around 8, and no row
from a held-out reference contributes to fitting the feature scaler, to the ridge coefficients, or to
any downstream diagnostic within that fold. This grouping construction removes the within-source
leakage that would otherwise arise if random splits placed samples from the same publication in both
the training and the test partitions, and it therefore provides a genuinely out-of-source estimate of
generalisation. Figure 7 summarises the full source-aware validation pipeline alongside the parallel
random-split protocol, making the leakage-prevention stages of the workflow directly inspectable.
For each protocol, R2, MAE, and RMSE were computed, and uncertainty intervals were estimated
(empirical 95% intervals for repeated random splits and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for group-
based predictions). Importantly, this protocol-comparison experiment uses a deliberately simple
ridge-regression surrogate to isolate the effect of validation design itself; therefore, the absolute metric
values here are not expected to match the best-performing XGBoost/LightGBM scores reported later
for the main model-comparison experiments.

3.2 Mechanistic Interpretation and Feature-Combination Rationale
Although the present study is data-driven, the dominant variables identified by the models are also
physically plausible from a supercapacitor design perspective. Current density is repeatedly ranked
first because it strongly affects ion-transport and charge-transfer timescales during electrochemical
testing; higher current densities shorten the effective charging window and often reduce the measured
specific capacitance when pore accessibility is kinetically limited. Activation temperature acts as a
structural-development variable: moderate increases can promote pore generation and surface-area
growth, whereas excessively severe activation may widen or collapse useful pore networks and degrade
capacitance. Holding time plays a similar process-window role by controlling the extent of burn-off
and activation completeness. Ash content is mechanistically meaningful because higher ash fractions
generally indicate more inorganic residue, which can reduce effective carbon purity and interfere
with desirable porous carbon formation. Volatile matter is likewise informative because it reflects
precursor decomposition behavior and can indirectly signal how readily the biomass matrix develops
pore-forming channels during thermal conversion.
These qualitative connections can be sharpened at the electrochemical level. At low current
densities, the diffusion time available for electrolyte ions to reach interior micropores is comparable to
or longer than the effective double-layer formation timescale, so the measured capacitance approaches
the electrode’s intrinsic value; at high current densities, this window narrows, IR drop grows relative
to the useful voltage window, and the measured capacitance collapses toward a rate-limited surface
response. Activation temperature and holding time together control where the precursor sits along
the classical burn-off–surface-area curve: too low, and pore volume remains under-developed; too
high or too long, and micropore walls coalesce into wider mesopores that lower the specific-surface
contribution. Ash content operates as an inverse purity marker, since inorganic residue does not
participate in double-layer storage and can even block ion pathways into otherwise active micropores.
Volatile matter, in turn, reflects the fraction of the precursor that leaves the matrix during pyrolysis
and therefore signals how much internal void volume can be templated into the final carbonaceous
framework. The convergence of the models onto these particular variables is therefore not accidental:
each of them is a directly interpretable handle on either the ion-transport regime or the pore-

A compact rationale also emerges for the observed differences among feature combinations.
The strong performance of G2 (industrial analysis + processing parameters) is reasonable because
proximate-analysis descriptors such as ash, volatile matter, moisture, and fixed carbon directly sum-
marize carbon yield potential, mineral residue burden, and thermal decomposition behavior, while
the process variables determine how those precursor tendencies are translated into final pore archi-
tecture and electrochemical response. By contrast, the structural-composition block alone is weaker
because cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin differences are partially homogenized during carboniza-
tion; their influence is real but less direct once the precursor is converted into a carbonaceous network.
In this sense, proximate-analysis variables and process settings form a more actionable feature block
for smart energy-storage carbon design, whereas structural descriptors play a secondary, context-
dependent role.
3.3 Limitations and Deployment Boundary Conditions
Several limitations should be recognized when interpreting the present results. First, the dataset is
literature-derived and contains 195 samples, which is adequate for comparative benchmarking but
still limited for claiming universal generalization across all biomass families, activation chemistries,
and testing protocols. Second, electrolyte identity and voltage-window settings are known to influ-
ence measured capacitance, as recently demonstrated in dedicated electrolyte-optimization studies
on transition-metal-oxide supercapacitor electrodes [43]; yet these variables were not available as
consistently harmonized descriptors in the adopted dataset and therefore could not be incorporated
as standardized predictors in the present modeling pipeline. The reported models should thus be
interpreted as design-support tools conditioned on the information that is reliably available across
sources rather than as complete electrochemical surrogates. Third, while the source-aware validation
analysis improves realism, the study remains computational and does not replace experimental confir-
mation for newly proposed precursor–process combinations. Concretely, the leave-one-reference-out
estimate reported in Section 3.1 shows a substantial drop in R2 relative to the within-corpus random-
split scores, so the reported accuracy should not be projected onto a completely new biomass family
that is absent from the training corpus; predictions for such precursors are intended as prioritisation
signals for experimental verification rather than as standalone forecasts. These boundary conditions
should be kept explicit when translating model outputs into laboratory decisions.
3.4 Research-Question-Centered Synthesis
To preserve a clear question-to-evidence flow, we map each research question to direct evidence from
the reported results.
• RQ1 (feature groups): Results across seven combinations indicate that process parameters
and proximate-analysis variables provide the strongest predictive signal among raw-material-
level descriptors.
• RQ2 (model family): Gradient-boosting methods, especially XGBoost, consistently offer the
most favorable accuracy–stability balance across combinations, while split-protocol sensitivity
analysis confirms that source-aware validation is necessary for realistic generalization assess-
ment.
• RQ3 (dominant parameters): Feature-importance analyses converge on current density,
activation temperature, holding time, ash content, and volatile matter as repeatedly influential
variables.
• RQ4 (actionable guidance): The combined model-comparison and feature-priority results
support a practical screening logic in which researchers prioritize process-condition optimization
before expensive deep characterization campaigns.

practically usable for early-stage screening of biomass-derived carbon electrodes. By systematically
evaluating multiple machine learning models across seven feature combinations, the work reframes

Across the benchmark experiments, XGBoost consistently delivered the strongest predictive per-

analysis variables with process settings, reaching a test-set R2 of 0.948, an MAE of 11 .796 F g −1,

optimistic than source-held-out evaluation, indicating that realistic reporting for literature-derived
smart-material datasets must go beyond conventional random-split accuracy. Because the leave-
one-reference-out estimate drops substantially below the random-split scores, the reported accuracy
should be read as a within-corpus benchmark rather than as a guarantee for unseen biomass families
or novel process regions, whose behaviour remains to be experimentally validated.

window definition. Across models, current density, activation temperature, holding time, ash content,
and volatile matter repeatedly emerged as the dominant variables. The superior performance of the
G2 feature block indicates that proximate-analysis descriptors combined with process parameters
provide the most decision-relevant view of capacitance behavior. In practical terms, this means that
process-condition tuning and precursor-quality screening should be prioritized before expensive deep
characterization campaigns when exploring new biomass-derived carbons.
The manuscript contributes a validation-aware and interpretation-oriented framework for data-
driven development of smart energy-storage materials. Future work should extend the dataset with
harmonized electrolyte and voltage-window metadata, expand out-of-source testing across new pre-
cursor families, and experimentally verify model-guided precursor–process candidates so that com-
putational screening can be translated into robust smart-material design rules. A natural extension
of the same framework is a multi-metric prediction layer that jointly forecasts specific capacitance,
energy density, power density, and cyclic stability from the same precursor-plus-process feature space.
The seven-combination structure and the source-aware validation protocol translate directly to that
setting; the main open challenge is harmonising the reporting conventions of the additional perfor-
mance metrics across literature sources so that a multi-output model can be trained without silent
leakage.
