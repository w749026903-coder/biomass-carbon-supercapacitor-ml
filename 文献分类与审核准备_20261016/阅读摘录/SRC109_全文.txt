ABSTRACT
Selective ion separation is essential for effective water treatment and resource recovery. Conventional techniques, such as 
adsorption, coagulation/flocculation, and membrane technologies, suffer from low selectivity, substantial cost, and high energy 
requirements. Alternatively, ion separation using capacitive deionization (CDI) has shown strong potential for energy efficiency, 
cost‐effectiveness, environmental sustainability, and selective removal. However, traditional carbon electrodes used in CDI have 
limitations for selective separations. MXene‐based electrodes present an attractive alternative for CDI due to their exceptional 
electrochemical properties. Nevertheless, MXenes are typically unstable over extended cycles, and to achieve ion selectivity, 
controlled interlayer spacing adjustment is required. Methods such as hybridization and functionalization can improve the 
selectivity properties of MXenes by tailoring their interlayer spacing. Several studies have reported the use of MXenes as CDI 
electrodes; however, most focus on brackish water desalination, while selective ion separation is rarely addressed. This review 
presents an in‐depth analysis of the role of modified MXene electrodes in advancing ion‐selective CDI technologies, exploring 
various strategies to achieve the desired selectivity. Moreover, it highlights critical challenges that hinder scalability and 
practical implementation. Finally, the review showcases the strategic roadmap for future opportunities toward next‐generation 
water purification and resource recovery solutions.

Introduction
Water scarcity and wastewater generation have become critical 
challenges of the 21st century due to increasing population and 
industrial growth [ 1, 2 ]. Projections indicate that global water 
demand may rise by over 50% in the coming decades, whereas 
nearly one‐fourth of major urban centers are already under sig -
nificant water stress [ 3 ]. Consequently, the need for water recla -
mation and resource recovery is growing rapidly to bridge the 
increasing gap between water demand and supply. Similarly,

containing recoverable water, energy, and high‐value nutrients.
To meet the increasing water demand, desalination technologies, 
including reverse osmosis (RO), membrane distillation, and 
electrodialysis, are found to be more efficient technologies in 
comparison with others [ 5–7 ]. However, these technologies often 
suffer from high energy consumption required for pumping or

2.1

Electrical Double‐Layer (EDL) Model
In carbon‐based CDI, the electrosorption is largely based on the 
electrical double‐layer (EDL) mechanism. EDLs are formed near 
the interface of the highly porous carbonaceous electrode ma -
terial and water, in which charges can be stored. Alternatively, 
when using pseudocapacitive materials, ion storage is facilitated 
by either a reversible surface redox mechanism or an ion de/ 
intercalation mechanism in the case of crystals or multilayered 
materials (Section 2.2 ).
A common EDL concept is depicted in Figure 2 by taking the 
positive electrode with the negative ions. When a potential 
difference is applied across the electrodes (only one electrode is 
shown for matters of simplicity), the ions present in the water 
move toward the electrodes having opposite polarities and start 
forming EDLs on the available surfaces. This explains why, in 
CDI, materials with high surface areas are preferred because 
they provide a larger surface for the formation of larger 
EDLs [ 24 ].

larger than the thickness of the EDL, we can assume that the

2.2

Pseudocapacitive Adsorption
For pseudocapacitive electrodes, ion storage is facilitated mainly 
by the ion de/intercalation mechanism. Here, the ions are 
intercalated into the crystal lattice (well‐defined channels) of 
layered crystals or nanocrystals, and intercalated easily when a 
reverse potential is applied. Because this is also a physical 
phenomenon like EDL, the electrode’s integrity is maintained 
over the cycle. However, there could be a lattice expansion/ 
contraction over time, which could be avoided by designing a 
highly stable crystal.
The ion storage capacity (specific capacitance ( C
s
)) is an impor -
tant parameter in the CDI system, which gives an idea about how 
many ions could be stored by a particular electrode per unit mass. 
Usually, it is measured in capacitance per gram of active mass 
either in (F g
− 1
) or (mAh g
− 1
). Electrochemical analyses such as 
cyclic voltammetry (CV), electrochemical impedance spectros -
copy, and galvanostatic charge‐discharge (GCD) can give valu -
able information regarding the electrosorption capacity and 
mechanism of the electrode material and its selectivity toward a 
particular ion. Particularly for intercalation materials, CV gives 
good information for the selection of an active electrode material 
for a particular ion removal. The clue is that a higher cathodic 
peak potential associated with the intercalation of a particular ion 
is directly correlated with the preferred intercalation of that ion 
toward the electrode [ 29, 30 ].
In practice, the ion storage process is not fully governed by a single 
mechanism; rather, it often involves contributions from both 
pseudocapacitive/intercalation and EDL formation. To differen -
tiate between capacitive and pseudocapacitive‐controlled contri -

Synthesis of MXene
The origin of MXene lies in the etching of MAX phases, which 
are metal carbides and nitrides with a general formula M
n + 1
AX
n 
where M is a D‐block metal, A is the layered group (A‐layer 
such as Al or Si), and X is carbon (C) or nitrogen (N) [ 40, 41 ]. 
MAX phases can be considered as 2D materials connected by 
M–A layers. Here, the M–A bond is relatively weaker than the 
M–X bond, and hence, the A‐layer can be removed by a proper 
etching method. This process results in vacant layers in the 
MXene material with a stronger M–X bond, making it suitable 
for several applications, including ion sieving, water desalina -
tion, and seawater splitting [ 42–44 ].

3.1.3
|
Delamination
After the etching process, the prepared MXene tends to restack 
due to the attraction of van der Waals forces [ 61 ]. This 
restacking phenomenon in MXene limits the interlayer spacing 
and, consequently, intercalating activity and SAC of prepared 
electrodes. To avoid this, delamination is frequently used to 
expand interlayer spacing using organic solvents. Delamination 
is also used to get individual MXene sheets from multilayer 
MXene.
Dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO) is amongst the first delaminators 
employed for Ti
3
C
2
T
x 
delamination [ 62 ]. Although relatively 
nontoxic, it could penetrate the skin and act as a carrier of dis -
solved substances, and hence it should be used in ventilated areas 
with proper personal protective equipment. In the delamination 
process, organic solvents are drawn into stacked MXene for a few 
hours, which weakens the van der Waals attractive forces and 
regenerates proper interlayer spaces [ 63 ]. Other organic solvents 
used in delamination are tetrabutylammonium hydroxide 
(TBAOH) and lithium chloride [ 64 ]. These solvents and salts are 
comparatively less toxic. However, they are strong bases and 
should be treated with safety protocols. Apart from inserting 
organic solvents, sonication with deionized water is also an option 
for further delamination and size reduction. However, it could 
add significant defects to the MXene structure [ 65 ].
Although MXene shows promise as an electrode material for 
CDI, its practical application is limited by structural instability 
during cycling. A common strategy to mitigate MXene 
restacking is hybridization with materials such as graphene 
oxide (GO) or reduced graphene oxide (rGO). Table 1 summa -
rizes recent progress in engineering MXene CDI electrodes, 
highlighting strategies such as doping, hybridization, and 
functionalization that have been employed to achieve superior 
CDI performance.
3.2
|
Chemical Functionalization and Surface 
Tuning for Enhanced CDI Performance
Several MXene modification strategies can be employed not 
only to tailor MXene properties based on process requirements, 
but also to stabilize the MXene's chemical reactivity. A 
comprehensive schematic illustration of MXene modification, 
including chemical functionalization, hybridization and nano -
compositing, interlayer tuning, and surface terminations, is 
presented in Figure 4 . The surface of MXene, terminated with 
groups such as –O, –OH, and –F, provides a versatile platform 
for precise chemical modification. These functionalization 
strategies are essential for tailoring MXene’s properties, such as 
its interlayer spacing, electrical conductivity, and specific af -
finity for target ions, to enhance its performance in applications 
such as selective separation and energy storage. Common 
methods for MXene surface tuning include direct chemical 
modification of its native –O, –OH, and –F terminations and 
grafting of functional molecules or polymers. Approaches, such 
as silanization or polymer intercalation, are employed to sys -
tematically tailor interlayer spacing, stability, and surface 
chemistry for enhanced performance.
Challenges such as surface oxidation and re‐stacking in aqueous 
environments limit their performance. Several studies have 
explored innovative strategies to enhance the stability and per -
formance of MXene electrodes in CDI applications. These 
strategies include surface modifications, for example, via an 
alkali treatment followed by thermal annealing [ 84 ], formation 
of binder‐free composites that exhibit resistance to surface 
oxidation after 30 desalination cycles [ 85 ], and structural engi -
neering, with a focus on alkali‐induced strategies to produce 
wrinkled surfaces [ 86 ], which collectively contribute to 
improved electrochemical stability and desalination efficiency.
To break the brackishwater limitation of traditional CDI, Yang

General Selectivity Criteria and Strategies
MXenes can be hybridized with other dimensional materials 
and engineered for multiple removal mechanisms, such as ion 
intercalation and modified redox centers. Beyond these, selec -
tivity can be enabled by tailoring pore sizes and surface charge 
chemistry. This section will focus on general selectivity criteria 
and strategies, including electrode properties, ion characteris -
tics, the use of ion exchange membranes, and the tuning of 
operational parameters such as voltage, pH, and flow rate.
4.1
|
Properties of Ions and Electrodes
Several studies support that ionic properties such as hydrated 
radius, ionic charge, and valence significantly affect the selec -
tive and sequential removal of ions from a multi‐ionic solution 
in CDI [ 80, 93–95 ]. Morphological features of electrodes, such 
as pore size, pore size distribution, and pore shape, as well as 
intrinsic charge properties of the electrode, are among the key 
factors in achieving such an ion selectivity [ 18 ]. The combina -
tion of these features and properties induces several removal 
mechanisms that can be categorized as physical (e.g., size 
exclusion via interlayer spacing and electrostatic affinity via 
tuning terminal groups) and chemical mechanisms (affinity via 
surface functionalization).
In a nanocomposite electrode with tight mesopores, the ion‐ 
sieving effect, illustrated in Figure 5A , governs selectivity. Ions 
with smaller hydrated radii are selectively adsorbed, as the 
restrictive pore architecture physically excludes larger hydrated 
ions from accessing the internal surface area [ 96–98 ]. For 
instance, in the study by Gabelich et al. [ 99 ], ion selectivity in 
carbon aerogel was governed by a combination of hydrated 
radius and pore size constraints. The electrodes exhibited higher 
selectivity for monovalent ions (e.g., Na
+
) over divalent ions 
(e.g., Ca
2 +

inherent capacitive and intercalation mechanisms.

Tuning Operational Parameters
Although material engineering is fundamental to achieving ion 
selectivity, the performance of CDI systems is equally governed 
by operational parameters. The deliberate tuning of variables, 
including cell voltage, current density, feed concentration, flow 
rate, and pH, provides a complementary and essential strategy 
for optimizing selective separation.
4.3.1
|

Voltage Change
Generally, increasing the cell voltage or current density reduces 
the selectivity of a target ion over a competing ion. In the 
absence of an applied voltage, the intrinsic selectivity of an 
electrode material is primarily governed by the affinity between 
the ion and the electrode surface. However, as the voltage in -
creases, ions with higher mobility tend to be selectively adsor -
bed, thereby compromising the selectivity of the target ion. Li 
et al. [ 77 ] investigated the effect of voltage on the selectivity 
between divalent and monovalent ions using TiVCT

Their study revealed that the selectivity ratio of Ca

/Na

from 1.0 to 1.6 V. Despite this reduction in selectivity, the SAC 
was found to be higher, consistent with trends reported in 
existing literature [ 115, 116 ]. Similarly, Jiang et al. [ 117 ] inves -
tigated the impact of voltage on ion selectivity, revealing that the 
selectivity for Li
+
over Mg
2 +
decreased significantly from 18.7 to 
6.6 as the applied voltage increased from 0.6 to 1.2 V. Thus, 
applied voltage/current density could be manipulated to 
enhance selectivity toward a certain ion.
4.3.3
|

Flow rate can positively or negatively influence selectivity, 
depending on the specific conditions. Jiang et al. [ 117 ] investi -

the influence of flow rate on ion selectivity. For the separation

+

mass transport.
In contrast, Xu et al. [ 118 ] reported the effect of flow rate on

efficient delivery of ions to the electrode surface. Therefore, the 
dependence of both selectivity and adsorption performance on

investigation and optimization.

Challenges in MXene‐Based Electrodes in CDI
Besides the advantages of the MXene electrode over common 
carbon‐based electrodes, MXene faces several challenges that 
need to be addressed for wider application in the field of CDI. 
The next sections list some challenges associated with MXene‐ 
based electrodes.
5.1
|
Material Stability and Durability
Delaminated MXene flakes are prone to oxidation when 
exposed to air and water. An oxidized layer on the MXene 
surface can significantly reduce the adsorption capacity and 
expected selectivity by decreasing the electrical conductivity 
and altering the oxygen groups [ 120 ]. When water adsorbs onto 
the hydrophilic MXene surface, it can trigger hydrolysis re -
actions, resulting in the formation of metal hydroxides and the 
oxidation of the transition metal [ 121 ]. The nucleophilic attack 
of water initiates the oxidation of high‐valent metal atoms. The 
process involves complex intermediates that are modulated by 
surface terminations and defects present in the MXene [ 122 ]. 
Wan et al. [ 123 ] employed Ab initio molecular dynamics 
(AIMD) simulations to investigate the stability of MXenes in 
aqueous solution. The study reveals that the oxidation of 
Ti
2
CT
x 
MXene in water was highly dependent on surface 
termination, pH, and applied potential. − O‐terminated and 
vacancy‐defected surfaces easily degraded under neutral con -
ditions, whereas − F or − OH‐terminated surfaces remained 
stable by preventing close contact between water and surface 
Ti atoms through electronic repulsion and steric hindrance. 
Ghassemi et al. [ 124 ] systematically studied the interaction of 
MXene (Ti
3
C
2
T
x
) with air using environmental transmission 
electron microscopy along with Raman spectroscopy. Upon 
oxidation, Ti
3
C
2
T
x 
was found to gradually convert into a TiO
2
‐ 
carbon hybrid material, with the characteristics of TiO
2
, such 
as particle size, crystal structure, and morphology, strongly 
influenced by the temperature. Other factors, such as MXene 
etching, MXene concentration, and flake size, also contribute 
to MXene oxidation, and hence all of them are relevant to the 
CDI process [ 125 ].
Long‐term stability ( > 1000 cycles) of MXene electrodes in CDI 
devices is still warranted. Most long‐term stability studies have 
been conducted in three‐electrode electrochemical systems with 
concentrated electrolytes, which is a different environment from 
a CDI cell. Zhang et al. [ 126 ] employed a WS
2 
antioxidant 
coating for enhancing the durability of MXene as a CDI elec -
trode. The WS
2

long‐term durability, retaining ~90% of its capacitance after 
1000 GCD cycles in 1 M NaCl solution. However, only 40 CDI 
cycles were studied with a capacitance retention of ~95% (vs. 
~84% for bare MXene). Recently, Liu et al. [ 127 ] utilized rice 
husk‐derived activated carbon/MXene composites (RHAC/ 
MXene) as supercapacitor electrodes and studied much longer 
stability. The optimized RHAC/MXene electrode exhibited 
about 85%–90% capacity retention after 10,000 GCD cycles in 
6 M KOH solution. Although the work focuses on super -
capacitors, the stability performance information can be rele -
vant in CDI as well.
A few attempts have been reported to suppress the MXene 
oxidation by manipulating pH, potential, and terminal groups. 
Kumar et al. [ 128 ] chemically encapsulated Ti
3
C
2
T
x 
MXene 
nanosheets with succinic acid. The modification prevented 
oxidation in aqueous media by forming beneficial hydrogen‐ 
bonding interactions with Ti sites having a reduced coordi -
nation number. Consequently, MXene modified with succinic 
acid demonstrated long‐term stability in water for up to 
35 weeks by maintaining a specific capacity of 321 F g
− 1 
at 
1 A g
− 1
. Similarly, Zhao et al. [ 129 ] found that chelation‐ 
forming antioxidants (citric acid, tartaric acid, and oxalic 
acid) effectively prevented Ti
3
C
2
T
x 
and Ti
2
CT
x 
MXene oxida -
tion. The prevention mechanism was driven by chelation in -
teractions with the transition metal (Ti) atoms on the 
nanosheet surface, showing minimal MXene degradation after 
the 14‐day storage period.
The stability of MXene electrodes in CDI devices could also be 
compromised by ion intercalation, which induces dynamic 
changes in the MXene interlayer spacing during charge – discharge 
cycling. Repeated ion intercalation and deintercalation can cause 
mechanical strain and progressive volume changes within the 
electrode structure. These cumulative structural degradations 
ultimately lead to performance decay through capacity fading 
[ 130 ], representing one of the most critical challenges in devel -
oping durable CDI electrode materials. Advancements in elec -
trode design for electrochemical energy storage systems, which 
address similar issues of structural stability during ion insertion/ 
extraction, could provide valuable strategies to enhance the long‐ 
term cycling performance of MXenes in CDI applications.

unlike many other 2D materials, was not constrained by sub -
strate size or precursor limitations. Their study confirmed that 
scaling the reaction from 1‐g to 50‐g batches yielded MXene 
flakes with essentially identical structural, morphological, and 
electrical properties. This direct scalability, achieved by simply 
increasing the reactor volume, underscores the strong potential 
for the commercial production of high‐quality MXenes, a sig -
nificant advantage for their application in CDI and other in -
dustrial technologies. Furthermore, Zaed et al. [ 134 ] conducted 
a cost analysis of MXene production aimed at identifying 
pathways for low‐cost synthesis. Their study considered the 
complete production process, including the costs of precursors, 
ball milling, thermal treatment, grinding, etching, stirring, 
hazard control, washing, drying, characterization, and labor. 
They estimated the total cost to be approximately $20.33 USD 
per gram. This is significantly higher than the typical cost of 
conventional carbon‐based materials, such as AC, which ranges 
from $0.15 to $0.28 USD per kilogram. Additionally, extensive 
life cycle assessment (LCA) studies on MXene synthesis could 
support scalable production. Firouzjaei et al. [ 135 ] conducted an

adopted in CDI applications, future efforts must focus on 
reducing production costs along with the carbon footprint 
associated with material synthesis. On the other hand, when 
moving from desalination with water as the main product to ion 
removal for recovery purposes, the value of the recovered ma -
terial adds to the economic equation.

Fouling and Regeneration
MXene flakes are highly susceptible to fouling when used as a 
CDI electrode in water and wastewater systems containing 
organic and inorganic contaminants. Their intrinsically high 
surface energy and hydrophilic nature, originating from surface 
functional groups such as − OH, − O, and − F, make them prone 
to attract a wide range of foulants [ 136 ]. These include NOM, 
humic substances, surfactants, as well as pH‐dependent inor -
ganic scaling agents, which can precipitate and block active sites 
and interlayer spaces [ 137 ]. Additionally, microbial colonization 
and the accumulation of sediment or colloidal particles further 
exacerbate fouling, leading to performance degradation [ 138 ].
A major challenge with MXene‐based electrodes is achieving 
effective and sustained regeneration over prolonged cycling. 
Repeated use in fouling‐prone environments can lead to irre -
versible structural changes, such as oxidation, pore clogging, 
and the restacking of nanosheets [ 139 ]. These issues compro -
mise the regeneration efficiency, progressively reducing ion 
SAC and limiting long‐term operational stability.
Most of the work on electrode fouling is reported for capacitive 
materials [ 137, 140–142 ]. However, the fouling effect could be 
significantly different for intercalation materials. Liu et al. [ 143 ] 
investigated the fouling effect of humic acid (HA) on both 
capacitive and insertion electrodes for desalination. They found 
that while HA adsorbs onto the mesopores of capacitive carbon 
electrodes, it has a negligible impact ( < 5% capacity loss) on 
their ion storage capacity and cycling stability. In contrast, HA 
fouling severely degrades insertion electrodes (Na
4
Mn
9
O
18
), 
causing over 25% capacity loss. This high capacity decay was 
attributed to both hindered ion diffusion in the presence of 
adsorbed HA, as well as HA mediated electrode dissolution.
Moreover, Zeng et al. [ 144 ] developed a fouling‐resistant lead 
ion‐selective electrode (Pb
2 +
‐ISE) for electrochemical systems 
through a strategic protective layer design. A coating of den -
dritic peptides provided the antifouling properties by rendering 
the surface highly hydrophilic and electrically neutral. This 
modification reduced the water contact angle from ~90° to ~30°, 
which facilitated a protective hydration barrier that physically 
blocks the adhesion of contaminants. The efficacy of this layer 
was validated in antifouling experiments, where the modified 
electrodes maintained a stable Nernstian response for up to six 
days while immersed in concentrated bacterial suspensions (10
6 
CFU mL
− 1 
of Escherichia coli or Staphylococcus aureus ). 
Detailed fouling investigations on MXene electrodes in CDI are 
barely reported. Therefore, comprehensive studies are required 
to develop effective anti‐fouling strategies, surface modifica -
tions, and regeneration techniques that can preserve the struc -
tural integrity and electrochemical performance of MXene 
electrodes across extended CDI cycles.

Integration With Hybrid Systems
MXene electrodes typically exhibit lower selectivity for re -
sources such as lithium compared to conventional battery 
electrodes. However, combining MXene‐assisted CDI systems 
with existing ion separation techniques holds promise for 
enhancing selectivity and improving resource recovery.
Chung et al. [ 154 ] demonstrated a reverse osmosis‐flow capac -
itive deionization (RO‐FCDI) hybrid system that uses seawater 
or RO concentrate as the flowing electrolyte, eliminating the 
need for a separate salt solution. This integrated system ach -
ieved a remarkably low energy consumption of 1.3 kWh m
− 3 
in 
batch mode, producing a high‐quality effluent with a total dis -
solved solids below 300 mg L
− 1 
and demonstrating over 95% ion 
removal efficiency. Furthermore, by employing selective IEMs, 
the FCDI unit selectively removed monovalent ions, producing 
a final water rich in divalent ions that is more suitable for dis -
tribution and can minimize remineralization costs.
Moreover, Chen et al. [ 155 ] attempted a novel membrane stack 
design for continuous and energy‐efficient desalination using an 
integrated capacitive‐electrodialysis (CapED) process. The 
anode/AEM/CEM/AEM/CEM/cathode configuration was based 
on three chambers separated by IEMs, enabling simultaneous 
desalination and concentration of streams. During charging, 
both capacitive electrosorption and electrodialytic separation 
contributed to salt removal. In the discharging step, stored en -
ergy pushed further desalination via dialytic separation. The 
system achieved continuous operation with enhanced salt 
removal at higher voltages and low energy consumption 
(0.53–1.45 kWh kg
− 1
). CapED was reported to effectively 
combine capacitive and electrodialytic mechanisms for 
improved desalination. Similar approaches can also be utilized 
to explore smart combinations, aiming to include or enhance 
selective ion separation.
Furthermore, Xu et al. [ 156 ] developed an integrated CDI‐ 
photocatalysis system for efficient ammonium removal from 
low‐concentration wastewater. By making reduced graphene 
oxide/cerium dioxide (rGO/CeO
2
) composites, an electro -
sorption capacity as high as 7.66 mg g
− 1 
was achieved under 
optimal conditions (1.2 V, 1.0 mmol L
− 1
, 30 mL min
− 1
, 20°C). 
The composite also showed strong photocatalytic activity, 
removing 88% of ammonia and reducing total ammonium to 
1.35 mg L
− 1
, meeting China’s surface water quality standards. 
This performance was driven by the synergistic effect between 
rGO and CeO
2
.
Additionally, progress in materials for other processes, for 
example, catalysis, could also advance CDI [ 157, 158 ]. A recent 
study elucidates that orbital hybridization governs adsorption, 
activation, and transformation in heterogeneous catalysis, 
thereby affecting activity and selectivity [ 159 ]. These insights 
could be applicable to CDI electrode materials by tuning surface 
orbital interactions with target ions, enabling selective ion sep -
aration through controlled electron transfer.
These recent studies underscore that coupling CDI with 
complementary processes creates a synergistic effect, signifi -
cantly enhancing advanced wastewater treatment efficiency and 
resource recovery. We expect further implementation of 
advanced MXene electrodes in such hybrid systems remains an 
important future research direction.

Concluding Remarks
CDI is an elegant electro‐driven ion separation technique that 
not only can be used for brackish water desalination but also is 
particularly interesting for a wide range of ion‐selective treat -
ment and resource‐recovery applications. However, traditional 
carbon‐based electrodes used in CDI do not provide sufficient 
SAC and selectivity toward target ions. This is where emerging 
materials such as MXene come into play; their employment as a 
CDI electrode offers a high capacity due to their distinctive 2D 
layers, tunable and reversible, selective ion storage, and facile 
integration possibilities with other advanced materials to further 
exploit these interesting properties. In this review, a compre -
hensive overview of CDI technology is presented, focusing on 
the latest studies on MXene‐based electrodes and their, possibly 
underrated, potential in selective ion separations. The discus -
sion includes in‐depth details of state‐of‐the‐art CDI techniques, 
architectures, electrode materials, additives, electrolytes, sepa -
rators, membranes, and various configurations. With a focus on 
MXene, the discussion delves into the latest developments in 
their preparation techniques, highlighting the critical challenges 
associated with conventional MXene synthesis and exploring 
potential opportunities for emerging MXene etching techniques. 
MXene engineering strategies for selective ion separation are 
also included, such as interlayer spacing adjustment, surface 
charge modification, and the introduction of redox‐active cen -
ters through functionalization, hybridization, and doping. 
Additionally, the tuning of operational parameters, such as pH, 
applied voltage, flow rate, and initial ion concentrations, for 
selective ion separation is critically analyzed. Furthermore, 
challenges associated with MXene‐based materials, such as 
limited cyclic stability and durability, susceptibility to fouling 
and oxidation, pore blocking, and high cost, have been dis -
cussed. Currently, these challenges remain significant obstacles 
to scaling up MXene‐powered CDI systems, prompting the need 
for further research. Finally, potential opportunities such as the 
development of suitable MXene composites, AI‐driven optimi -
zation strategies, and integration of CDI with existing technol -
ogies are explored. All combined, this review aims to serve as a 
valuable resource for guiding future innovations in CDI and
