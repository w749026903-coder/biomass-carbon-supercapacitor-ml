ABSTRACT: Developing oxygen reduction reaction (ORR)
catalysts with high efficiency and stability represents a key
requirement for zinc-air batteries. Employing a mixture of iron
nitrate nonahydrate, citric acid, and ammonium persulfate as
precursors and g-C
3
N
4
as a sacrificial template, this study
synthesized Fe, N, S co-doped porous carbon nanosheets via
ball-milling and one-step pyrolysis, offering a solvent-free and
simple process. In alkaline electrolyte, Fe-NSC 1000 exhibited
outstanding ORR performance, showing onset and half-wave
potentials of 1.00 and 0.896 V, respectively. Fabricated into the
cathode of a zinc-air battery, it offered outstanding durability (up to
1025 h at 5 mA cm
−2
) in addition to a specific capacity of 760.65
mA h g
Zn
−1
and a peak power density of 145.65 mW cm
−2
. This
work provides a simpler and greener strategy for designing iron-based sulfur-doped ORR catalysts, driving forward zinc-air battery
technology.
1. INTRODUCTION
Given the global shift in energy systems alongside the
implementation of the “dual-carbon” strategy, zinc-air batteries
(ZABs) have emerged as a critical energy storage technology to
overcome traditional energy dependency and resource
constraints, owing to their advantages including economic
viability, excellent theoretical energy density, and ecological
compatibility.
1−3
Nevertheless, the practical application of this
technology remains constrained by several core challenges: the
sluggish ORR kinetics at the air cathode, the prohibitive cost of
commercial Pt/C, and the insufficient catalytic activity of non-
noble metal catalysts.
4−6
Consequently, creating low-cost ORR
catalysts based on non-noble metals with both high activity and
excellent durability has emerged as a key route to optimize the
energy mix and safeguard energy security.
Heteroatom doping is a widely adopted strategy for
modulating carbon materials’ electronic configuration and
defect abundance for better catalysis.
7−12
Nitrogen (N) doping
represents the most prevalent form. Owing to its relatively high
electronegativity, the carbon skeleton’ electronic configuration
can be tuned by nitrogen atoms, thereby inducing the
formation of additional defects and creating active sites such
as graphitic nitrogen.
13−15
Owing to their role in facilitating
oxygen adsorption and electron transfer, these active sites bring
about a pronounced increase in the ORR catalytic performance
of the material.
16
However, external doping with nitrogen
alone can easily lead to excessive nitrogen incorporation,
thereby compromising the overall structural stability of the
material.
17,18
Therefore, selecting a nitrogen-rich template as a
nitrogen source is crucial for achieving effective N-doping
while maintaining structural integrity. Moreover, as a self-
sacrificial template, g-C
3
N
4
can be directly converted into
nitrogen-doped porous carbon during high-temperature
carbonization. The g-C
3
N
4
possesses a graphite-like planar
structure and has been extensively studied owing to its
distinctive features, such as excellent thermal stability, robust
stability, tunable structure, and chemical inertness.
19
For
instance, Qin et al. utilized g-C
3
N
4
as a support to synthesize
double-shelled carbon nanospheres (FSCN-NS).
20
In this
system, g-C
3
N
4
also served as a protective agent; its stable
supporting effect effectively mitigated chemical corrosion,
thereby ensuring that the as-prepared FSCN-NS exhibited
exceptional structural stability and durability during the ORR
process at the fuel cell cathode. This not only effectively
compensates for the loss of nitrogen at elevated temperatures
but also circumvents the cumbersome process of removing the
template using complex etching steps, fully demonstrating its
unique advantages as a self-sacrificial template.
21,22
Furthermore, sulfur doping can expand the interlayer
spacing of materials, which facilitates the transport of reactants

23−25
First-principles
studies based on density functional theory further confirm that
sulfur dopants can also introduce significant spin polarization
into the carbon lattice, thereby enhancing the oxygen
reduction reaction performance.
26
Gu et al. compared the
ORR and oxygen evolution reaction activities of graphdiyne
doped with different heteroatoms (B, N, P, S) through density
functional theory (DFT) calculations.
8
The computational
results revealed that S-doped graphdiyne exhibits the optimal
ORR activity, which is primarily attributed to the relatively
high positive charge density on its active centers. Beyond
single-heteroatom doping, multi-heteroatom co-doping is also
recognized as an effective strategy for enhancing ORR catalytic
performance. Among these, nitrogen/sulfur (N/S) co-doping
has been extensively investigated due to its superior perform-
ance arising from the synergistic effects.
27−30
N/S co-doped
three-dimensional nanoporous carbon materials were success-
fully prepared by Chen et al. through a combined approach of
self-assembly and carbonization, utilizing a hybrid of covalent
triazine framework and sodium lignosulfonate (CTF@LS) as a
multifunctional precursor.
31
Theoretical calculations demon-
strate that the combined effect of nitrogen and sulfur
heteroatom doping significantly facilitates the desorption of
*OOH and its conversion to *O, thereby markedly
accelerating ORR process.
Previous studies have demonstrated that iron-based catalysts
possess advantages such as outstanding stability, high catalytic
activity, and economical viability.
32−34
Specifically, Fe−N−C
enhance ORR activity of individual active sites by forming
highly active FeN
X
sites within microporous graphitized
carbon, achieving a level of performance that equals or
overtakes traditional platinum catalysts.
35−37
Chen et al.
developed a 3D bifunctional catalyst,denoted FeN
x
/N,S−C,
containing co-doped nitrogen, sulfur, and iron, using a simple,
eco-friendly molten salt-assisted route.
38
It is the combination
of uniformly dispersed FeN
x
sites, surrounding S heteroatoms,
and the 3D porous architecture that synergistically provides
the catalyst (FeN
x
/N,S−C) with high accessibility, abundant
open pores/channels, and dense active sites that facilitate mass
transfer. In actual catalytic environments, multiple components
typically work in synergy. Iron-based catalysts often contain
adjacent species such as Fe
x
Oγ, Fe, and Fe
3
C. Although these
components are not the most direct active sites themselves,
they significantly influence the reduction/desorption behavior
of peroxide intermediates in ORR, thereby boosting the
intrinsic ORR activity of FeN
x
.
39−44
Recent studies have further reported a variety of non-noble-
metal catalysts and air-electrode designs for oxygen electro-
catalysis and ZABs.
45−48
Collectively, these representative
studies highlight the importance of integrating active-site
regulation with porous-structure engineering and efficient
mass-transfer pathways to improve ORR activity and practical
battery performance.
Despite these advances, considerable attention has been
devoted to heteroatom composition, active-site configuration,
porous-structure engineering, and electrode architecture,
whereas the influence of pyrolysis temperature on the
evolution of the carbon framework, Fe-containing species,
and ORR performance within a fixed precursor system has
been less systematically clarified. Therefore, in this study, a
facile method combining ball milling with one-step pyrolysis
was employed to successfully synthesize a hierarchically porous
carbon nanosheet material (denoted as Fe-NSC), using ferric
nitrate nonahydrate, ammonium persulfate, and citric acid as
precursors, and g-C
3
N
4
as a sacrificial template. The effect of
temperature regulation on its catalytic activity was sub-
sequently investigated. In contrast to literature methods that
typically involve complex solvent systems, multi-step reactions,
or harsh conditions, this method consists of only two steps:
solvent-free ball milling and one-step pyrolysis. This strategy
significantly streamlines the experimental procedure, and
enhances experimental reproducibility.
49
Electrochemical test
results demonstrate that Fe-NSC 1000 exhibits superior ORR
catalytic activity and superior durability over commercial Pt/C
when tested in 0.1 M KOH. The catalyst also demonstrates
exceptional battery performance when assembled into a zinc-
air battery. In summary, the Fe-NSC material developed in this
study integrates advantages such as high catalytic activity, and
facile synthesis, making it highly promising for ZABs.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials
Urea, citric acid, ammonium persulfate, and iron(III) nitrate
nonahydrate (98 wt %) were purchased from the following suppliers:
Titan Scientific Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), Mio Chemical Reagent
Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China), Damao Chemical Reagent Factory
(Tianjin, China), and damas-beta (Shanghai, China).
2.2. Synthesis of Fe-NSC
The preparation of the Fe-NSC is shown in Scheme 1. First, g-C
3
N
4
was prepared. A crucible containing 20 g of urea was subjected to heat
treatment in a muffle furnace at a ramp rate of 5 °C/min up to 550 °C
and then sintered at that temperature for 4 h. The resulting product
was subsequently allowed to cool naturally to room temperature,
yielding layered graphitic carbon nitride (g-C
3
N
4
).
The Fe-NSC materials were synthesized via a straightforward
method combining ball-milling with one-step pyrolysis. Specifically, 1
g of citric acid, 1 g of ammonium persulfate (APS), 1.5 g of g-C
3
N
4
,
and 0.02 g of iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate were placed in a ball-
milling jar and processed for 120 s, yielding a homogeneous yellow
precursor powder. Subsequently, it was subjected to pyrolysis in a N
2
atmosphere. At a ramp rate of 3 °C/min, it was heated to 900, 1000,
or 1100 °C, isothermally treated for 2 h at the target temperature,
subsequently furnace-cooled to room temperature. The products were
nitrogen, sulfur, and iron co-doped carbon nanosheets, designated as
Fe-NSC 900, Fe-NSC 1000, and Fe-NSC 1100, respectively. To
interrogate the contribution of each precursor component, a series of
control materials were prepared by individually omitting iron(III)
nitrate nonahydrate, g-C
3
N
4
, citric acid, or ammonium persulfate from
the Fe-NSC 1000 synthesis procedure.

Furthermore, The Fe-rich domains are spatially embedded
within the N-containing carbon matrix. Notably, although
elemental mapping shows spatial overlap of S with Fe, no
characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline
Fe−S phases were detected in the XRD pattern, ruling out the
presence of highly crystallized iron sulfides. In other words, the
S atoms are dominantly doped into carbon skeleton rather
than bonding with Fe species to form iron sulfides. This mainly
depends on the decomposition characteristics of the precursor
during the high-temperature pyrolysis process. During the
heating-up stage, citric acid decomposes and generates carbon
intermediates rich in oxygen functional groups (such as

to produce active S species (such as SO

51,52
These oxygen functional groups have a strong
chemical affinity for S species, causing the active S to be
preferentially captured by the functional groups on the carbon
intermediates and subsequently anchored at the defect sites of
the carbon framework. Therefore, S is ultimately stably doped
in the form of C−S−C and C−SO
x
-C, which is consistent with
the XPS results. On the other hand, the doped sulfur does not
directly combine with the FeN
4
sites but by forming thiophene

centers, which in turn regulates the adsorption energy of
oxygen intermediates.
53

prepared at different pyrolysis temperatures (900, 1000, and
1100 °C) are shown in Figure 2a. A broad characteristic
diffraction peak near 25.2° is observed for all samples, which is
assigned to the (002) plane of graphitic carbon, indicating the
formation of a partially graphitized carbon framework. No
obvious diffraction peaks attributable to crystalline Fe or Fe
3
C

locally observed Fe-rich nanoparticles and Fe

crystalline Fe-containing domains, if present, are not dominant
as bulk crystalline phases or are below the detection capability
of the present XRD measurement. Therefore, the Fe
3

species observed by HRTEM are described as locally observed
nanocrystalline domains rather than a dominant bulk
crystalline phase.
Similarly, no obvious diffraction peaks corresponding to
crystalline iron sulfides, such as FeS or FeS
2

the XRD patterns. This indicates that highly crystalline iron
sulfide phases are not formed in detectable amounts. It should
be noted that EDS elemental mapping mainly reflects the

confirm the formation of crystalline Fe−S phases. Therefore,
based on the combined XRD and EDS results, detectable
crystalline FeS/FeS
2

Fe−S coordination or amorphous iron sulfide-like species
cannot be completely ruled out. The chemical state of sulfur is
further discussed based on the S 2p XPS analysis below.

(I

55

value of 0.95, indicating a

pyrolysis temperature increases to 1000 °C, the I

intensified carbonization, heteroatom incorporation/removal,
and the formation of a more disordered porous carbon

may contribute to the exposure and stabilization of Fe-related
active species and heteroatom-doped carbon sites, thereby
benefiting the ORR process. When the pyrolysis temperature
further increases to 1100 °C, the I

/I
G
value decreases to 0.92.
This decrease may be attributed to thermal annealing and
structural rearrangement of the carbon framework, which
promote graphitization and partially remove or reconstruct
unstable defect/disordered sites.
56

temperature may also lead to partial loss of heteroatom
dopants, further affecting the density and configuration of
defect-related active sites. Combined with the XRD and
Raman results, 1000 °C is considered an appropriate pyrolysis
temperature for constructing Fe-NSC with a suitable balance
between carbon framework ordering and structural disorder,
which is beneficial for improving electronic conductivity,
exposing potential active sites, and facilitating electrocatalytic
performance.

3.2. Electrocatalytic Performance of Catalysts
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was first carried out in a 0.1 M KOH
to probe the electrocatalytic performance of Fe-NSC. with the
results shown in Figure 3a. Under oxygen-saturated conditions,
the CV curves exhibited distinct reduction peaks, which was
absent under nitrogen-saturated conditions, indicating that
oxygen molecules underwent a reduction reaction on the Fe-
NSC catalyst surface, thereby confirming its oxygen reduction
catalytic activity. Specifically, the reduction peak for Fe-NSC
1000 was located at 0.877 V, significantly higher than those of
Fe-NSC 900 (0.812 V) and Fe-NSC 1100 (0.813 V),
preliminarily suggesting that Fe-NSC 1000 possesses the
optimal oxygen reduction catalytic activity. To further verify
the optimal pyrolysis temperature for Fe-NSC, the half-wave
potentials (E
1/2
) of the three catalysts were determined using
linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) in an alkaline environment
and compared with that of Pt/C catalyst. As shown in Figure
3b and 3c, the E
1/2
of Fe-NSC 1000 was 0.896 V, significantly
higher than those of Fe-NSC 900 (0.873 V) and Fe-NSC 1100
(0.881 V), confirming that its optimal pyrolysis temperature is
around 1000 °C. Notably, the E
1/2
of Fe-NSC 1000 is 44 mV
higher than that of Pt/C catalyst, indicating that Fe-NSC 1000
possesses superior catalytic activity and can promote the ORR
more effectively. Furthermore, the onset potential (E
onset

OCV of the ZAB assembled with Fe-NSC 1000 stabilized at
1.500 V, notably higher than that of Pt/C. Figure 4b presents
the power density curves calculated from the discharge curves.
The Fe-NSC 1000-based ZAB achieved a peak power density
of 145.65 mW cm
−2
, surpassing the Pt/C-based counterpart by
approximately 22 mW cm
−2

galvanostatic discharge curves at a current density of 5 mA

5 mA cm
−2

Pt/C-based ZAB. Normalized by the weight of zinc consumed,
the specific capacity of the Fe-NSC 1000-based ZAB was
760.65 mA h g
Zn
−1
, markedly higher than the Pt/C-based ZAB.
Figure 4d illustrates the galvanostatic charge−discharge
processes at different current densities (2−40 mA cm
−2
). At
40 mA cm
−2
, the voltage of the ZAB based on Fe-NSC 1000
was 1.1 V, significantly outperforming the Pt/C-based ZAB
(0.8 V). When the current density returned to 2 mA cm
−2
, the

connected two ZABs assembled with Fe-NSC 1000 in series.
The series-connected battery pack successfully powered an

voltage gap became 1.0078 V, and the cycling efficiency was

To further evaluate the structural stability of Fe-NSC 1000, the
SEM, XRD, and XPS were performed after the battery
durability test. As shown in Figure S11, the SEM image of
the Fe-NSC 1000 shows that the porous nanosheet-like
morphology is largely retained, without obvious collapse of the
carbon framework or severe aggregation. Because of the
relatively low catalyst loading, the post-cycling catalyst-coated
carbon-paper/Ni-foam air cathode was directly subjected to
XRD characterization. Therefore, the sharp graphitic carbon,
metallic Ni, and PTFE-related peaks mainly originate from the
carbon paper, Ni-foam current collector, and PTFE-containing
electrode component, respectively. As shown in the enlarged
carbon diffraction region in the inset, a broad carbonaceous
background is present underneath the intense carbon-paper
reflection, consistent with the broad carbon peak of Fe-NSC
1000 being overlapped and largely obscured by the stronger
substrate signal. Apart from the diffraction features associated
with the electrode substrate and PTFE component, no
additional resolvable peaks attributable to newly formed
crystalline Fe-containing phases are observed after long-term
cycling. In addition, the XPS spectra confirm that Fe-, N-, and
S-containing species are still detectable after the durability test.
Although slight changes in the relative proportions of surface
species may occur, probably due to surface oxidation,
electrolyte exposure, or partial surface reconstruction under
alkaline conditions, the major chemical features of Fe-NSC
1000 are largely preserved.

These results demonstrate the
good morphological and compositional stability of Fe-NSC
1000, which contributes to its durable Zn-air battery
performance. As summarized in Table S4, Fe-NSC 1000
exhibits competitive Zn-air battery performance compared
with recently reported Fe/N/S-containing carbon electro-
catalysts, which highlights its practical application potential.
4. CONCLUSION
In this study, a solvent-free ball milling combined with one-
step pyrolysis strategy was employed to prepare hierarchical
porous carbon nanosheet materials, denoted as Fe-NSC, using
iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate, citric acid, and ammonium
persulfate as precursors and g-C
3
N
4
as a sacrificial template at
different temperatures (900, 1000, and 1100 °C). In alkaline
electrolyte, Fe-NSC 1000 exhibited optimal oxygen reduction
reaction catalytic performance, showing onset and half-wave
potentials of 1.00 and 0.896 V, respectively. Fabricated into the
cathode of a ZAB, this material offered outstanding durability
(up to 1025 h at 5 mA cm
−2
) in addition to a specific capacity
of 760.65 mA h g
Zn
−1
and a peak power density of 145.65 mW
cm
−2
, indicating outstanding battery performance. This work
examines the influence of temperature on the catalytic activity
of iron-based catalysts, identifying 1000 °C as the optimal
synthesis temperature. At relatively low temperatures, the pore
structure within the catalyst is insufficiently developed,
resulting in a low specific surface area and inadequate exposure
of active sites. At excessively high temperatures, excessive
thermal etching leads to the precipitation of iron atoms,
disrupting the FeN
x
structure. Concurrently, the high temper-
ature induces nitrogen loss and promotes the rearrangement of
carbon atoms to repair structural defects, ultimately leading to
a significant reduction in FeN
x
active sites. This work offers a
simpler and greener method for synthesizing high-performance
catalytic materials for zinc-air battery applications.
