ABSTRACT  
Green energy-based power generation is a most rising topic in scientific society. Carbon water interaction-based 
hydrovoltaic (HV) power generation is a futuristic current research theme to generate power; however, the actual 
process remains an open question. Here in this work, a novel three-dimensional (3D) tubular HV device is 
fabricated, and its voltage distribution is analyzed. Under ambient conditions, a single tubular activated carbon 
hydrovoltaic (TAC) device can generate a voltage of up to 0.9 V and a current of up to 0.7 mA. Moreover, the 
outstanding power deliver from TAC is nearly 600 μ W for 1.2 g of active materials, and it is stable for more than 
ten days without any external light, wind, or heat. Further voltage improvement is tested with various factors 
such as light, heat, and humidity. Based on water flow-induced potential, 4 TACs can light an LED for successive 
ten days under ambient conditions (without any external energy supply). Moreover, the fabricated TAC device 
shows excellent self-life after one year of the experiment. Overcoming the two-dimensional (2D) HV devices, this 
self-charging 3D tubular high-power configuration is a low-cost HV device based on activated carbon and has 
advantages for practical applications.

population and modern technologies has been a significant concern in 
energy crisis over the recent years [ 1 , 2 ]. Green energy-based technol -
ogies are well developed and improved to reduce carbon footprints for a 
better tomorrow. Popularized technologies like piezoelectric [ 3 ], 
photovoltaic [ 4 – 6 ], thermoelectric [ 7 ], and photodetector [ 8 ]

technologies have inspired to develop new technical approaches for 
generating electric power in a sustainable manner using nanotech -

manage in large-scale applications. Generally, carbon is a highly abun -
dant, low-cost material on earth that is popularized in various fields such 
as EM shielding [ 9 ], supercapacitor [ 10 ], freshwater production [ 11 ,

via solid-liquid ( carbon-water ) interaction (CWI) was found promising to 
demonstrate potential induction without any high external energy 
consumption [ 13 ]. Briefly, power generation through water evaporation 
is an emerging green technology called hydro-voltaic (HV) to produce 
power through carbon-water molecular interaction. In this approach, 
water molecules are pumped to the top by capillary action, the resulting 
voltage is generated by the gradient of charges of the water molecules 
interacting with functional groups-rich carbon nanostructures. Any 
water-like liquid, droplet, moisture, or vapour leads to interaction with 
carbon nanostructures to generate the HV effect. When water passes 
through the functional groups rich carbon substrate, it forms an electric 
double layer (EDL). This type of interaction along the length of device is 
varies, as the amount of water molecules differ along the length. The 
amount of H
+
ions in the vertical porous media varies, forming a 
gradient of EDL which can create an electric potential along the device. 
This mechanism is schematically shown in Fig. 1 . 
Voltage generation from CNT was reported in the year 2003 [ 14 ]. So 
far, colossal carbon-based materials exhibit the HV effect without 
non-uniformity of pressure along its length. Yet, the impact of HV is an 
ultimate future technology to fabricate a power generator with low 
energy consumption. Several theories were used to explain the mecha -
nism behind the hydro voltaic, such as the electric double layer and 
pseudocapacitance mechanism [ 15 , 16 ], ion diffusion-induced mecha -
nism [ 17 ], and so on. However, the explanation for the HV working 
mechanism remains controversial. Diverse devices were being devel -
oped for hydrovoltaic power generation, like moisture-induced elec -
tricity generation [ 18 ], droplet movement-induced power generation 
[ 19 ], flow-induced electricity generation [ 20 ], and evaporation-induced 
electricity generation [ 21 ]. Of these methods, evaporation-induced 
power generation is natural and efficient in producing a high potential 
and current; generally, in this method, the abundance of functional 
groups in the carbon results in voltage generation when the water 
molecules interact via capillary flow. Plasma treatment to add func -
tional groups, [ 18 , 22 ] and hydroxide-based materials [ 23 ], were used to 
enhance the interaction between carbon and water. Also, the structural 
change in the device could be a factor in enhancing the evaporation for 
better CWI. However, all the existing HV-based reports show consider -
able voltage [ 23 , 24 ], of the order of 1 V [ 13 ], but the induced electric 
current from the device is in the range of few nA to μ A [ 13 , 25 , 26 ]. 
Notably, external sources like light, heat, or wind (apart from ambient 
conditions) also essential to generate power from the devices. All re -
ported HV devices are thin film-based structures that are limited by the 
low possibility of CWI. Also, arranging this 2D device at a particular 
angle for better capillary is complicated for a large-scale practical 
application and requires external energy. 
Here a novel 3D tubular hydrovoltaic device was fabricated that uses 
activated carbon (TAC) and water to generate high voltage and 
considerable current with less dependency on external energy such as 
light, heat, or wind. Converting 2D to 3D HV devices can show a higher 
possibility of CWI. This could be very useful for potential applications. 
Moreover, the device shows shelf-life of more than one year. This 
method can overcome other reported HV devices under ambient con -
ditions to generate electric current for practical electronic applications. 
The capillary flow of water is varied with different radii tubes to prove 
that the potential induction due to the substance ’ s interplay with water 
molecules. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Device fabrication 
A typical device fabrication process is illustrated in Fig. S1 , and the 
actual device image is attached in Fig. S2 . Briefly, both side openable 
acrylic 3 mm thickness and 50 mm long tube was taken and washed with 
double distilled (DI) water. Two aluminum foil electrodes were con -
nected along two ends of the tube and tiny pores were made using direct 
punching (less than 1 mm diameter) uniformly in the bottom part of the 
electrode ( Fig. S1a ). The commercial cotton fiber was attached to the 
bottom electrode for the continuous water supply and wettability 
( Fig. S1b ). Powdered activated carbon (made from coconut husk) was 
fully packed inside the tube, as shown in Fig. S1c (average weight of 
carbon is 1.2 g). The final device was taken to analyze the hydro-voltaic 
effect. To understand the CWI, different diameters and various lengths of 
tubes were used to vary the water pumping rate. Three different di -
ameters (1 cm, 1.5 cm, and 2 cm) ( Fig. S3 ) with the same length (5 cm) 
TAC were fabricated with the same amount of carbon to analyze the 
diameter dependence of CWI and named TAC1, TAC1.5, and TAC2. 
From the hydrovoltaic measurement, TAC1.5 performed best. Therefore, 
we selected this device for further measurements. Three different length 
tubes (4 cm, 5 cm, and 6 cm) with the same diameter TAC1.5 were also 
constructed to confirm the capillary rise and mentioned as TAC4, TAC5, 
and TAC6, respectively. Moreover, to differentiate this new 3D from 
other existing 2D designs, the same amount of carbon (1.2 g) was taken 
to construct a 2D HV device.

2.2. Hydro-voltaic measurement 
All hydrovoltaic measurements were carried out using a Tektronix 
Source meter (model no: 2450 SMU). Aluminum tape was used as a 
working electrodes. First, the open-circuit voltage and current were

electric power generation from the device by CWI. To compare and 
propose strong evidence of this new 3D design, the same amount of 3D

with same amount of carbon. This data was attached in supporting data

3. Result and discussion 
First, the TAC device was fabricated using a cylindrical tube filled 
with activated carbon. For an ideal HV device, the role of capillary ac -
tion is an important factor for a better EDL formation ( Fig. 1 ), so to 
understand the porous structure of this activated carbon, FESEM anal -
ysis was carried out. This activated carbon has many granules-like 
structures with a high amount of pores as shown in Fig. 2a . Further, 
for TAC, the stacking of activated carbon inside the tube increases the 
surface contact between nearby granules, further increasing the water 
uptake ability. Fig. 2 b shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) graph of the 
activated carbon from the coconut shell. The graph reveals the diffrac -
tion peak at 24
◦

depend on the number of pores and the surface area. So, BET analysis is a 
much-needed technique to evaluate the porous structure of the carbon. 
Popularly, the porous structure was created by an activation process 
with four pore development stages [ 28 ]. Fig. 2 c shows the nitrogen 
adsorption-desorption isotherm of the activated carbon. The obtained 
curve denotes the Type IV isotherm based on the International Union of 
Pure and Applied Chemistry classification (IUPAC). Type IV isotherms 
possess a characteristic hysteresis loop with condensation in the meso -
pores, limiting uptake at high relative pressure. From Fig. 2 d, it is clear 
that the pore size ranges between 2 and 20 nm, indicating the meso -
porous nature of the materials [ 29 ]. 
The hydro-voltaic performances of TAC were measured and dis -
played in Fig. S5 under various conditions, with varying carbon filling in 
the tube to examine the device ’ s CWI effect. Fig. S5 summarizes all the 
device ’ s performances. Under ambient conditions, all fabricated devices 
developed a potential of more than 0.5 V, which increases further 
depending on device structure and external factors. However, the 2D HV 
device with the same amount of carbon shows less voltage difference 
due to the less CWI. Moreover, the existing 2D designs need an external 
supply of energy for water uptake in the thin film or 2D surface plane, 
indicating the practical difficulty. Adhesive materials were added for 
stabiliy of 2D device, which reduces the EDL formation. The packing 
density in 3D device ensures stability and neglect the needs for adhesive 
materials. This further facilitates better CWI to improve the efficiency. 
Measurements at varied humidity and different length tubes validates 
the device ’ s CWI. Performance was assessed in a humid controlled room 
to evaluate the fabricated device ’ s all-weather compatibility. The 
room ’ s relative humidity (RH) was kept between 30% and 90%, and its 
open circuit voltage (V
oc
) levels were measured. To avoid the galvanic 
effect, two identical electrodes were utilized. The porous property of 
activated carbon assisted water wicking, which causes water to interact 
with carbon to form an EDL. The pumping rate of water changes 
generating streaming potential [ 18 ].

the HV effect. The same phenomena are expected to develop the voltage

of TAC increases the interaction because of the 3-axes model, and the 
confinement of carbon particles increases the EDL formation and 
wettability.

To further confirm the CWI as the origin for current and voltage 
generation, electricity generation in a dry state (without water) and wet 
state were studied. Fig. 4 a demonstrates the TAC showing zero voltage 
for up to 2500 s during a dry state (without adding water), and suddenly

water is the key component for voltage generation. The developed 
current in TAC during dry and wet states has also been verified, shown in 
Fig. 4 b. From these two graphs, it was clear that the voltage generated 
from a device takes time to attain maximum output in the range of 300 
s – 500 s, this means, this is the actual time of the water pumping rate

Therefore, different morphological carbons with differences in func -
tional groups were also taken. Carbon Black (CB) is a carbon-based 
material with very less functional groups. Therefore, it is hydrophobic 
in nature. This was taken to understand the effect of CWI by loading the 
CB instead of activated carbon. As assumed, no voltage/current is 
developed during the CB load because of its high hydrophobic property 
(i.e.) water would not rise to the top by capillary action along its length,

more than 0.5 V (the less voltage with Fig. 3 a due to TAC6 and different

Next, the voltage was measured during light ON and OFF conditions to 
conclude the effect of external energy (light) on power generation. 
Generally, we know water ’ s evaporation can vary depending on the light 
exposure. However, due to the tube-like structure of TAC, the light- 
absorbing area is small. By increasing TAC ’ s surface area, the light 
absorbing area could also be varied, but it will affect the pumping rate. 
Thus, light plays a minor role here for TAC devices, as shown in Fig. 4 d. 
Voltage has enhanced during light ON condition and slightly reduced 
(not to zero) when light is OFF. This cyclic behavior also specifies the 
voltage has been developed under ambient conditions and boosted when 
external energy comes into play. Under the room temperature and hu -
midity, TAC can provide more than 0.7 V constantly for a long time. This 
confirms the effect of ambient energy is sufficient for power generation. 
Various humid environments (30% – 70% of RH) have been devel -
oped to study the water uptake and potential change to reveal the 
contribution of possible water-flow-induced potential generation. The 
device produced an output of about 0.805 V under the RH of 70% and 
increased to 0.865 V when RH was reduced to 30%, as shown in Fig. 5 a. 
To confirm the mechanism with further proof; relative humidity was 
altered to analyze the effect of TAC. Reduction in RH from 70% to 65% 
in the first 1900 s shows the voltage has slightly increased from 0.86 to 
0.87 V, indicating that the reduction of atmospheric humidity enhances 
the evaporation rate and rapidly shifts to 0.89 V when hot air passes by. 
During the passing of hot air on the top of the TAC device, more water 
molecules evaporated from the top in a short interval of time (evapo -
ration rate increases). Thus, more water molecules are promoted to the 
top through wicking, favouring EDL formation. This gives a rapid in -
crease in the voltage. 
After the removal of hot air, the voltage drops to 0.87 V between the 
electrodes, as recorded in Fig. 5 b. Even though the effect of humidity 
does not provide a huge change in the system, the above conducted 
measurements confirm the generation of voltage is due to the CWI 
gradient through porous carbon media. The water pumping-induced 
voltage and current can be steadily sustained for several days and sud -
denly fall to zero in short-circuit conditions.

4. Conclusion 
An efficient 3D tubular hydrovoltaic (TAC-HV) device was been 
constructed via the evaporation-induced power-generating method. 
Overcoming the drawbacks of existing 2D HV devices, this 3D device 
showed self-charging ability without any external energy apart form 
ambient condition. This self-charging, free-standing device shows a 
maximum open circuit voltage of more than 0.9 V and a short circuit 
current of 0.72 mA and is enhanced when external energy is applied. A 
single TAC HV can deliver the maximum power of 600 μ W/1.2 g, which 
is higher than any reported HV devices and highly beneficial for real- 
time practical applications. Several experiments were conducted by 
varying humidity, carbon morphology, sealing, light ON-OFF conditions 
etc, to confirm the role of CWI in power generation, and its depending 
factors were also analyzed. Series connection of 4 TACs could glow an 
LED for ten days without any external energy. This low-cost, one-step 
fabrication has great potential for resolving energy crisis. Further 
detailed studies are required to understand the mechanism of power 
generation, and to enhance the voltage and current output. Various 
carbon nanostructure-based materials can be used to resolve the limi -
tations in low cost power generation.
