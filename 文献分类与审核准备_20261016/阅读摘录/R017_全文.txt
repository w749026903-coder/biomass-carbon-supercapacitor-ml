ORIGINAL ARTICLE
Biomass‑derived nanostructured carbon materials 
for high‑performance supercapacitor electrodes
Mehrnaz Ebrahimi1 · Hassan Hosseini‑Monfared1  · Mehran Javanbakht1 · Fatemeh Mahdi1
Received: 12 November 2022 / Revised: 24 December 2022 / Accepted: 28 December 2022 / Published online: 10 January 2023 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2023
Abstract
To achieve a higher energy density for the supercapacitor without degrading cycle stability, functionalization of biomass 
based-AC with oxygen-containing groups was applied. Nanostructured carbon materials were produced from barley straw 
biomass waste using the activation-functionalization by  FeCl3 and urea via the pyrolysis method in a tubular reactor at 
900 °C. The electrode made up of carbon BCF-U in the three-electrode cell in aqueous KOH electrolyte showed high-
specific capacitance of 515 F  g–1 at current density of 2 A  g−1  and in aqueous  Na2SO4 electrolyte the capacitance of 379 F 
 g−1  at 3 A  g−1 . The BCF-U electrode showed cycling stability, retaining more than 99% capacitance after 5000 cycles in 1 M 
 Na2SO4 electrolyte. The fabricated symmetric supercapacitor device with carbon BCF-U showed a high-energy density of 
10.7 Wh  kg–1 and a power density of 720 W   kg−1  in aqueous 6 M KOH. Different characterizations were carried out to show 
that the functional group and morphological features of the activated carbon were closely related to the superior capacitive 
performance by enhancing diffusion of electrolyte ions. The analysis proved the formation of graphitic nature with mixed 
micro- and mesoporous textures in addition to carbon nanotube, carbon nano rod, and graphene. These unique features make 
synthetic activated and functionalized samples possess good adsorption capacity for electrical double layer capacitors. The 
results demonstrate that nanostructured carbon materials derived from a low-cost biomass waste can serve as an economical 
carbon feedstock for production of high-performance electrode material for supercapacitors.
Keywords Supercapacitor · Barley straw-derived carbon · Carbon nanostructures · Functionalization
1 Introduction
Lignocellulosic biomass is readily available, low cost, and 
bio-renewable with great potential for the preparation of 
carbon materials, biofuels, and biochemicals [1 ]. Mega 
gram amounts of lignocellulosic biomass residue are gener-
ated through forestry and agricultural activities annually in 
world [2]. Converting lignocellulosic biomass into added-
value products such as biochar provides less dependency 
on petrochemical resources while providing a sustainable 
waste management alternative. Application of biochar to soil 
can improve organic matter in soil [3 , 4]. Biochar presents 
other important features such as low cost, high surface area, 
porosity, high electrical conductivity, and high chemical 
stability [5], which have stimulated tremendous interest in 
preparation of the valuable activated carbon-based electrode 
materials for supercapacitors [6].
Replacing fossil fuels with renewable and sustainable 
resources is an urgent strategy for society to retard the global 
warming rate. Moreover, this changing strategy responds the 
ever-increasing global energy crises which are rising due 
to economic and population growth. It is critical to expand 
the production of sustainable and renewable clean energy 
technologies. The main renewable energy sources, includ-
ing wind, solar, and hydropower are fluctuating because of 
weather conditions. Therefore, there are unique challenges 
to store and supply renewable energy on a stable way. Elec-
trochemical supercapacitors (ES) are efficient energy storage 
systems for many applications due to their superior power 
density, stability, cycle lives, and fast charging/discharging 
ability without any risk of explosion [7, 8]. Supercapacitors 
are employed in various fields, such as transport systems, 
military devices, computers, and medical and industrial 
instruments [9 ]. By their charge storage process, all the

To reuse waste biomass in energy storage equipment, 
Cheng et al. prepared a series of porous carbons (CSCs) 
with high surface area (1754 to 1987  m2  g−1 ) by the car -
bonization of  ZnCl2-pretreated chestnut shells and evalu-
ated their electrochemical performances [12]. Among all 
the CSC-based supercapacitors, the sample CSC-7 exhib-
ited the best electrochemical performance with the spe-
cific capacitances of 105.4 F  g−1  at 0.1 A  g−1  and 92.0 
F  g−1  at 10 A  g−1 . Obtaining the capacitance as large as 
92.0 F  g−1  at high-current density is good, but the capaci-
tance improvements just by increasing the surface area 
were not remarkable. In another study, Cao et al. prepared 
porous activated carbon (AC) from corn stalk pith with the 
high-specific surface area (2495  m2  g−1 ) using potassium 
hydroxide [13]. The electrode made by this sample showed 
low inner resistance of 1.0 V and the specific capacitance 
of 323 F  g−1  in 6 mol  L−1  KOH electrolyte at a current 
density of 0.1 A  g−1 . The surface area enlargement resulted 
to high-specific capacitance. In 2019, a tubular porous car -
bon was prepared by Chen without any doping from straw 
cellulose waste with NaOH activation [14]. The prepared 
carbon had a high-specific surface area and displayed 
specific capacitance of 313 F  g−1  in 6 M KOH at 0.5 A 
 g−1  for supercapacitors. The tubular porous carbon-based 
supercapacitor device provided a high-energy density of 
8.67 Wh  kg−1  at a power density of 3.50 kW   kg−1  in 6 M 
KOH. Furthermore, Yakaboylu et al. prepared porous acti-
vated carbons from hybrid willow and miscanthus grass 
by pyrolysis, oxidative torrefaction, and KOH chemical 
activation [15]. Analyses of the prepared carbon revealed 
the presence of oxygen (about 11 to 19%), various sized 
porosity, and high surface area of 3265  m2/g. The prepared 
two-electrode symmetrical supercapacitors were stable 
in a 6 M KOH aqueous electrolyte for 1000 cycles and 
showed specific capacitance up to 162 F/g at 100 mA/g. 
In another study, Lin et al. prepared phosphorus-doped 
porous carbon from biomass sawdust [16]. The P-doped 
carbon material exhibited fine specific capacitances of 
292 F  g−1  at low current density 0.1 A  g−1  and the high-
energy density of 10.6 W h   kg−1 . Furthermore, Li et al. 
noticed the importance of templating agent and prepared 
porous carbons from sodium alginate and calcium algi-
nate to study the effect of different regulation component 
(sodium/calcium) on the electrochemical properties of the 
obtained charcoals as EDLC [17]. The higher capacitance 
of about 313 F  g−1  was obtained with sodium alginate-
derived porous carbon when the KOH/C ratios were 2 and 
4. The surface area of the prepared electrode material was 
very high with respect to the previously reported stud-
ies and the observed capacitance and power density were 
comparable; at low current density range (50 − 250 mA/g), 
the energy density, and power density were 17.5 W·h/kg 
and 209 W/kg, respectively. An alternative strategy that 
has been used to enhance the capacitive feature of AC is 
the functionalization. Bandosz et al. used melamine and 
urea to study the combined effect of N- and O-enriched 
AC of coconut shell on electrochemical performance of 
supercapacitors [18]. They found that EDLC behavior is 
mainly achieved with the O- and N-containing functional 
groups located in pores between 5 Å and 6 Å. Functional 
groups pyrrolic-N, pyridinic-N, and quinone-O showed the 
most enhancing effects on capacitance. Furthermore, Xing 
et al. have already reported the preparation of nanoporous 
carbons (the surface area 600–2600  m2  g−1  and pore-struc-
ture ~ 2.1 nm) from sunflower seed shell by impregnation-
activation/carbonization-activation processes and used 
as electrode material for EDLC in 30% KOH [19]. The 
carbons made by impregnation-activation process showed 
better capacitive behavior at high current density (244 F 
 g−1  at 0.25  Ag−1  and 171  Fg−1  at 10 A  g−1 ) which was 
ascribed to the abundant macroscopic pores and less inte-
rior micropore surface. The capacitive performances of 
these carbons are better than ordered mesoporous carbons.
In comparison to rechargeable batteries and fuel cells, 
the commercially available supercapacitors suffer from the 
low energy density (~ 10 Wh  kg−1 ) [20]. Therefore, there are 
plenty of research to improve the energy density of super -
capacitors [21]. In this study, to achieve a higher energy 
density for the supercapacitor without degrading cycle sta-
bility, functionalization of biomass based-AC with oxygen-
containing groups was applied. Carbon surface functional 
groups may contribute to the enhanced capacitance [22]. 
This research not only offers a method for the conversion of 
biomass waste into added-value products such as graphene 
and CNT, but also provides a highly efficient method for the 
functionalization of the carbonized biomass for enhanced 
electrochemical energy storage devices.

2.2  Preparation of functionalized porous carbons
The barley straw (BS) biomass was obtained from local 
farms in Iran. First, it was washed repeatedly with DI-water 
and dried in an oven at 90 °C for 24 h. The dried barley straw 
was milled to pass a 0.50 mm size screen. A two-step car -
bonization process was employed. The schematic prepara -
tion of the activated carbons is shown in Fig. 1. The BS was 
first carbonized to char at 500 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 
5 °C   min−1  under nitrogen flow in a tubular furnace. Then, 
 FeCl3‧6H2O and urea thoroughly mixed with the obtained 
char in char/FeCl 3‧6H2O mass ratio 3 and urea/biochar in 
mass ratios equal to 1, 2, and 3. The thoroughly mixed and 
grinded samples were carbonized and activated at 900 °C for 
2 h at a heating rate of 5 °C   min−1  under nitrogen flow. It was 
then allowed to cool down to room temperature naturally. 
After precarbonization and activation, the samples were 
washed sequentially with hydrochloric acid 6 M, HF (20%) 
and subsequently with plenty of deionized water until neu-
tral pH. The powders were finally dried at 105 °C for 12 h. 
For simplicity, in this study, we denote the urea with U. The 
obtained AC samples are designated as BCF for the sample 
without urea additive and BCF-U, BCF-2U, and BCF-3U, 
with the numeration corresponding to the mass ratio of urea

2.3  Electrochemical measurements
The electrochemical properties were studied using both the 
conventional two- and three-electrode systems with 6 M 
KOH or 1 M  Na2SO4 solution as the aqueous electrolyte. In 
the three-electrode system, the active carbon material was 
placed as the working electrode, and a Pt wire and Ag/AgCl 
electrode were used as the counter and reference electrodes, 
respectively. The slurry for working electrodes were made by 
mixing 80 wt % as-prepared carbon materials with 10 wt % 
conductive acetylene black and 10 wt % polyvinylidene fluo-
ride (PVDF) dissolved in N -methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP). 
The working electrode was prepared by coating the slurry 
on a piece of fresh nickel foam (1 cm × 1 cm) and dried at 
80 °C for 12 h. The mass loading of the active electrode 
was in the range of 0.005 g   cm−2 . All the electrochemical 
tests were carried out at room temperature. Galvanostatic 
charge − discharge (GCD), cycling tests, cyclic voltammetry 
(CV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) 
measurements were carried out in a potentiostat–galvanostat 
PGSTAT302N-Autolab tester. Electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted at open circuit potential 
in a frequency range from 10 m Hz to 100 kHz. The experi-
mental spectra of EIS were modeled numerically using the 
ZView equivalent circuit fitting program.
For the two-electrode system, two symmetrical elec-
trodes, each measuring 11-mm diameter were prepared the 
same way as described above with a Whatman filter paper 
separator in between the two electrodes, which was previ-
ously soaked in 6 M KOH or 1 M  Na2SO4 electrolyte. CV 
and GCD were used in evaluation of the capacitive per -
formance. The specific gravimetric capacitances (F/g) of 
a single electrode were calculated from the galvanostatic 
discharge curves, the area of the voltammograms, and EIS 
by means Eqs. (2) to (4), respectively:

accessible with low cost. As shown in Fig. 1, a two-step pro-

chemical activation by  FeCl3 is achieved in several steps.

All samples exhibit both type I and type IV isotherms

Chemistry. The adsorption isotherm of BCF-3U has a con-

indicates the presence of a large amount of micropores. 
On raising the pressure, the slope of the adsorption and 
the presence of hysteresis indicating the presence of

diagrams between p/p 0 = 0.2 to 0.6 show that total (BET) 
surface area and micropore area of the carbon compounds 
are increased by the increasing urea/biochar weight ratios. 
The average pore volume also increases with an increase 
in the urea/biochar weight ratio. This may be attributed to 
the expansion of the carbon lattice caused by functionali-
zation. The data derived from  N2 isotherms showed that 
oxidation by urea decreased the surface area for BCF-U 
(from 31  m2  g−1  for BCF to 25  m2  g−1  for BCF-U) and 
volume of the micropores for BCF-U and BCF-2U. Prob-
ably, functional groups block some pore or destroy the 
pore walls. It seems the limitations and error in porosity 
measurements cause that average pore size of BJH model 
to be the same for all the samples. In spite of that the pore 
diameters obtained by BET method are different. Although 
this method is not as accurate as BJH method, however, 
it shows that average pore size diameters of the various 
samples are not the same and the BCF-U has the high-
est pore diameter among the functionalized BS-derived 
carbons. The abundant macroscopic pores and less inte-
rior micropore surface may enhance capacitive behavior 
of BCF-U [19]. The wide ion transport paths ensure rapid 
electrolyte ions storage and diffusion. Ion-transport kinet-
ics in electrodes play a crucial function in the execution of 
supercapacitors. The ion-transport kinetics is influenced 
by the surface area and pore structure of the electrode. 
The presence of the mesopores and micropores provides 
a channel for the fast transport of electrolyte and fast ion 
adsorption due to high surface area, respectively [ 34]. 
Therefore, in addition to a high surface area, the conjunc-
tion of both micropores and mesopores is expected to have 
a major effect on the electrochemical performance of non-
Faradaic supercapacitors.

The prepared AC samples were analyzed for graphitic and

the Raman spectra of the samples excited by a laser at 
514 nm. The activated carbons show Raman peak of crys-
talline graphite centered at 1590   cm−1  (G-band) because of 
their symmetry, whereas amorphous particles exhibit broad 
peaks around 1333 (D-band) and 1590   cm−1  (G-band). The 
D band results from the out of plane vibrations of the disor-
dered  sp2 graphitic carbon components, while the G-band is 
attributed to the in-plane vibrations of ordered  sp2-bonded 
graphitic carbon atoms [40]. Therefore, the intensity ratio of 
D/G bands  (ID/IG) is used to assess the degree of defects and 
graphitization on the samples [41]. The used weight ratio of 
urea/biochar during the activation/functionalization process 
affects the degree of structural order in the synthesized AC. 
The  ID/IG value (~ 2.26) of the BCF-2U is lower than those 
of BCF-U (~ 3.02) and BCF-3U (~ 2.73), showing the higher 
graphitic degree for the BCF-2U compound. This finding 
was also confirmed by the XRD analysis (Fig. 5). The higher 
values of graphitization lead to the higher electrical conduc-
tivity [39].
3.4  FTIR analysis
The FTIR spectra of the functionalized samples and AC 
without functionalization are displayed in Fig.  7. The broad 
peak at 3443   cm−1  should be attributed to the fundamental 
vibrations of O − H unit of the adsorbed water and carboxylic 
acid groups. The stretching vibration of the phenolic O–H 
groups appears at 3745   cm−1 . The band appearing in the 
region 1536–1640   cm−1  can be ascribed to C = C aromatic 
skeletal vibrations. The peak at ~ 1740   cm−1  is due to the 
C = O groups [42]. The band at 1262  cm−1  is assigned to the 
C − O stretching vibration and C–O–H bending vibrations. 
The band related to the aromatic ether group (= C–O–C) is 
located at 1026   cm−1 . It can be clearly seen that these peaks 
of BCF-U, BCF-2U, and BCF-3U are significantly stronger 
than those of BCF in the region 2000–400   cm−1 . This result 
led to the conclusion that the predicted oxygen contain-
ing polar groups have been introduced on the surfaces of 
the synthesized carbon material via the functionalization 
by urea. The oxygenation of a carbon surface by oxidation 
may introduce carboxyl (–CO 2H), lactone, lactol, phenol, 
or anhydride oxygen-containing functional groups that are

3.5  Electrochemical performance
3.5.1  Cyclic voltammetry
To explore the advantages of functionalized AC materials 
and their potential applications as electrodes for superca-
pacitors, the electrochemical properties of AC electrodes 
were measured in 6 M KOH using a three-electrode system.
As shown in Fig.  8a, the CV curves of AC materi-
als at scan rate of 100 mV   s−1  display quasi-rectangular 
curves in the voltage window from − 1.0 to 0 V, suggest-
ing that CV behaviors result from predominantly double-
layer mechanism of energy storage. The voltammograms 
become lens-shaped at lower scan rate of 10 mV   s−1  due 
to the limiting of capacitive contribution (Fig.  8b). The 
rectangular shape of the CV curve at a scan rate as high 
as 100 mV   s−1  indicates that charging and discharging 
are occurred at the same rate because of fast ion transport 
within prepared electrode materials [ 49]. The results 
indicate that functionalized ACs have better ion trans-
port property than BCF sample at higher and lower scan 
rates. For BCF the data indicate that the rate for charging 
and discharging of the system are not the same. Further -
more, close consideration of the CV curves at lower scan 
rate (10 mV   s−1 ) shows the emergence of small (redox) 
peaks in voltammograms of BCF and the involvement of 
non-capacitive (faradaic) behavior [ 50, 51]. It is clear that 
the pseudocapacitive peaks at—0.50 and—0.20 V reflect -
ing the non-reversible reduction/oxidation of functional 
groups [18] which are the most pronounced in BCF. The 
typical rectangular CV curves (Fig.  8a) are found espe-
cially for samples with higher urea/biochar weight ratio, 
indicating that they have excellent capacitance charac-
teristics which are reversible. In addition, functionalized 
AC maintained the shape of the curve better than BCF at 
the low or higher scan rates, which indicates that doping 
oxygen on the surface of porous carbon will improve its 
EDLC property. The specific capacity of the material had 
no regularity at all. Consideration of the CV curves at 
10 mV   s−1  shows that BCF-U electrode has the highest 
current and capacitance C CV (Fig.  8b). While at 10 mV/s 
the CV curve area order is BCF-U > BCF-2U > BCF-
3U > BCF, at 100 mV/s the CV curve area order becomes 
BCF-2U > BCF-3U > BCF > BCF-U. The sample with a 
small amount of the urea additive (BCF-U) appeared to 
have a much larger area at the scan rate 10 mV/s, which 
indicates that the capacitance is greatly enhanced. Inter -
estingly, the  SBET of BCF-U (25  m2  g−1 ) was lower than 
those of BCF-2U (112  m2  g−1 ), BCF-3U (257  m2  g−1 ), 
and BCF (31); illustrating that adding a small amount of 
urea can achieve oxygen doping in porous carbon mate-
rials, adjust pore size distribution and/or shapes, and 
induce the formation of nanostructures carbon materials 
which improve its EDLC property. It is worth noting that 
although  SBET is an important parameter for capacitors, 
not all pore structures are suitable for entering electrolyte 
ions. Additionally, high  SBET may increase the risk of 
electrolyte decomposition during charging and discharg-
ing [52]. In spite of its lower specific surface area, the

the strong  Na+-H2Od−  interactions caused by the large sur -
face charge density; the ionic mobility of  Na+ decrease with 
respect to  K+. The higher conductivity and ionic mobility 
help in fast charge transfer and more ion adsorption occur at 
the electrolyte/electrode interface for the smaller hydration 
sphere radius. Furthermore, comparison of the electrolyte 
anions on the electrochemical performance of a carbon elec-
trode has proved that the conductivity (198  cm2 Ω  mol−1 )

2‒ anion [67 ]. Hence, 
the higher capacitive behavior in KOH electrolyte is due 
to the higher conductivity and ionic mobility of  OH‒ ions. 
Additionally, the radius of the hydration sphere of  OH‒ is 
smaller than that of  SO4
2−  ions which leads to the forma-
tion of a higher electric double layer by increasing in the 
quantity of ions entering into the pores. Therefore, KOH 
aqueous electrolyte is suitable for the synthesized samples to 
achieve higher specific capacity value with respect to aque-
ous  Na2SO4 electrolyte at current density 2 A  g−1 .
3.5.3  Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)
To realize the facilitated ion and electron transport of the 
prepared electrodes in 6 M KOH electrolyte, the EIS tests 
were carried out in the open circuit voltage. The Nyquist plot 
of the electrodes and the corresponding equivalent circuit 
obtained by ZView software are shown in Fig. 10 and in the 
inset of Fig.  10, respectively; the relevant parameters are 
listed in Table  3 and Fig. S3. The whole capacitor circuit is 
composed of RS, Rct, ZW, and constant phase element CPE. 
The high frequency of the real axis intercept indicates the 
sum of the contact resistance and material resistance  (Rs), 
which results from the bulk solution, contact resistance of 
the electrode/electrolyte interface, and material resist -
ance (electrode active materials, electrolyte conductivity, 
and separator). The R ct is the width of the semicircle that 
appears at high frequencies. The Warburg resistance ZW (in 
the middle frequencies, the 45° slope of the line) is related 
to the ion diffusion/transport in the electrolyte, while the 
Rct is the electrode–electrolyte interface resistance to the 
redox reaction occurs by adsorption–desorption of ions at 
the electrode interface (a Faradaic reaction). Moreover, the 
CPE is affected by surface roughness, chemical inhomoge-
neity, and a heterogeneous electrode–electrolyte interface 
caused by ion adsorption [68]. CPE1 and CPE2 represent 
charge transfer capacitor and contact double layer capacitor 
layers formed during the charge–discharge process, respec-
tively [69]. As is shown in Fig.  10, the functionalized AC 
electrodes show similar equivalent series resistance (ESR 
or Rs) that are higher than that of the BCF sample (0.01 Ω). 
It means that the resistance caused by the electrolyte and 
the contact resistance of these functionalized AC electrodes 
were similar. As can be seen from the fitted results, the 
BCF-U electrode has the highest R ct (14.68 Ω), but a very 
low Warburg resistance (not detectable). The highest specific 
capacity of BCF-U is due to its efficient adsorbing and ion 
transportation of the electrolyte. BCF-U is composed of a
