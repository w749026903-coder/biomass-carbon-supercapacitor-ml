Valorizing palm date wastes for 
supercapacitor electrode synthesis 
and evaluation
Amany Mostafa1, Sodky H. Mohammed2, Nouby M. Ghazaly1 & Ahmed ElSheikh 1
Climate and energy issues continue to drive researchers to develop fossil-free energy technologies that 
are both sustainable and scalable. In this work, date seeds (DS) are used to produce activated carbon 
(DSAC), which is then used as a supercapacitor electrode. The synthesis method uses KOH activation 
coupled with argon-gas flow calcination at 950 °C. The produced DSAC has a high specific surface area 
of 1374 m2/g and a turbostratic microporous structure (pore size = 1.14 nm), in addition to oxygen-
containing functional groups (–C–O–, –OH). The DSAC electrochemical performance exhibits an electric 
double-layer capacitor (EDLC) with a minor pseudocapacitive contribution and delivers a 3-electrode 
specific capacitance of 1800 F/g at 4 A/g (estimated full-device specific capacitance is 450 F/g, energy, 
and power densities: 40 Wh/kg and 1440 W/kg, respectively). These numbers are remarkably higher 
than the current state-of-the-art carbon supercapacitor findings. Furthermore, it retains 83.3% of its 
initial discharge capacity after 2000 galvanostatic charge-discharge cycles at 10 A/g. Finally, the DSAC 
electrode performance is slightly enhanced with increasing the KOH concentration from 1 M to 3 M.
Keywords Biomass, Activated carbon, Supercapacitor, KOH activation
The climate change and energy supply crises continue to call for accelerated research and development efforts 
of sustainable energy technologies1. This is particularly true in electric mobility, where supercapacitors and Li-
ion batteries play a vital role. Supercapacitors or electrochemical capacitors are electrochemical cells comprised 
of two electrodes, a separator, and an electrolyte. Their inherent merits, which occasionally enable them to 
outperform Li-ion batteries, include fast charging and discharging, high power density, and long cycle life 2. 
There are two main mechanisms for storing electrical charge in supercapacitors: electric double-layer capacitors 
(EDLCs) and pseudocapacitors. The former adopts the electrostatic adsorption and desorption of electrolyte 
ions at the electrode-electrolyte interface, forming the double-layer configuration. The latter is achieved via a 
reversible redox charge-transfer reaction at the electrode-electrolyte interface. Occasionally, both mechanisms 
occur simultaneously in a single material, combining the advantages of both 3. Furthermore, supercapacitors 
could be classified based on their materials into metal oxide, polymer, and carbon-based. Unlike oxide and 
polymer-based supercapacitors, carbon-based supercapacitors have demonstrated stable performance in 
addition to their cost-effectiveness4.
Biomass-based electrodes have garnered considerable attention due to their inherent properties, such 
as raw material abundance, high surface area, chemical stability, high specific surface area, low weight, and 
interconnected porous structure5. Wood residues, coconut shells, apricot pits, agricultural wastes, and marine 
algae are some of the examples of biomass precursors that have been extensively studied in the preparation of 
porous activated carbons with well-developed micro/mesoporous structure that can be used in supercapacitor 
applications6–11. The chemical activation process can be used to produce hierarchical porous carbons with a 
large surface area and tunable pore structure using these lignocellulosic resources. Recent research work has 
shown that carbons produced by biomass have the potential to provide promising electrochemical properties 
when optimized activation conditions are used and thus can serve as sustainable alternatives to standard carbon 
materials. As an example, it has been reported that porous carbons obtained using many natural precursors as 
hydrogen storage and energy-related materials have been investigated in the past, thus showing the success of the 
chemical activation strategies in the development of pore structure and surface chemistry12,13.
A palm date tree (Phoenix dactylifera) generates 40 kg of waste from leaves, surface fibres surrounding the 
trunk, and fruit-bearing seeds per year 14. Palm dates are a crucial food whose total production reached 9.66 
million metric tons in 2024, with the Middle East countries (especially Egypt) as the top producers due to ideal

seed-derived activated carbon2, the authors have mixed seeds of olive and date seeds, and later added sugar to

highest specific capacitance of 528 F/g at 0.3 A/g with power and energy densities of 107 W/kg and 37 Wh/kg.
Recent studies confirmed that date-seed activated carbon (DSAC) is a promising and viable electrode 
substance in supercapacitors since it combines cost-effectiveness, environmental compatibility, large surface 
area, and porosity control. In a study of this type, a DSAC was assembled with Ni-doped α-Bi₂O₃ to form a 
hybrid electrode; the carbon part of the electrode provided electronic conductivity, increased ion-accessible 
surface area, and strengthened charge transportation, and the Ni-doped oxide part of the electrode provided 
pseudocapacitive redox properties. Accordingly, the Ni-doped α-Bi₂O₃ /DSAC composite exhibited a specific 
capacitance of 174 F/g at 5 mV/s, which is better than the oxide on its own and supports the positive synergy of 
electric double layer capacitance and faradaic charge storage 15. In another work DSAC was confirmed to give 
a parallel trend to a Zn 2O3/AC composite; their activation has significantly increased the BET surface area of 
1.6779 to 114.17 m2/g, promoted a porous nanosheet-like structure, reduced the charge transfer resistance, and 
provided a high specific capacitance of 281 F/g at 5 mV/s 16. In a different related study, discarded dates were 
transformed into porous carbon aerogels by hydrothermal pretreatment, freeze-drying, and KOH activation, 
which offered a three-dimensional heteroatom-rich porous structure with a specific surface area of 1348.87⁻¹ m 
2 g− 1 , the optimized material had a high capacitance of 769 F/g at 0.5 A/g, highlighting the high energy-storage 
capability of porous DSAC17.
Although date-seed-derived carbons have been previously reported for supercapacitor applications 14,18–21, 
most studies focus primarily on demonstrating feasibility rather than systematically optimizing activation 
conditions and correlating pore structure with electrochemical performance. In this work, emphasis is placed 
on tailoring the activation strategy and elucidating the structure–performance relationship to achieve enhanced 
capacitive behavior.
Experimental
Chemicals and methods
The raw material is date seed (DS) waste from households in Qena, Egypt. The chemicals used in this synthesis 
are KOH, HCl, poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) as a binder, and dimethylformamide (DMF) as a solvent, in 
addition to argon and nitrogen gases. The devices are a D8 Advance XRD diffractometer (Bruker, Germany), 
a Confocal Raman Microscope (WiTec, 300R alpha, Germany), an ST2 on NOV A touch 4LX equipment (SN 
17016062702), an FEI Quanta 250, Thermo Fisher Scientific (Czech Republic), a ATR-FTIR Bruker Alpha IFS-
125, a JEM-2100 (Japan), and a Quartz Kejia Tube furnace. For the evaluation of electrochemical capacitors, an 
Origalys OFG + 500 potentiostat/galvanostat (France) was used.
Synthesis of date seed activated carbon (DSAC)
Figure 1 exhibits the synthetic procedure of activated carbon synthesis from date seeds. Household DSs were 
collected, washed with tap water, and dried. Then, they were carbonized at 350 °C for 4 h in a muffle furnace in 
an air atmosphere. The pre-carbonization was designed to remove volatiles, promote initial carbon framework 
formation, and introduce some oxygen functionalities. Following, DSs were manually ground using a zirconia 
planetary ball mill at 400 rpm for 2 h, followed by 30-minute hot sonication (50 °C) to dissolve the carbon in 
distilled water. Thereafter, they were chemically activated using KOH, since it is a versatile method to activate

conductivity, a desirable property for supercapacitor applications. For example, increasing the temperature 
from 800 °C to 1000 °C enhances the graphitic order 25–27. The argon flow was 350 sccm. The temperature was 
increased from room temperature to 950 °C within 30 min, corresponding to a relatively rapid heating ramp. 
Such a heating profile can influence the activation pathway by affecting volatile release, KOH etching kinetics, 
pore widening, and carbon framework contraction. The activated carbon powder was rinsed with 0.1 M HCL to 
neutralize the KOH, followed by a cold water-ethanol mixture bath washing. Subsequently, it was dried at 90 °C 
for 24 h. The DSAC yield following this method is 41%.
Physiochemical characterization
X-ray diffraction (XRD) data of the samples were collected with a D8 Advance with Davinci design (Bruker, 
Germany), using an X-ray source the Cu Kα radiation (wavelength λ = 1.5418 Å), at 40 kV and 40 mA, a 2θ range 
of 20–80°, a step size of 0.02°, and a time/step of 0.6 s. A Si zero-background sample holder operated by Diffrac 
was used. Raman spectroscopy was applied to study the structure of the produced carbon. A confocal Raman 
spectrometer equipped with a laser source of 532 nm was utilized to collect the spectra. N2 adsorption/desorption 
measurements, at 77.35°K, utilizing the St 2 on NOV A touch 4LX equipment to analyze the Brunauer-Emmett-
Teller (BET) surface area and porosity, were performed. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-
SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) were performed using FEI Quanta 250, Thermo Fisher 
Scientific. The ATR-FTIR measurement was carried out by Fourier transformation infrared spectrophotometer 
model Bruker Alpha IFS-125 in the wavenumber range 400–4000  cm − 1, scan time = 32, resolution = 4.High-
resolution transmission microscopy (HRTEM) was performed utilizing JEOL JEM-2100. The suspension of the 
material was sonicated for 10 min on an ultrasonicator (Crest Ultrasonics Corp., USA). Then, a few drops were 
loaded onto a carbon-coated copper grid and left to dry.

Results and discussions
Physiochemical characterization
The DSAC yield for the current work is 41%, close to other reported yields in28 , 29. However, it is slightly lower, 
likely due to the 350 °C air precarbonation. The XRD pattern (Fig. 2. A) shows a broad hump around ~ 23–26° 
(2θ) corresponding to the (002) plane of turbostratic/amorphous carbon and a weak, broad peak around 44° 
associated with the (100) and (101) planes of disordered graphite-like domains. The broad diffraction peak 
observed at ~ 23–26° (2θ), corresponding to the (002) plane, was used to estimate the interlayer spacing (002) via 
Bragg’s law, yielding values in the range of ~ 0.34–0.38 nm. This spacing is slightly larger than that of crystalline 
graphite (~ 0.335 nm), indicating a turbostratic and disordered carbon structure with expanded interlayer 
distance. These diffraction features are characteristic of poorly ordered graphitic carbon and are consistent with 
the standard ICDD Powder Diffraction File (PDF) card No. 41-1487 for graphitic carbon. Similar patterns were 
obtained with other DSAC samples3,14,30,31. The absence of any other minor sharp or broad peaks signals the clean 
carbon production. The high intensity at low diffraction angles (< 30°) does not reflect structural information but

This confirms that the DSAC possesses a hierarchical micro–mesoporous structure with moderate adsorption

samples1 and significantly higher than others2,3,5. This indicates the 950 °C calcination did not harm the carbon 
porous structure, unlike26. The Raman spectrum (Fig. 2. B) shows prominent D (~ 1350 cm −1 ) and G (~ 1580 
cm−1 ) bands with ID/IG ≈ 0.98, indicating highly disordered, defect-rich turbostratic carbon with small graphitic 
domains. The weak 2D band confirms poor graphitization, consistent with porous biomass-derived activated 
carbon, which is suitable for electrochemical applications. Similar results are reported in 28,30,31. The EDX (Fig. 
2. E) shows the elemental composition of DSAC. Typically, biomass-derived carbons after KOH activation 
contain: C (dominant framework of the material, O (surface functionalities of OH, COOH, C = O), residual 
KOH reaction K, and minor inorganics (Si, Ca, Mg) from natural biomass ash. DSAC is constituted mainly 
from C (77.7 at%), O (19.8 at%), and K (1.89 at%). The detected K content (~ 1.89 at%) is attributed to residual 
potassium species remaining after KOH activation. Despite acid washing, trace potassium may remain trapped 
within micropores or bound to surface functional groups. The absence of crystalline K-containing phases in 
XRD indicates that potassium exists in a highly dispersed form rather than as a distinct phase. The DS dense 
precursor structure allows KOH etching but resists deep intercalation, resulting in a cleaner surface. Figure 2. 
F shows the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectrum of DSAC. The FTIR spectrum of DSAC 
shows a broad band at ~ 3400 cm⁻¹ corresponding to O–H stretching, indicating hydroxyl groups and adsorbed 
moisture, while weak peaks at ~ 2920–2850 cm −1  arise from aliphatic C–H vibrations. The band near ~ 1600 
cm−1  reflects aromatic C = C and/or C = O groups, confirming partially graphitized carbon. Peaks in the ~ 
1200–1000 cm⁻¹ region are attributed to C–O stretching (phenols/ethers), evidencing oxygen-containing surface 
functionalities. Overall, the spectrum indicates a defect-rich, oxygen-functionalized carbon structure suitable 
for pseudocapacitive behavior. Although EDX and FTIR analyses confirm the presence of oxygen-containing 
functional groups, XPS analysis would provide more detailed information regarding their chemical states and 
relative abundance, which will be considered in future work.
To analyze the DSAC surface topography and morphology, SEM and HRTEM analyses were performed 
(Fig. 3). The SEM images show irregular, fractured carbon particles with rough, etched surfaces (Fig. 3. A). At 
low magnification, angular particles and interparticle voids indicate macroporosity that aids mass transport. At 
higher magnification (Fig. 3. B), a sponge-like surface with pits and cavities reveals mesopores and pore openings. 
Although micropores are not directly visible, their presence is implied by activation. Overall, the material exhibits 
a hierarchical pore structure (macro–meso–micro), suitable for adsorption and electrochemical applications. 
The average pore size (~ 1.14 nm) indicates dominant microporosity, which is favorable for charge storage due to 
the increased surface area and effective ion confinement. Notably, the pore size is well matched with the hydrated 
ion size of KOH electrolyte (~ 0.3–0.4 nm), facilitating efficient ion adsorption within micropores. In addition, 
the presence of mesopores provides ion transport pathways and reduces diffusion resistance, particularly at 
high scan rates. This hierarchical micro–mesoporous structure enables a synergistic effect, leading to enhanced 
capacitance and rate performance. The TEM image (Fig. 3. C) shows thin, wrinkled, and turbostratic carbon

Electrode evaluation
To evaluate the DSAC electrochemical performance, a three-electrode system was assembled and attached to 
an OFG + 500 potentiostat/galvanostat to run the cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge and discharge 
(GCD), and stability tests. The applied electrolytes are 1 M and 3 M KOH. All CV and GCD scans were performed 
four times to ensure reproducibility. Figure 4a exhibits the cyclic voltammograms of the DSAC electrode at 5, 10, 
20, 50, 100, and 200 mV/s. Quasi-rectangular voltammograms are observed for almost all scan rates up to 100 
mV/s, which signals the favorable capacitive performance of the produced DSAC. The absence of pronounced 
redox peaks suggests that the charge storage process is primarily dominated by electric double-layer capacitance 
(either at the electrode-electrolyte interface or inside the porous structure), although surface functional groups 
may contribute to additional pseudocapacitive behavior32. It is noteworthy that some minor broad humps appear 
with increasing the scan rate at 100 and 200 mV/s (Fig. 4A), which might be attributed to the oxygen-groups-
associated redox reactions.
Upon increasing the scan rate, however, there is a notable diversion from the rectangular shape, which 
could be attributed to the decreasing contact between the ions and the electrode. As confirmed by the EDX and 
FTIR analyses (Fig. 2E, F), the prepared carbon contains a significant amount of oxygen functionalities (e.g., 
–OH, C–O, and C = O groups). These groups are known to contribute to pseudocapacitive behavior in alkaline

T o understand the charge storage kinetics, the Dunn method was applied7. The Dunn method is a standard 
and effective tool for elucidating charge-storage mechanisms in supercapacitor electrodes and guiding the design 
and optimization of materials to improve performance. Equation (8) was applied to estimate both the capacitive 
(surface-controlled) and diffusion-controlled contributions:
 𝑖𝑖(𝑣𝑣) = 𝑘𝑘𝑣𝑣𝑘 𝑘𝑘𝑣𝑣0.5 (8)
The 𝑘𝑘1𝑣𝑣 represents the capacitive (surface-controlled) current contribution, while the 𝑘𝑘2𝑣𝑣0.5 represents the 
diffusion-controlled contribution. The k1 and k2 coefficients are determined from the linear fit of the i/v0.5 
versus v0.5 plot, as illustrated in Fig.  5a. Following, the surface-controlled (capacitive) and diffusion-controlled 
contributions could be estimated at each scan rate, and that is plotted in Fig. 5b.
In contrast to another study 7, the diffusion-controlled contribution is dominating the charge-transfer of 
the current DSAC, which could be directly correlated to its interconnected porous structure, with micropores 
dominating it. But both works agree that increasing the scan rate decreases the diffusion-controlled contribution 
and increases the capacitive one. Figure 5c demonstrates that at 10 mV/s, only 10% of the specific capacitance is 
contributed by the surface capacitive interactions, while the remaining is contributed by the diffusion-controlled 
processes.
Figure 6a shows the stability testing of the DSAC electrode via a 2000-cycle GCD test at 10 A/g in 1 M KOH. 
It shows that the DSAC electrode retains 83.3% of its initial capacity after 2000 discharge cycles. The 10-A/g 
current density was chosen because lower currents would take a few days to charge/discharge < 2000 cycles, 
which proves the high capacity to store electric charges. The 17% loss of capacitive performance after 2000 cycles 
is relatively high compared to conventional > 90% capacity retention after 5000–10,000 GCD cycles, which calls 
for deeper investigation. Although the exact reasons for the capacity decay cannot be accurately identified, several 
insights could be drawn. First, the pseudocapacitive and the diffusion-controlled EDLC (inside the microporous 
channels) contributions are significant, as shown in Fig. 6. The pseudocapacitive contribution stems from the 
surface oxygen-functional groups (Fig. 2E, F). Unfortunately, the ion uptake into the electrode material during 
oxidation causes swelling and strain, generating internal stresses and forming cracks. Furthermore, the surface 
oxygen groups might experience degradation with repeated redox reactions. Such changes could reduce the 
available surface area and ion accessibility for further charge/discharge cycles34.
Alternatively, the applied PVDF binder strength of adhesion with the carbon matrix might have been 
weakened with the repeated GCD cycles. Other DSAC papers have found similar decays in the capacitive 
performance of the DSAC supercapacitor 7. In another study 28 that used banana peel activated with H 3PO4 to 
develop a Zn-ion hybrid supercapacitor, also 83% (initial capacity is 70 F/g) of the capacity was retained after 
50,000 cycles at 1 A/g.
To study the effect of increasing the KOH electrolyte concentration on the DSAC electrode performance, CV 
(Fig. 6b) and GCD tests in 3 M KOH were performed. Figure  6c exhibits the GCD of DSAC in KOH 1 M and 
3 M at 4 A/g, which demonstrates an increase in the capacity overall in the case of 3 M compared to 1 M KOH. 
The GCD results indicate that 3 M KOH exhibits longer charge–discharge times, suggesting higher capacitance 
due to increased ionic conductivity and higher utilized surface area in the KOH 3 M case.
To evaluate the performance novelty in this work, two parameters: specific surface area (m 2/g) and 
2-electrode specific capacitance (F/g), were chosen to compare the current material performance to previously-
published articles. In general, the electrochemical performance of the current DSAC electrode surpasses most 
of the published reports of biomass-derived activated carbon supercapacitor electrodes. Y et, to make the 
comparison reliable and meaningful, Refs1,3,5,15–17 were chosen to compare with since the same raw material and 
supercapacitor application are pursued, while the synthesis and experimental measurements are not identical. 
Figure 7 shows the comparison, and it demonstrates that the current DSAC electrode is one of the best in terms 
of its surface area and specific capacitance.
The comparison presented in Fig. 7 provides a visual assessment of the relationship between surface area 
and specific capacitance for date-seed-derived carbon materials reported in the literature. It is evident that 
capacitance is not solely governed by surface area; for example, some studies report high surface areas but 
relatively moderate capacitance1, indicating that not all surface area is electrochemically accessible. In contrast,

ion-transport pathways, and other pseudocapacitive contributions to capacitance by metal oxides in some 
studies. The high surface area and a turbostratic/defect-rich carbon structure in the current study indicate the

that capacitance is more sensitive to the accessibility of the pore and structural characteristics rather than to the

The present DSAC demonstrates a favorable balance between surface area and electrochemical performance,

charge storage. This highlights that pore accessibility and structural characteristics play a more critical role than 
surface area alone in determining electrochemical performance.
Conclusions
Date seeds were successfully valorized and upgraded to produce a high-surface-area and functionalized 
activated carbon (DSAC) that can be used to fabricate a hybrid supercapacitor electrode. The prepared DSAC 
has a turbostratic amorphous and hierarchical porous structure combining micro- and mesopores with 1374 
m2/g with functional oxygen groups. The electrochemical evaluation findings ensure that the DSAC has a hybrid 
EDLC-pseudocapacitive charge storage behavior with the pseudocapacitive contribution dominating. The 
3-electrode-calculated specific capacitance is remarkably 1800 F/g (450 F/g of the full device). at 4 A/g. Also, 
the DSAC material can retain 83.3% of its initial capacity after 2000 GCD cycles. Finally, the prepared DSAC 
material achieves a better performance in the 3 M KOH M concentration than the concentrated 1 M one.
