Abstract
Water electrolysis is the dominant approach for producing green hydrogen from renewable 
energy, providing an alternative to fossil fuels. Conventional water electrolysis faces many 
problems due to the intermittent nature of renewable power and the need for a membrane to 
avoid the formation of an explosive gas mixture. To avoid the use of a membrane, the 
decoupled water electrolysis approach has been introduced, where one catalyst electrode is 
replaced by a solid redox mediator. So far, redox mediators for decoupled electrolysis have 
been prepared using fluoropolymer binders, which pose a serious environmental burden and 
are facing a ban. This is a limiting factor for further implementation of emerging decoupled 
water electrolysis technology. In this work, we report a binder-free charcoal electrode for use 
as a redox mediator electrode in acid and alkaline decoupled water electrolysis. The binder-
free redox mediator electrodes were prepared by a solvent-free spark plasma sintering. The 
sintering temperature was optimised to obtain a volumetric redox mediator with a high specific 
capacitance of 175 F g-1 and a specific charge capacity of 41 mAh g-1 at a current density of 50 
mA g-1. The observed values are higher than reported in the literature for volumetric carbon-
based supercapacitor materials. 
Keywords: Spark plasma sintering, decoupled electrolysis, activated charcoal, double-layer 
capacitor.
Introduction
The energy sector is currently undergoing a transformation from fossil to renewable energy 
sources (solar, wind, hydropower, tidal, etc.) [1]. Renewable energy is inexhaustible, and it 
does not pollute the environment. However, they are characterised by intermittency. Energy 
consumers require stability, availability, and energy security; therefore, there must be a stable, 
efficient, and secure connection between the renewable energy production plant and consumers. 
One of the most promising is “green hydrogen”.

Green hydrogen is hydrogen produced by water electrolysis powered by renewable energy 
sources. Since 2021, green hydrogen production has risen by 50 %, reaching a production 
capacity of 1 million tonnes per year (Mtpa). In parallel, the installed capacity of water 
electrolysers has grown ninefold, and electrolyser production has increased fourfold, 
highlighting the growing role of hydrogen in the global energy transition. The International 
Energy Agency (IEA) has also strongly recommended further increases in green hydrogen 
production [2].
In water electrolysis, water is split into hydrogen and oxygen. A conventional electrolysis cell 
consists of two metal catalyst electrodes (anode and cathode) immersed in an aqueous 
electrolyte. An oxygen evolution reaction (OER) occurs on the anode, and a hydrogen 
evolution reaction (HER) occurs on the cathode. As a result, hydrogen and oxygen are 
produced by consuming electricity and splitting water.
Currently, the highest technology readiness levels (TRLs) available for low-temperature 
electrolysis are alkaline electrolysers (AWE), proton exchange membrane electrolysers 
(PEMWE), and anion exchange membrane electrolysers (AEMWE). The voltage efficiencies 
of these electrolysers range from 60-80 % for AWE, 65-82 % for PEMWE, and 52-67 % for 
AEMWE [3]. 
All of the aforementioned water electrolysers use membranes or diaphragms to separate the 
cathode and anode and prevent the formation of an explosive mixture of oxygen and hydrogen. 
There are several challenges for existing membrane-based water electrolysis. The diaphragm, 
or membrane, typically does not exceed 0.5 mm in thickness, which causes cross-diffusion of 
hydrogen and oxygen. Moreover, oxygen is released at twice the volume of hydrogen; therefore, 
pressure management is required to avoid membrane deformation and rupture. In addition, 
membranes used in water electrolysis increase the overpotential for gas generation due to the 
resistance of ion exchange and transport across the membrane [4]. Membranes are made from 
harsh fluoropolymers, and their manufacturing and exploitation cause severe environmental 
issues [5].
For this reason, decoupled water electrolysis (DWE) has recently been proposed to split water 
without using membranes [6]. In this concept, one of the metal/catalyst electrodes in a water 
electrolysis cell is replaced by a solid redox electrode, and the membrane is removed. The 
process is typically cyclic. In one cycle, the OER reaction occurs at the metal electrode while 
the redox electrode is reduced. In the second cycle, the HER reaction occurs at the metal 
electrode, and the redox electrode is oxidised [7]. In such a process, simultaneous release of 
oxygen and hydrogen is avoided. 
Redox mediator electrode selection and design are critical for the successful construction of a 
DWE device for H 2 production. Turning attention to solid-state redox electrodes, they are 
typically charge-storage electrodes that can be divided into three major groups according to the 
charge-storage mechanism: battery-type [8], pseudocapacitive [9], and electric double-layer 
capacitance electrodes [10]. Electrodes must meet specific criteria for successful decoupled 
electrolysis. Redox potentials of electrode materials should lie between the OER and HER 
onset potentials; the materials should be durable for long-term redox cycling, non-corrosive in

the environment, non-toxic, and readily available. Battery pseudocapacitive electrodes have 
been the most studied since 2013 [11], when Cronin first proposed the concept of decoupled 
water electrolysis [12]. Both organic and inorganic electrodes have been demonstrated in 
alkaline [13,14], acidic [15,16], and amphoteric conditions [17,18], achieving 70-95% 
efficiency with 100% faradaic efficiency, good stability, cyclability, and durability. 
A high current density (0.952 A cm -2) in DWE has been achieved using an electric double-
layer (EDL) charge/discharge mediator based on metal-free carbon [19,20]. EDL 
charge/discharge occurs without a Faradaic redox process, unlike a reduction-oxidation process, 
thus eliminating diffusion limitations within active material. The EDL capacitance is purely a 
function of the electrode area, and by using activated large-surface carbon materials, it is 
possible to achieve large specific surfaces and increase the power-to-volume ratio of the 
electrolysis device [21,22].
The significant drawback for redox mediators is the need for a fluoropolymer binder to prepare 
the electrode material [23]. Binder significantly affects the electrode's conductivity and blocks 
the active surface, thus limiting overall capacity, and introduces the electrode's dead mass [24]. 
Moreover, fluoropolymers raise significant environmental issues and are expected to be banned 
in the EU soon [25]. For this reason, attention has been paid to the synthesis of binder-free 
electrodes. The binder-free carbon electrodes for supercapacitors have been made by drop-
casting [26], thermal pyrolysis of phenolic resin-impregnated cellulose paper [21], and vacuum 
filtration [27]. The various methods are summarised in a review article [28]. However, these 
approaches can hardly be up-scaled, and prepared electrode materials have not been tested as 
redox mediators in DWE.
Herein, we demonstrate the use of spark plasma sintering (SPS) to produce binder-free carbon 
redox mediators for both acidic and alkaline DWE. We will discuss the effects of processing 
parameters on sample microstructure and how these effects are reflected in electrochemical 
performance in DWE. 
2. Experimental
2.1. Preparation of carbon redox mediator materials
Carbon redox mediator materials were prepared from activated charcoal powder (Sigma 
Aldrich, 100 mesh, 161551) as demonstrated in Figure S1. Samples were prepared using a Dr 
Sinter SPS 825 (SPS Syntex, Japan). Sample series with sintering temperature 500-1000 °C 
were prepared.  1.6 g of activated charcoal was poured into a 20 mm graphite mould lined with 
0.35 mm graphite paper. The outside of the mould was covered with a 4.5 mm-thick carbon 
fibre thermal insulation material, held in place by a carbon fibre cord. A built-in thermocouple 
was used to measure temperature during sintering. The graphite mould was closed by graphite 
disks. After inserting the mould into the SPS machine, the distance between the electrodes was 
reduced until the top electrode contacted the top graphite disk. The pressure applied to the 
sample was then gradually increased to 38 kg cm -2. The atmospheric pressure in the chamber 
was reduced to approximately 6 Pa, and the sample was held for 20 min until no further 
shrinkage was observed. In the SPS process temperature was increased by approximately

2.2. Characterisation of physical properties
Sample morphology was studied by scanning electron microscopy (SEM/STEM, Verios 5 UC, 
Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Before SEM measurements, samples were crushed to access 
their cross-sections. Chemical compositions before and after sintering were analysed by X-ray 
fluorescence spectrometer (XRF, Rigaku Supermini, Japan) in a helium atmosphere, as well as 
by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Escalab Xi +, ThermoScientific, USA). The SPS 
tablets were ground prior to XPS measurements, and the powders were pressed into the indium 
substrate. The carbon peak at 284.8 eV was used as a calibration point at XPS to correct the 
peak shift. Peaks were fitted using Avantage 5.2 software.
The specific surface area and pore distribution of the SPS tablets were determined using an N2 
adsorption porosimeter (Quantachrome Nova 1200e, USA) and a Brunauer-Emmet-Teller 
(BET) analysis. Before analysis, all samples were air-dried at 100 °C for 4 hours to eliminate 
any moisture. Multipoint BET was measured at P/P 0 intervals of 0.08 and 0.44. Data were 
analysed using the built-in Nova software and the BET model. Pore size distribution was 
analysed based on the built-in DFT (density functional theory) and the DA (Dubinin-Astakhov) 
module.
Raman analysis was conducted at room temperature using the Renishaw InVia V-727 (United 
Kingdom) spectrometer in backscattering geometry. A green laser (Ar+, λ = 514.5 nm, grating 
– 1200 mm -1, 10 mW) was utilised for phonon excitation, and the sample exposure duration 
was 10 s. Raman spectra were fitted using open source KherveFitting 1.7 software [29].
The electrical resistivity was measured on cuboid pieces approximately 18.5 mm in length, 4.3 
mm in width, and with the original height after sintering and primary sanding, ranging from 3 
to 5.1 mm, cut from sintered samples using the MTI Corporation (USA) SYJ-160 low-speed 
diamond saw.  The Cu wires were attached to a planar surface using MG Chemicals 843AR 
super shield silver-coated copper-particle conductive paste, as shown in Figure S2a. Electrical 
resistance was measured with a Keysight 34455A (USA) digital multimeter. Resistance 
measurement for each sample was taken as the average of a continuous 10-minute measurement. 
Differences in the resistance did not exceed 1% during the measurement.

3. Results and discussion
In this work, we used the SPS method to prepare a carbon redox mediator (working) electrode 
for decoupled electrolysis. The SPS enables the production of materials with industrial-scale 
productivity and reproducibility [30], while avoiding the use of organic binders. Currently, the 
vast majority of capacitive carbon electrode materials used in supercapacitors and 
pseudocapacitors, or as solid redox mediators in electrolysis, are prepared by using an organic 
(mostly fluoropolymer) binder to provide mechanical integrity [23]. However, the binder 
introduces inactive mass that increases internal electrical resistance, blocks pores, and surface 
active sites [24]. Moreover, fluoropolymers are expected to be banned in the European Union 
soon due to the environmental issues they cause [31]. 
For preparing carbon electrodes by SPS, we chose charcoal. The charcoal contains P 2O5, a 
natural minor component of the plant from the soil [32]. Phosphorus-containing species derived 
from P 2O5 during thermal treatment can cross-link carbon structures by forming bridges 
between carbon atoms via C–O–P or C–P–O linkages [33]. The presence of phosphorus in the 
charcoal was confirmed by XRF, which showed a P content of approximately 2.5 wt% 
(Supplementary Table S1). P 2s signal was observed also in XPS studies (Supplementary 
Figure S4), as in direct confirmation in XRF results. For sintering, we also tested other carbons 
that do not contain a significant amount of phosphorous, such as Acetylene black or MWCNT, 
but mechanical integrity was not observed, as presented in Supplementary Figure S5.
The charcoral carbon powders were sintered at different temperatures to meet the optimal 
microstructures for carbon electrodes in decoupled electrolysis. The sintering pressure of 12 
kN was chosen to ensure safe repeated use of graphite dies. We performed sintering in a wide 
temperature range. With increasing the sintering temperature from 500 °C to 1000 °C, the 
averaged sample shrinkage increased from 5.2 % to 31.5 % (Figure 1b), Supplementary Figure 
S6), the density increased from 0.72 g cm -3 to 1.08 g cm -3 (Figure 1b), BET specific surface 
area decreased from 1043 m 2 g -1 to 673 m 2 g -1 (Figure 1c, Supplementary Figure S7). The 
morphology of the sintered materials was determined considering the measured adsorbed N 2 
volume and the shape of the isotherm (Supplementary Figure S7).

mesopores. The pore-size distribution for various samples is shown in Figure 1d. The total pore

To better understand changes in charcoal structure during plasma sintering, Raman 
spectroscopy with peak fitting was used (SI Fig. S9). Five distinctive Raman peaks in the 500-
2000 cm-1 range were convoluted. In the raw material, a peak at 1697.3 cm -1, assigned to the 
C=O stretching vibration, is observed. After sintering, this peak disappears due to the thermal 
decomposition of oxidised carbon species. This can also be observed in the XPS data, where 
the C=O signal is most prevalent in the raw material, as discussed below.
To probe changes in charcoal during sintering, the D 1/G1 ratio can serve as a metric. The D 1 
band reflects high regularity of the carbon material with a combination of 6 or more benzene 
rings, while D2 corresponds to alkyl-alkyl ether sequences, and the shoulder peak D 3 at 1061 
cm-1 is related to hydrogen in the charcoal structure [34]. D 1 can also reveal the presence of 
heteroatoms and some defects. The G 1 band centered at 1598.6 cm -1 reveals two distinctive 
vibrations, representing the graphitic region, the quadratic one of the aromatic rings and the 
E2g2 vibration of activated charcoal. The G 2 band at 1526 cm -1 (valley band) is ascribed as a 
mixture of two vibration modes of sp2 carbon, asymmetric stretch, and the breathing mode [34–
37]. Charcoal, being highly disordered, non-graphitic, and amorphous, usually shows a high 
D1/G ratio. 
Initial material shows a relatively low D 1/G1 ratio of 0.681 (SI Table S3). When charcoal is 
exposed to SPS, the D1/G1 ratio increases up to 1.509. A higher D 1/G1 ratio indicates a higher 
degree of disorder, smaller graphitic domains, and a higher defect concentration [38].

The obtained samples were tested in acid decoupled water electrolysis (DWE). Acid-DWE has 
several advantages over alkaline electrolysis. Namely, the HER standard potential is 0 V vs 
SHE in an acidic environment, while -0.83 V vs SHE in an alkaline environment [40]. The 
conductivity of an acidic medium is higher, allowing a higher current density than in an alkaline 
medium [41]. Using an acidic medium also enables gas production at elevated pressures, 
reducing the energy required to pressurise systems. Also, in an alkaline environment, gas 
bubbles form more than in an acidic environment [42,43].
The cell used in electrochemical measurements is schematically shown in Figure 2a. The two-
electrode electrochemical cell consists of a RuO 2-coated Ti mesh for gas evolution, and a 
carbon redox electrode pressed on a Ti current collector for H + ion accumulation and release, 
as described in the experimental section. The electrodes were tested in a 0.5M H2SO4 aqueous 
solution.  Acid-DWE is a two-step process in which, in the first step (OER cycle), the negative 
pole of the power source is connected to the carbon electrode and the positive pole to the gas 
electrode. When a potential is applied, a double-layer charging occurs on the carbon electrode, 
in which H + ions are accumulated, while OER occurs on the gas electrode according to the 
reaction: 2H2O → 4H+ + O2 + 4e-. As the cycle continues, the potential gradually increases due 
to double-layer charging. When the set potential is reached during the OER cycle, the cell 
polarity is changed to continue with the HER cycle. In this step, the electric double layer is 
discharged by releasing H+ ions from the carbon redox electrode while the HER occurs on the 
gas electrode according to the reaction 2H + + 2e- → H2. The cycle continues until the EDL is 
discharged and reaches a set threshold potential, thereby avoiding OER on carbon.

The capacitance correlates well with the measured surface area of the carbon electrodes (Fig. 
1 (c)). This suggests that the main charging mechanism of the carbon electrode is the surface 
double-layer charging. To better understand the dominant mechanism of activated carbon 
electrodes, Raman spectroscopy was used to detect changes between the charged and 
discharged states (Figure S13). Raman spectra were measured ex-situ as an average of multiple 
points during OER and HER cycles. No significant changes in peak position or intensity were 
observed in Raman, further confirming the surface-dominated effect.

reducing the tablet thickness will increase the gravimetric charge capacity of the 1000 °C 
sample, indicating inefficient mass utilisation in the thicker sample.

To test the versatility of the sintered carbon electrode samples, we performed electrochemical 
tests in an alkaline environment (Figure 3). Here, Ni foil was used as the OER/HER catalyst 
electrode, and the electrolyte was 1 M KOH. Here, charging of the carbon electrode double 
layer occurs in the anodic potential region, as H2 is released on the Ni foil (negative electrode) 
and OH- charges the carbon electrode (positive electrode) (Figure 3 (a)). The charge capacity 
after 60 cycles (Figure 3(b)) for samples sintered at different temperatures in an alkaline 
medium is approximately half that in an acidic medium. The sample sintered at 600 °C exhibits 
the highest charge capacity of 26.5 mAh g -1  and capacitance of 119 F g -1. This indicates 
hindered diffusion and surface polarisation of OH - ions relative to H + ions. Both H + and OH- 
ions have large diffusion coefficients, but still the H + ion diffusion coefficient (7.62·10 -5 cm2 
s-1) is almost twice as large as the OH- ion (4.56·10-5 cm2 s-1) [47]. The difference in diffusion 
coefficients also results in differences in charge capacity. In general, the 600 °C sample shows 
the highest charge capacity among the other samples and also shows an increase in charge 
capacity in the middle of cycling, which could be associated with the formation of microcracks 
and deeper electrolyte access to the electrode surface. The other samples are characterised by 
an increase in charge capacity in the first 10 cycles, followed by a constant value in the 
remaining cycles.

In conclusion, a self-standing binder-free bulk electrode was prepared from commercially 
available charcoal powder via spark plasma sintering. Sintered supercapacitive electrodes were 
used as redox mediator electrodes in decoupled water electrolysis. The presence of phosphorus 
in the charcoal acts as a crosslinking agent during sintering. With increasing the sintering 
temperature from 500 °C to 1000 °C, the averaged sample shrinkage increased from 5.2 % to 
33.2 % the density increased from 0.72 g cm-3 to 1.08 g cm-3, and the BET specific surface area 
decreased from 1043 m2 g-1 to 673 m2 g-1. 
Decoupled water electrolysis using carbon electrodes in both acidic and alkaline environments 
was successfully demonstrated. As the sintering temperature increases, both the charge 
capacity and the capacitance of the carbon electrodes decrease, and this decrease is strongly 
correlated with the BET surface area. The results showed that the main ion-mediating 
mechanism at the carbon electrode is the electric double-layer charging. The best performance 
in the DWE cell is achieved with the electrode sintered at 700 °C in an acidic environment, 
yielding a specific capacitance of 175 F g-1 and a charge capacity of 42 mAh g-1. Produced gas 
volume measurements in the OER and HER cycles show 98.4 % faradaic efficiency. The 
obtained energy efficiency for complete water splitting is 54.2 %.
