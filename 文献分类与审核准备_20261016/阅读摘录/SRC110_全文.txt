1. Introduction 
The global shift toward sustainable energy systems necessitates the development of advanced 
energy storage technologies capable of mitigating the intermittent nature of renewable energy 
sources such as solar and wind [1], [2] . Electrochemical capacitors (ECs), commonly known as 
supercapacitors, have gained significant attention as promising candidates in this transition due to 
their high-power density, fast charge–discharge rates, and excellent cycle stability [3]. Compared to 
conventional batteries, ECs offer superior operational safety and reliability; however, their relatively 
low energy density continues to constrain their widespread adoption in large -scale ap plications, 
including electric vehicles and grid-level energy storage systems.

 This work reports the development of an electrode material derived from 
bamboo stem via NaOH activation, integrating hierarchical porosity, and 
surface functionalization for electrochemical capacitor applications. The 
synthesized carbon exhibits a flaky, fibrous morphology with micro –
mesoporous networks as confirmed by SEM, and a predominantly 
amorphous structure with short -range graphitic ordering from XRD. 
Elemental analysis via EDS reveals a high ca rbon content (94.65%) and 
nitrogen content (3.4%), which enhances charge storage and electrical 
conductivity. BET and BJH analyses indicate a high surface area (865 –
1147 m²/g), a pore volume of 0.552 cm³/g, and an average pore diameter 
of 2.43 nm, favourab le for ion diffusion and electrolyte access. 
Electrochemical testing demonstrates a high specific capacitance of 833 
F/g at 0.5 A/g, attributed to combined electric double -layer capacitance 
and pseudocapacitive contributions from oxygen- and nitrogen-containing 
functional groups, as evidenced by FTIR and CV (b = 0.787; 21% 
diffusion-controlled contribution). Electrochemical impedance 
spectroscopy shows a low equivalent series resistance of 0.2 Ω, while 
charge–discharge cycling confirms excellent stability w ith 94% 
capacitance retention after 5000 cycles. The CV and GCD further confirm 
its potency for efficient charge transport and high energy storage 
performance. These findings highlight the potential of bamboo -derived 
activated carbon as a sustainable and c ost-effective electrode material. 
However, the overall energy and power densities remain constrained by 
the narrow voltage window of the aqueous electrolyte and the limited 
cycling evaluation, indicating the need for further study with other 
electrolytes with higher voltage window and long-term testing.

ECc store energy through two primary mechanisms: electrical double -layer capacitance (EDLC) 
[4], wh ich involves non -faradaic ion adsorption at the electrode/electrolyte interface, and 
pseudocapacitance, which results from faradaic redox reactions associated with surface functional 
groups or transition metal compounds [5], [6]. The overall performance of ECs is largely dependent 
on the intrinsic properties of the electrode material, particularly its specific surface area, pore 
structure, electrical conductivity, and surface chemistry [7]. Among various c andidates, carbon-
based materials have emerged as the dominant class for supercapacitor electrodes, owing to their 
high surface area, tunable porosity, low density, and excellent chemical stability [8]. However, 
conventional activated carbons are often derived from fossil -based or synthetic precursors, raising 
environmental and economic concerns that conflict with the broader goals of sustainability and 
carbon neutrality [9]. 
 
In response, there has been a growing inter est in biomass -derived carbons as eco -friendly 
alternatives that align with circular economy principles [10]. Biomass precursors, including coconut 
shells, rice husks, sawdust, and corn stalks, have demonstrated promising performance when 
properly activated and functionalized [11]. Among these, bamboo has emerged as a particularly 
attractive candidate due to its fast growth rate (up to 1 m/day in some species), abundant availability 
in tropical and subtropical regions, and favourable lignocellulosic composition [12]. Rich in 
cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, bamboo biomass provides a carbon-rich framework suitable for 
conversion into porous carbon structures. Its naturally hierarchical vascular architecture further aids 
the deve lopment of interconnected micro -, meso -, and macropores essential for efficient ion 
transport and charge accumulation [13]. 
 
Despite its potential, the use of bamboo-derived carbon in supercapacitors remains underexplored, 
particularly in the context of tailored chemical activation and heteroatom doping. Previous studies 
have reported specific capacitance values ranging from 150 to 400 F/g using physical activation or 
simple pyrolysis [14], which are lower than those achieved with advanced processing techniques. 
One key challenge lies in engineering a pore structure that optimally balan ces micropores (for 
increased surface area and energy density) with mesopores (to facilitate ion diffusion and power 
delivery) [15]. Additionally, improving the surface chemistry through nitrogen and oxygen doping 
can introduce faradaic red ox-active sites, thereby enhancing pseudocapacitive behaviour and total 
capacitance [16]. 
 
Chemical activation using alkali hydroxides, especially NaOH and KOH, has been shown to 
significantly enhance porosity and surface area of carbon materials. NaOH, in particular, reacts 
effectively with carbonaceous precursors to form pore -generating gases and salts, yielding well -
developed hierarchical pore systems [17]. When combined with thermal treatment under inert 
atmosphere, NaOH activation can produce highly porous carbon with tailore d textural and 
electrochemical properties. The incorporation of nitrogen -containing functional groups during 
carbonization or activation, particularly species such as pyridinic-N and pyrrolic-N has been widely 
associated with enhanced electrochemical performance, including increased capacitance, improved 
wettability, and higher electrical conductivity [18]. 
 
This study reports the synthesis of NaOH-activated carbon derived from bamboo waste, specifically 
sourced from discarded scaffold material. The objective was to address the performance limitations 
typically observed in conventional biomass-derived carbons for electrochemical energy storage. The 
precursor underwent controlled pyrolysis  followed by chemical activatio n to induce hierarchical 
porosity. The resulting material was thoroughly characterized using scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM), Brunauer –Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area analysis, Fourier -transform infrared

Electrochemical performance was assessed through cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge–

While the bamboo -derived carbon electrode exhibited a high specific capacitance of 833 F/g, the

electrolyte, which inherently limits the operating voltage to 1.0 V. Future studies will explore 
organic or gel-polymer electrolytes to expand the voltage window and unlock higher energy outputs. 
 
2.  Materials and Methods 
 
2.1 Materials 
Dry bamboo stems were sourced locally from a building site as waste materials from a  local 
scaffold. The stems were thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove surface impurities, dirt, 
and residual biomass contaminants. Analytical -grade NaOH were obtained from African University 
of Science and Technology (AUST) Laboratory and used without further purification. Analytical -
grade Hydrochloric Acid (HCl) was used to wash the activated carbon to neutralize residual NaOH.  
 
2.2 Preparation of Bamboo Stem-Derived Activated Carbon 
The cleaned and dried bamboo stems were then ground using a milling machine into fine powder. 
Then sieved using an electric sieve for a uniform particle size of 100 µm to increase surface area for 
subsequent carbonization. 
 
2.2.1  Carbonization 
The powdered bamboo was placed in a ceramic crucible with a lid and heated in a tubular furnace 
under a nitrogen atmosphere to prevent oxidation.  Carbonization was carried out at a t emperature of 
500 °C for 2 hours, with a heating rate of 10 °C/min. This step decomposed the cellulose, 
hemicellulose, and lignin in the bamboo, resulting in a preliminary carbon-rich material. 
 
2.2.2 Chemical Activation 
 After cooling to room temperature, the carbonized bamboo powder was mixed with NaOH at a weight 
ratio of 1:3 (carbonized material: NaOH) in deionized water to create a uniform paste. The mixture 
was stirred thoroughly and then dried in an oven at 110°C for 12 hours to remove any excess wat er. 
The dried sample was subsequently subjected to a second heat treatment in a tubular furnace under a 
nitrogen atmosphere, this time at a higher activation temperature of 800 °C for 1 hour, with a 
controlled heating rate of 5 °C/min. During this step, th e NaOH interacted with the carbon matrix, 
effectively promoting the development of a porous structure and significantly increasing the surface 
area of the material. 
 
2.2.3  Washing and Neutralization 
After activation, the carbon material was carefully remo ved from the furnace and washed multiple 
times with 0.1 M hydrochloric acid to neutralize any residual NaOH and eliminate potential metallic 
impurities. This was followed by thorough rinsing with deionized water until the filtrate reached a 
neutral pH of 7 , ensuring the complete removal of acid and by -products. The cleaned material was 
then oven-dried at 105 °C for 12 hours and subsequently ground into a fine powder, yielding the final 
bamboo-derived activated carbon.

2.3  Characterization of Bamboo Stem-Derived Activated Carbon 
2.3.1 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) 
The surface morphology of the activated carbon was analysed using a scanning electron microscope 
(SEM, JEOL JSM -7100F) to assess the formation of pores, variations in pore size distribution, a nd 
the overall surface texture. This provided visual insight into the material's porous architecture 
resulting from the activation process. 
 
2.3.2 Surface Area and Porosity (BET Analysis) 
The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was employed to measure the specific surface area of 
the activated carbon using a nitrogen adsorption -desorption isotherm at 77 K. Pore size distribution 
and total pore volume were determined by the Barrett –Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method, focusing on 
mesopore and micropore distribution. 
 
2.3.3 Surface Functional Groups (FTIR) 
Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) was conducted to identify the surface functional 
groups present on the activated carbon, as these can play a key role in influencing its electrochemical 
behaviour. The spectra were collected over a wavenumber range of 4000 to 500 cm⁻¹, allowing for 
the detection of various oxygen - and nitrogen-containing groups introduced during activation and 
doping. 
 
2.4 Electrochemical Characterization 
2.4.1 Electrode Preparation 
The bamboo-derived activated carbon was combined with conductive carbon black and 
polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder in a weight ratio of 8:1:1 to prepare the electrode material. 
This composition ensured good electrical conductivity and mechanical stability  for subsequent 
electrochemical testing. N -methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) was used as a solvent to create a 
homogeneous slurry, which was then coated onto a cleaned nickel foam current collector and dried at 
80°C for 12 hours. The coated electrodes were pressed to ensure uniform thickness and good contact 
between materials. 
 
2.4.2 Electrochemical Testing 
2.4.1.1 Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) 
CV was performed in a 3-electrode setup using the electrode made from the bamboo-based activated 
carbon infused on nickel foam as current collector, a platinum counter electrode, an  Ag/AgCl 
reference electrode, and a 6 M KOH electrolyte. The potential window was set between 0 to 1.0 V 
(vs. Ag/AgCl) with scan rates ranging from 5 to 100 mV/s to investigate the specific capacitance and 
redox behaviour.

2.4.1.3  Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) 
Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) was performed over a frequency range of 0.01 Hz to 
100 kHz to assess internal resistance, ion transport behaviour, and overall electrical conductivity. The 
resulting data were presented as Nyquist plots, which are commonly used to interpret EIS results. For 
the bamboo -derived activat ed carbon, the Nyquist plot provided key insights into the electrode's 
equivalent series resistance (ESR) and ion diffusion characteristics. These findings reflect the 
material's ability to support efficient charge transfer and ion movement, both of which are critical for 
high-performance supercapacitor applications. 
 
3. Results and Discussion 
 
3.1 Surface Area and Porosity Analysis (BET and BJH) 
The textural properties of the bamboo -derived activated carbon were investigated using nitrogen 
adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K. Analyses based on the Brunauer –Emmett–Teller (BET) 
method (Figure 1a) and the Barrett –Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model (Figure 1b) provided detailed 
information on the material’s surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, key  factors that 
directly influence ion transport and charge storage performance in electrochemical capacitors [21]. 
The BET surface area was measured at 865.26 m²/g, indicating the presence of a well -developed 
porous network. This high surface area is particularly beneficial for electric double-layer capacitance 
(EDLC), as it offers more active sites for electrolyte ion adsorption at the electrode –electrolyte

3.2 Morphology Analysis (SEM and EDS) 
3.2.1 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) Analysis 
The surface morphology of the NaOH -activated bamboo -derived carbon was examined using 
Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), with images shown in Figure 2a - d at various magnifications. 
The SEM micrographs reveal a highly porous, flaky structure, ch aracterized by interconnected 
networks of micro-, meso-, and macropores. This hierarchical pore architecture is a key feature for 
effective electrochemical capacitor electrodes, as it facilitates both rapid ion transport and efficient 
charge storage [26]. 
 
At lower magnifications (500×–1000×), the surface appears rough and irregular, with large cavities 
and fractured textures resulting from the intense chemical etching during NaOH activation at 800 °C. 
These larger macropores, likely formed through the removal of vola tile components and structural 
decomposition of lignocellulosic biomass, serve as ion -buffering reservoirs that shorten diffusion 
paths between the electrolyte and internal pores. 
 
At higher magnifications (1500× –2000×), the carbon material exhibits micro -sheeted domains and 
fibrous textures, with clear evidence of mesoporous channels and micropore openings. These smaller 
pores significantly increase the specific surface area (BET: 865 –1147 m²/g) and enable double-layer 
formation and fast electrolyte accessibility. The porous walls and fractured surfaces also expose more 
active sites for faradaic reactions, consistent with the pseudocapacitive behaviour observed in CV and 
FTIR analyses. The flaky morphology observed is a typical indicator of layered carbon a ssemblies 
with interconnected pore systems, suggesting good mechanical integrity and electron percolation 
pathways. This contributes to the excellent rate capability and low internal resistance (EIS: 0.2 Ω) 
demonstrated by the electrode.

3.4 Raman Spectroscopy Analysis 
Raman spectroscopy was employed to investigate the structural ordering and degree of graphitization 
in the bamboo -derived activated carbon [29]. As shown in Figure 4, the spectrum displays two 
prominent peaks: the D -band located around 1350 cm⁻¹ and the G -band around 1580 cm⁻¹. The D -
band corresponds to the presence of disordered carbon structures or structural defects associated with 
sp³-hybridized carbon atoms [30], while the G -band arises from the E2g vibrational mode of sp² -
hybridized graphitic carbon domains [31], [32]. 
 
The intensity ratio of the D to G bands (ID/IG) is a widely used metric to evaluate the level of disorder 
and the extent of graphitic structure in carbon -based material s. In this study, the ID/IG ratio was 
calculated to be 0.89, indicating a moderately disordered carbon matrix with the presence of short -

3.5  X-ray Diffraction (XRD) Analysis 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to investigate the crystallographic structure and degree of 
graphitization of the bamboo -derived activated carbon. The XRD pattern, shown in Figure 5a & b, 
displays a broad diffraction peak centred at approximately 23° (2θ) , which corresponds to the (002) 
plane of turbostratic carbon structures [35]. This broad peak is characteristic of amorphous or 
disordered carbon, commonly observed in biomass-derived carbons subjected to chemical activation. 
The low-intensity and broad nature of the (002) peak indic ates the presence of short-range graphitic 
domains without significant long -range ordering. This structural disorder is beneficial for 
electrochemical applications, as it provides abundant defect sites that can enhance ion accessibility 
and facilitate faradaic reactions. The absence of sharp, well-defined peaks associated with crystalline 
graphite (typically at ~26.5°) confirms that the carbon structure remains largely amorphous, even after 
high-temperature activation at 800 °C under nitrogen atmosphere. 
 
In addition to the dominant amorphous carbon peak, several minor crystalline reflections were 
observed. Weak peaks around 28° –34° (2θ) may be attributed to residual inorganic salts, including 
NaCl (halite) and SiO₂ (silicon oxide), which are likely remnants  from the NaOH activation or 
inherent to the bamboo precursor’s mineral content. Trace peaks related to osumilite and other silicate 
phases were also detected, consistent with prior reports on biomass-derived carbons containing natural 
mineral impurities. 
 
Importantly, the lack of heavy metal oxide residues (e.g., Fe, Ti, Ca) indicates the effectiveness of the 
post-activation washing and purification steps. This contributes to the material’s electrochemical 
stability and minimizes parasitic reactions during cycling. While high crystallinity is often associated 
with better electrical conductivity, the combination of amorphous domains and short -range ordering

3.8  Charge Storage Mechanism and Role of Surface Functionalities 
The superior electrochemical performance observed in the bamboo -derived activated carbon is 
attributed to a hybrid charge storage mechanism, involving both electric double -layer capacitance 
(EDLC) and pseudocapacitance. The EDLC behaviour originates from the physical a dsorption of 
electrolyte ions at the electrode –electrolyte interface, primarily supported by the material's high 
specific surface area (865–1147 m²/g) and hierarchical micro–mesoporous structure, which facilitates 
ion transport and rapid charge–discharge cycles. In addition to EDLC, pseudocapacitive contributions 
play a significant role in enhancing the overall capacitance. These arise from faradaic redox reactions 
involving heteroatoms, notably oxygen and nitrogen-containing functional groups, introduced during 
the NaOH activation and pyrolysis steps. Evidence from FTIR analysis confirms the presence of C=O 
(carbonyl), C–O–C (ether), and O–H (hydroxyl) groups, while EDS and elemental mapping reveal a 
nitrogen content of 3.4 %, mainly as pyridinic-N, pyrrolic-N, and quaternary-N configurations. 
 
These surface functionalities facilitate reversible redox reactions at the electrode surface, contributing 
to pseudocapacitance through the following mechanisms: 
i Oxygen functional groups, particularly carbonyl and hyd roxyl groups, engage in surface 
faradaic reactions such as equation (4): 
> 𝐶 = 𝑂 + 𝐻+ + 𝑒− ↔> 𝐶 − 𝑂𝐻          (4) 
which can reversibly store charge at the carbon/electrolyte interface. 
ii Nitrogen functional groups enhance capacitance in multiple ways: 
▪ Pyridinic-N and pyrrolic -N can act as redox -active centres, contributing directly to 
faradaic reactions. 
▪ Quaternary-N improves electrical conductivity by enhancing the delocalization of π -
electrons within the carbon matrix, facilitating faster electron transport. 
▪ It also enhances wettability, allowing better electrolyte infiltration into the pore network. 
The hybrid behaviour is supported by the CV scan rate analysis, where the b -value of 0.787 and 
diffusion-controlled contribution of 21% indicate mixed charge storage behaviour. This synergy 
between EDLC and pseudocapacitance results in the high specific capacitance (833 F/g at 0.5 A/g) 
and excellent rate capability and cycling stability.

4. Conclusion 
 
In this study, a sustainable and high-performance electrode material was successfully developed from 
bamboo stem biomass using NaOH chemical activation. The resulting carbon featured a hierarchical 
pore structure and a high surface area (BET: 865 m²/g; BJH: 1147 m²/g), along with surface 
functionalities rich in oxygen and nitrogen-containing groups—all of which contributed to its strong 
electrochemical performance. 
 
Detailed structural and morphological analyses showed that the carbon had a flaky, fibrous texture 
with a well -connected network of micro -, meso -, and macropores. This interconnected pore 
architecture plays a crucial role in facilitating efficient ion transport and enabling rapid charge –
discharge behaviour, making the material highly suitable for supercapacitor applications.

FTIR, Raman, and XRD characterizations confirmed the presence of defect -rich amorphous carbon 
domains, redox-active surface groups, and partial graphitic ordering. These features enabled a hybrid 
charge storage mechanism, combining electric double-layer capacitance and pseudocapacitance. 
Electrochemical evaluation demonstrated a remarkably high specific capacitance of 833 F/g at 0.5 
A/g, excellent rate capability, and 94% capacitance  retention after 5000 cycles, highlighting the 
material’s robust stability and energy storage potential. The low equivalent series resistance (0.2 Ω) 
further validated the material’s high conductivity and fast ion diffusion pathways. Compared with 
other recently reported biomass-derived carbons, the bamboo-derived electrode material outperforms 
in terms of both capacitance and sustainability. 
 
This work demonstrates that bamboo stem, an abundant and renewable biomass resource, can serve 
as an effective prec ursor for producing activated carbon with tunable porosity and functional 
chemistry. The green synthesis route, combined with scalable processing, positions this material as a 
promising candidate for next-generation eco-friendly supercapacitor devices. 
Although the bamboo-derived activated carbon demonstrated high specific capacitance and excellent 
short-term cycling stability, the study is limited by the narrow aqueous voltage window and absence 
of long-term or large-scale evaluation. Future work will focu s on optimizing the activation process  
and expanding the voltage window through alternative electrolytes.
