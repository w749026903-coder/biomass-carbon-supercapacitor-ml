Abstract
Cobalt-based nanomaterials have emerged as a highly versatile class of functional materials due
to their tunable electronic structure, multiple accessible oxidation states, and diverse structural
architectures. At the nanoscale, cobalt exhibits enhanced magnetic, catalytic, optical, and
electrochemical properties that signiﬁcantly outperform its bulk counterparts. This review
provides a comprehensive overview of recent advances in cobalt nanomaterials, encompassing
their dimensional and compositional classiﬁcation, synthetic strategies, physicochemical
properties, and wide-ranging applications. Morphology-controlled synthesis routes, including
wet chemical, organometallic, physical, and green approaches, which enable precise regulation
of size, crystallinity, defect density, and surface chemistry, are described. The structure–
property relationship governing performance in energy storage and conversion systems such as
lithium-ion batteries, supercapacitors, fuel cells, and electrocatalytic water splitting (hydrogen
evolution reaction [HER]/oxygen evolution reaction [OER]) is discussed. Emerging applications
in heterogeneous catalysis, environmental remediation, magnetic devices, and biomedical
technologies are also examined. Despite signiﬁcant progress, challenges related to oxidation
stability, scalability, reproducibility, cytotoxicity, and environmental persistence remain critical
barriers to commercialization. The review concludes by outlining future perspectives. By
integrating advances in synthesis, characterization, and application-driven optimization, cobalt
nanomaterials are poised to play a pivotal role in next-generation energy, environmental, and
technological systems.
Keywords
cobalt nanomaterials sustainable synthesis heterostructures magnetic nanoparticles
electrocatalysis environmental remediation nanotoxicity
1. Introduction
Nanostructured materials based on transition metals have emerged as key components in
advanced technologies owing to their tunable physicochemical properties, high surface area,
and unique electronic characteristics at reduced dimensions. Among these materials, cobalt-
based nanomaterials have attracted considerable attention due to their multiple accessible
oxidation states, magnetic behaviour, catalytic activity, and structural versatility, which enable
applications across energy storage, catalysis, environmental remediation, and biomedical
applications [ – ].

The nanoscale engineering of cobalt materials, including nanoparticles, nanowires, nanosheets,
hollow nanostructures, and hierarchical architectures, has enabled precise control over
morphology, surface chemistry, and electronic structure, thereby signiﬁcantly enhancing their
functional performance compared with bulk counterparts [ , , ]. In particular, cobalt oxides,
sulﬁdes, phosphides, and layered double hydroxides (LDH) have demonstrated excellent
electrochemical properties in supercapacitors, lithium-ion and sodium-ion batteries, and
electrocatalytic water-splitting systems [ ]. Recent studies have shown that heterostructure
engineering and defect modulation can further improve charge transfer kinetics and catalytic
activity, enabling high-efficiency oxygen evolution and hydrogen evolution reactions (HER) for
clean energy applications [ ]. Beyond energy systems, cobalt nanomaterials also exhibit
promising performance in environmental and biomedical domains. Their strong redox
capability facilitates the degradation of organic pollutants, reduction of heavy metal ions, and
photocatalytic remediation of wastewater contaminants [ ]. In biomedical contexts, cobalt-
based nanoparticles have been investigated for drug delivery, antimicrobial activity, imaging,
and therapeutic applications owing to their magnetic properties and surface functionalization
potential [ ]. However, concerns regarding nanotoxicity, environmental persistence, and long-
term biological interactions remain critical challenges that require systematic evaluation to
ensure safe implementation.
Recent research directions are increasingly focused on advanced design strategies, including
two-dimensional cobalt nanostructures, single-atom catalysts (SACs), metal–organic
framework-derived architectures, and hybrid nanocomposites with conductive carbon
materials [ ]. Furthermore, the integration of computational modelling, machine learning, and
artiﬁcial intelligence into materials discovery is accelerating the rational design of cobalt
nanomaterials with optimized catalytic activity, stability, and scalability. These developments
are expected to play a pivotal role in next-generation energy technologies and sustainable
industrial processes.
This review, therefore, provides a comprehensive overview of cobalt nanomaterials, covering
synthesis strategies, structural properties, and multifunctional applications, while highlighting
current challenges and future research opportunities. Understanding structure–property
relationships and developing scalable fabrication approaches will be essential to translate
laboratory-scale innovations into practical technological solutions.
2. Classiﬁcation of cobalt nanomaterials
Due to their distinct size-dependent magnetic, catalytic, and electrical properties that set them
apart from bulk materials, cobalt nanoparticles (Co-NPs) are extremely desirable. In order to
maximize their uses in energy storage, biomedical diagnostics, cancer treatment, and industrial
catalysis, their classiﬁcation based on dimension (0D-3D) and composition is essential [ – ].

2.1 Dimensional classiﬁcation
Current advancements in zero-dimensional (0D) cobalt-based nanomaterials have moved
toward the development of high-performance, multipurpose, sustainable systems for
biomedicine, energy storage, and catalysis. Several works have reported on the synthesis of 0D
to Three-dimensional (3D) cobalt-based nanomaterials due to their potential applications [ ,
]. Ali et al. successfully synthesized Co-NPs with diameters of 40–60 nm, which showed
signiﬁcant antibacterial activity against diverse bacterial strains [ ]. One-dimensional (1D)
cobalt-based nanomaterials, such as nanowires, nanotubes, and nanorods, along with their two-
dimensional (2D) counterparts, are being developed in recent times to improve electrocatalytic
activity and energy storage through nano structuring, thereby maximizing speciﬁc surface area
and electron transport [ , ]. Among the major advances, Wan et al. reported the synthesis of
1D-on-1D CoFeSe @CoNi-CH hybrid nanowires as advanced battery-type supercapacitor
electrodes [ ]. Rossella et al. reported a one-step hydrothermal synthesis of 2D-
CoO@Co(OH) composite for microplastics degradation and hydrogen evolution [ ]. Three-
dimensional cobalt-based nanomaterials are widely utilized for their superior electrical
conductivity, high surface area, and magnetic properties. Iiao et al. also reported a 3D
conduction network of Pt–Co alloy [ ].
2.2 Compositional classiﬁcation
A number of cobalt-based nanomaterials have been developed based on their composition in
order to optimize their distinct magnetic, catalytic, and electrical capabilities for cutting-edge
uses [ – , , ].
2.2.1 Zero-valent cobalt nanoparticles, Cobalt Sulﬁde, and oxide nanostructures
Zero-valent cobalt (ZVCo) nanoparticles are highly magnetic metallic cobalt particles
characterized by their small size, high surface-to-volume ratio, and superparamagnetic
properties. Current advancements in ZVCo nanoparticles concentrate on their incorporation
into multipurpose composites for cutting-edge magnetic technologies and sophisticated
pollution management. ZVCo-decorating metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), speciﬁcally the
ZVCo-MIL-88 A(Fe)@ β -CD composite, were highlighted by Chen et al. [ ].
Also, cobalt-based nanomaterials, especially oxides (CoO, CoO) and sulﬁdes (CoS, CoS,
CoS), are high-performance materials for energy storage and catalysis due to their high
electrical conductivity, structural stability, and tunable morphology. Recent developments in
cobalt sulﬁde and cobalt oxide nanostructures offer promising morphological engineering, size
control, and hybrid composite materials to overcome traditional performance limitations in
energy storage and catalysis [ – ]. Cardenas et al. reported the synthesis of CoO
nanostructure sheets by direct cobalt anodization, followed by low-temperature heat treatment
for potential applications in catalysis, sensors, and energy storage [ ]. Lihui et al. prepared
CoS nanoparticles incorporated in ordered mesoporous carbon by a chelation-assisted self-
assembly method [ ].

2.2.2 Cobalt hydroxides and LDH
Two-diemnsional cobalt hydroxide nanosheets and LDH represent a major class of
nanomaterials whose performance relies on intercalation chemistry for energy storage and
catalysis. Important developments include the use of Mo/Fe doping to increase activity, 3D
nanoarrays for better electron transport, and the combination of Ni–Co LDHs with conductive
carbon materials for increased stability. Shuo et al. reported the synthesis of Co-LHs@CoFe-
LDHs composite with signiﬁcantly improved oxygen evolution reaction (OER) performance
[ ].
2.2.3 Cobalt based hybrid/polymer nanocomposites
Cobalt-based nanocomposites and cobalt-carbon hybrids are extensively studied and employed
mainly for their remarkable effectiveness in biomedical applications, high-performance
catalysis, and energy storage. Hakimeh reported the synthesis of a novel magnetic core-shell
nanocomposite, CoO@mSiO, supporting an ionic/palladium liquid (CoO@mSiO/IL-Pd),
which was tested for use as an efficient catalyst for nitrobenzene reduction [ ]. Yan et al.
developed a ternary nanocomposite photocatalyst by combining ZnInS  nanosheets, CdS
quantum dots, and CoS nanocages [ ].
Cobalt-carbon hybrids serve as affordable, long-lasting, and highly conductive substitutes for
noble metal catalysts. The main advancements focus on improving stability, selective chemical
conversion, and catalytic efficiency. Giddaerappa et al. developed a novel electrocatalyst, octa-3-
thienyl-substituted cobalt phthalocyanine hybridized with carbon nanoparticles, to enhance the
oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) [ ].
Cobalt-polymer composites represent major advances because of their special combination of
features, which include advanced magnetic functionality, high-performance energy storage,
and better electromagnetic interference shielding. Ans et al. have synthesized new
magnetoactive PVA/CoFeO polymer composites for remote actuation and sensing in the
biomedical sector [ ]. Kangqi et al. have developed a novel strategy for the synthesis of
polymer-derived cobalt-carbon composites (PLC–Co), intended for next-generation portable
energy storage devices, using a metallic ionic liquid [ ]. Lingchen et al. described the synthesis
of NiCoO nanosphere heterostructures and FeCoNi–LDH nanosheets for an efficient
electrocatalytic oxygen evolution reaction [ ]. Zahrah et al. synthesized CoS@NiS
nanoparticles via a hydrothermal route. The results demonstrated that the formation of a
heterojunction between CoS and NiS could synergistically stimulate charge transfer and
promote edge sites, thereby enhancing the activity of the HER [ ].

3. Synthetic strategies for cobalt nanomaterials
The synthesis of cobalt nanomaterials has evolved into a diverse ﬁeld where chemical, physical,
and green methodologies enable precise control over particle size, morphology, crystallinity,
and surface chemistry. Central to all synthetic strategies is the interplay between precursors,
surfactants, and ligands, which collectively govern nucleation and growth kinetics. Surfactants
such as oleic acid, oleylamine, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and cetyltrimethylammonium
bromide (CTAB) act as capping agents that stabilize speciﬁc crystal facets, thereby directing
anisotropic growth into spheres, rods, cubes, or hollow structures [ ]. Ligands modulate
precursor reactivity and inﬂuence reduction pathways, while precursor choice, whether
inorganic salts or organometallic complexes, determines decomposition temperature, purity,
and achievable morphologies. Each synthetic route offers distinct advantages and limitations in
terms of scalability, environmental impact, and structural control [ – , ].
3.1 Wet-chemical methods
Wet-chemical methods are cost-effective and versatile but may suffer from impurities and broad
size distributions. Organometallic routes provide exceptional control over monodispersity and
morphology but rely on hazardous solvents and precursors. Physical methods yield high-purity
ﬁlms and particles but require expensive equipment and high energy input. Green synthesis
approaches offer sustainability and biocompatibility but currently lack the precision and
reproducibility of conventional methods. Ultimately, the choice of method depends on the
desired application – whether magnetic storage, catalysis, biomedical imaging, or energy
storage – and the required balance between scalability, environmental impact, and structural
control [ – ].
3.2 Co-precipitation
Co-precipitation is one of the simplest and most scalable methods for producing Co-NPs,
typically involving the precipitation of cobalt hydroxides or carbonates from aqueous cobalt
salts using alkaline agents. The method beneﬁts from low cost, mild conditions, and
compatibility with large-scale production. However, it often yields broad size distributions and
limited control over crystallinity unless combined with post-treatments such as annealing or
surfactant-assisted growth. Impurities from residual ions may also affect magnetic properties
[ , ].
3.3 Sol–gel synthesis
Sol–gel routes rely on the hydrolysis and condensation of cobalt alkoxides or salts to form
polymeric networks that, upon calcination, yield cobalt oxides or metallic cobalt. This method
enables excellent compositional homogeneity and is widely used for cobalt-based mixed oxides
and catalysts. The main limitations include long processing times, shrinkage during gel drying,
and the need for high-temperature calcination, which may induce particle agglomeration [ ].

Hydrothermal and solvothermal syntheses provide enhanced control over particle morphology
through high-pressure, high-temperature environments that promote crystallization. Solvent
choice, mineralizers, and surfactants strongly inﬂuence the resulting nanostructures, enabling
the formation of nanorods, nanoplates, hollow spheres, and porous frameworks. These methods
offer high crystallinity and tunable morphology but require specialized autoclave equipment
and may involve long reaction times [ – ]. Hydrothermal and solvothermal techniques are
widely employed for the synthesis of Co-NPs due to their ability to provide controlled reaction
environments that facilitate precise tuning of particle size, morphology, and crystallinity. These
approaches typically involve chemical reactions conducted in sealed autoclaves under elevated
temperature and pressure, allowing enhanced solubility and reactivity of precursors while
limiting contamination from atmospheric oxygen.
So, Seong et al. reported the preparation of Co-NPs through a supercritical hydrothermal
reduction process involving the decomposition of formic acid at temperatures approaching 420
°C and pressures around 22 MPa [ ]. In order to prevent oxidation of the cobalt species during
synthesis, the precursor materials were introduced into the reaction vessel under an argon
atmosphere using an inert isolation chamber. The resulting particles exhibited predominantly
spherical morphologies with sizes ranging from approximately 20–400 nm. Such conditions
illustrate the ability of hydrothermal systems to promote nucleation and controlled growth of
metallic nanoparticles while maintaining structural stability. Similarly, Alagiri et al.
demonstrated a solvothermal route for producing Co-NPs using cobalt chloride dissolved in
ethanol [ ]. The solution was vigorously stirred for approximately two hours until a
homogeneous, transparent purple solution was obtained. Under these conditions, the
subsequent reduction process yielded highly dispersed nanoparticles with an average diameter
of about 2 nm and a spherical morphology. Structural characterization by X-ray diffraction
(XRD) conﬁrmed the formation of pure metallic cobalt (Co ⁰ ) without detectable secondary
phases, indicating that solvothermal methods can provide high-purity nanomaterials with
excellent size control.
3.5 Thermal decomposition routes
Thermal decomposition routes for the synthesis of cobalt nanomaterials involve heating organic
or inorganic precursors under controlled reaction conditions (reaction time, temperature, use of
capping agent) to break down the precursor into the desired nanomaterials [ – ]. This
method is simple, involves short reaction times, and generates size-controlled and morphology-
tailored particles.
3.5.1 Metal–organic precursors
Organometallic precursors, such as cobalt carbonyls or cobalt acetylacetonate, decompose
cleanly at controlled temperatures, producing highly monodisperse nanoparticles. These
precursors allow precise control over nucleation events, often yielding superior uniformity
compared to aqueous methods [ – ].

Thermal decomposition in high-boiling organic solvents enables the separation of nucleation
and growth stages, following LaMer-type kinetics. Rapid nucleation, followed by slow growth,
produces narrow size distributions. Reaction parameters, including ligand concentration,
heating rate, and precursor reactivity, govern the ﬁnal particle size and shape [ ].
3.5.3 Inﬂuence of ligands and surfactants
Ligands such as oleic acid and trioctylphosphine act as both stabilizers and growth-directing
agents. They bind selectively to cobalt crystal facets, promoting anisotropic growth and
preventing aggregation. While this method yields high-quality nanocrystals, the use of toxic
organic solvents and air-sensitive precursors limits its environmental sustainability and
scalability [ , , ].
3.6 Physical methods
Physical methods for producing cobalt (Co) nanomaterials include physical vapour deposition
(PVD), sputtering, laser ablation, inert gas condensation, and mechanical ball milling, which
are used to generate cobalt, cobalt oxide, and cobalt sulﬁde composite nanoparticles. Physical
methods are generally fast and do not require hazardous chemicals.
3.6.1 Chemical Vapor Deposition
Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) enables the deposition of cobalt thin ﬁlms and
nanostructures through the decomposition of volatile cobalt precursors on heated substrates. It
offers excellent purity, uniformity, and compatibility with microelectronics. However, it
requires expensive equipment and high temperatures and is less suited for producing colloidal
nanoparticles [ – ]. Vapour-phase techniques, such as CVD and vapour condensation,
provide alternative routes for producing cobalt nanostructures with controlled composition and
morphology. These methods typically involve the evaporation or decomposition of metal
precursors, followed by nucleation and growth of nanoparticles in the vapour phase. Dong et al.
synthesized Co-NPs using a chemical condensation approach, resulting in both metallic cobalt
and cobalt oxide nanoparticles with sizes ranging between 10 and 25 nm [ ]. The produced
particles exhibited spherical as well as chain-like morphologies, demonstrating the diversity of
structures achievable through vapour-phase synthesis. In another study, Wang et al. prepared
carbon-coated cobalt nanocapsules through vapour condensation techniques [ ]. The obtained
nanocapsules had diameters between 20 and 60 nm and were encapsulated by an amorphous
carbon shell approximately 4–6 nm thick. X-ray diffraction analysis revealed the presence of
cobalt carbide phases such as CoC and CoC. Importantly, vapour condensation methods allow
particle size and structure to be adjusted by modifying parameters such as chamber pressure
and precursor decomposition temperature, although the resulting nanoparticles may still
display relatively broad size distributions.

Physical vapor deposition techniques, including thermal evaporation and electron-beam
evaporation, produce cobalt nanostructures by the physical transfer of cobalt atoms to a
substrate. These methods allow precise thickness control and are widely used in magnetic
storage technologies. Their main drawbacks include a limited ability to produce free-standing
nanoparticles and high energy consumption [ , ].
3.6.3 Laser ablation and sputtering
Laser ablation in liquids generates ligand-free Co-NPs with high purity, making it attractive for
biomedical applications. Sputtering, on the other hand, is ideal for producing cobalt thin ﬁlms
and multilayers with controlled composition. Both methods avoid chemical contaminants but
require sophisticated instrumentation and offer limited control over particle shape compared to
wet-chemical routes [ ].
3.7 Green and sustainable synthesis approaches
Green synthesis of cobalt nanomaterials uses plant extracts or microorganisms as eco-friendly
reducing/capping agents, thus substituting toxic chemicals.
3.7.1 Plant extracts
In recent years, green synthesis approaches based on biological resources have gained signiﬁcant
attention as sustainable alternatives to conventional chemical methods. Plant extracts, algae,
and other natural biomolecules can serve simultaneously as reducing and stabilizing agents,
eliminating the need for hazardous reagents and reducing environmental impact. Plant-
mediated synthesis uses phytochemicals such as polyphenols, ﬂavonoids, and sugars as reducing
and stabilizing agents. This approach enables low-temperature synthesis. While
environmentally friendly, it often suffers from limited reproducibility due to variability in
extract composition [ – ].
Samuel et al. proposed a green synthesis strategy employing extracts derived from Vitis
rotundifolia to produce Co-NPs [ ]. In this method, the plant pulp extract was gradually
introduced into a cobalt chloride solution under continuous heating and stirring.
Phytochemicals present in the extract facilitated the reduction of cobalt ions while also
stabilizing the resulting nanoparticles. The obtained particles exhibited a relatively broad size
distribution and possessed a polycrystalline cubic structure characteristic of metallic cobalt, as
conﬁrmed by XRD analysis. The diversity of organic compounds present in plant extracts often
inﬂuences nucleation kinetics, which may explain the observed variability in particle size.
Marine organisms have also been explored as effective bio-reducing systems. Ajarem et al.
utilized red algae extracts to synthesize Co-NPs through a straightforward aqueous process [ ].
The extract was ﬁrst prepared by heating dried algae at approximately 70 °C for four hours.
Subsequently, cobalt nitrate was added dropwise while the solution was stirred continuously at
room temperature for 24 hours. The reaction was accompanied by a distinct colour change from
pale pink to dark brown, indicating nanoparticle formation. A related approach was reported by
Bibi et al., who used pomegranate (Punica granatum) peel extract as a natural reducing agent for
cobalt precursors [ ]. In this process, the extract was mixed with the cobalt salt and heated to

3.7.2 Biotemplates
Biotemplating employs natural structures such as bacteria, fungi, cellulose, or proteins to direct
the formation of cobalt nanostructures with hierarchical or porous architectures. These
templates provide unique morphologies but may require complex puriﬁcation steps [ ].
Microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi have also been investigated as biological templates
for the synthesis of cobalt nanostructures. These organisms contain functional biomolecules
capable of binding metal ions and promoting the controlled nucleation of nanoparticles under
mild conditions.
Jang et al. reported the formation of rod-shaped Co-NPs using the bacterium Bacillus subtilis as a
biological scaffold [ ]. In this method, cobalt chloride was incubated with a bacterial
suspension, allowing cobalt ions to interact with surface biomolecules and subsequently
undergo reduction. After incubation, the nanoparticles were separated and puriﬁed through
centrifugation and washing steps. This strategy enabled nanoparticle formation at room
temperature and demonstrated potential for applications in nanobiocatalysis due to the unique
morphology and biological functionalization of the particles. Fungal systems have also proven
effective in nanoparticle synthesis. Vijayanandan and Balakrishnan employed the fungus
Aspergillus nidulans to generate Co-NPs through a bio-mediated process [ ]. In their study,
cobalt acetylacetonate was incubated with fungal mycelium on a rotary shaker at room
temperature for ﬁve days. Following incubation, the reaction mixture was ﬁltered and washed
thoroughly to remove residual culture medium components. The nanoparticles were then
recovered by centrifugation. The resulting particles displayed an average diameter of
approximately 20 nm and were stabilized by sulfur-containing proteins secreted by the fungus
during growth, as revealed by FTIR analysis.
3.8 Low-energy synthesis
Low-energy routes, including microwave-assisted synthesis, ultrasound-assisted reduction, and
mechanochemical methods, signiﬁcantly reduce reaction times and energy consumption [ , ,
]. They offer promising scalability but may still require optimization to achieve precise
morphological control.

4. Structural, optical, and magnetic properties of cobalt
nanomaterials
Cobalt-based nanomaterials exhibit optical, structural, and magnetic properties, which depend
heavily on their morphology, size, and surface defect density (oxygen/sulfur vacancies) [ – ,
]. To date, several key strategies, including the optimization of surface chemistry and the
creation of composite/hybrid structures based on cobalt nanomaterials, have been developed to
improve their magnetic, optical, catalytic, and structural properties.
4.1 Structural property
Cobalt oxides, especially CoO, often display a cubic spinel structure that is very stable and has
a large theoretical potential for energy storage [ ]. Alternatively, because of their diverse,
highly conductive structures, such as hexagonal and cubic phases, cobalt sulﬁdes exhibit higher
catalytic activity and superior electronic conductivity for water splitting and are among the
most extensively studied materials [ ]. Crystallite size, morphology, and phase are among the
structural properties of cobalt-based nanomaterials that are primarily determined by the
synthesis process, annealing temperature, pH levels, and capping agents [ ]. It has been
reported that CoO nanoparticles usually adopt a cubic spinel crystal structure in which the
oxygen anions form a cubic close-packed lattice, while the cobalt cations (Co  and Co )
occupy two different types of interstitial sites [ ]. This structure is favourable for both HER and
OER reactions because of its mixed valence state (Co  and Co ) which facilitates electron
transport and active site generation [ ]. Tizazu et al. deposited cobalt sulﬁde thin ﬁlms with
varied phases, namely hexagonal CoS, face-centred cubic CoS , and cubic CoS [ ].
4.2 Optical properties
The optical and electronic properties of cobalt nanostructured materials are highly dependent
on the synthesis method, uniform distribution of activators in the host lattice, morphology,
crystal structure, and particle size, according to previously reported studies [ – ]. Recent
reports on the development of cobalt-based nanomaterials reveal that varying the particle size
through controlled synthesis, using various precursors, leads to signiﬁcant improvements in
their optical and electrical properties. Also, band gaps can be narrowed and engineered to
improve electronic conductivity and photo-absorption by combining metals, carbon, polymers,
and metal sulﬁde/oxide composites [ ]. In this regard, Montakan et al. reported the synthesis
of Ag-doped α -Co(OH) nanosheets by electrodeposition. The resulting materials exhibit a
narrower band gap (2.45 eV) than β -Co(OH) (2.85 eV), leading to higher electrical
conductivity. The signiﬁcant change in optical properties was attributed to the intercalation of
silver in adjacent α -Co(OH) layers [ ].
4.3 Magnetic behavior of cobalt nanomaterials

5. Structural and physicochemical characterization of cobalt
nanomaterials
Cobalt nanomaterials exhibit distinct structural, magnetic, electronic, and catalytic properties
arising from their reduced dimensions, high surface-to-volume ratio, and tunable crystal phases
(hexagonal close-packed (hcp), face-centred cubic (fcc), spinel CoO, CoO). These nanoscale
effects lead to behaviours not observed in bulk cobalt-based materials, such as super
paramagnetism, enhanced catalytic activity, and size-dependent optical transitions. These
unique properties, therefore, require specialized characterization techniques.
5.1 Morphology and size analysis
The chemical and physical properties of materials, and consequently, their applications, are
signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced by morphology. The morphology of cobalt nanomaterials, whether
spherical, rod-like, cubic, ﬂower-like, or core–shell, strongly inﬂuences their magnetic
anisotropy, catalytic activity, and oxidation behaviour [ ]. For example, cobalt nanorods
exhibit higher coercivity than spherical nanoparticles due to shape anisotropy, while porous
CoO nanostructures show enhanced electrochemical performance [ ].
These materials are characterized by techniques that include scanning electron microscopy
(SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and atomic force microscopy (AFM).
The SEM technique can provide information on the elemental composition, particle size
regularity, average size, and microstructure, and it offers high-resolution imaging of the surface
morphology of nanomaterials. Scanning electron microscopy provides high-resolution images
of surface topography and is widely used to examine Co-NPs, cobalt nanowires, and CoO
nanoﬂakes [ , ]. SEM can reveal particle agglomeration, surface roughness, and
morphological uniformity, which are critical for applications such as magnetic storage and
catalysis [ ].

morphology, grain-boundary plane, and grain-boundary core of cobalt-based nanomaterials

5.2 Crystallinity and phase identiﬁcation
The crystallinity of cobalt nanomaterials, just like that of all other materials, inﬂuences their
applications. For this reason, it is important to have precise control over the lattice structure of
nanomaterials to enhance efficiency in processes such as hydrogen production. For many
applications, crystalline materials with tailored properties are desired, necessitating an
adjustment of the size or morphology [ ].
Cobalt nanomaterials can crystallize in multiple phases: hexagonal close-packed (hcp), face-
centred cubic (fcc), CoO, and CoO, each with distinct magnetic and catalytic properties. For
example, hcp cobalt typically exhibits higher magnetic anisotropy than fcc cobalt, while CoO
is a key material in electrochemical and photocatalytic applications [ , ]. Characterization
techniques such as powder X-ray diffraction (p XRD) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR)
spectroscopy provide insights into the crystal structure and lattice arrangement. The chemical
bonding and functional groups present on the nanoparticle surface, as well as the phase
transitions that occur during synthesis, could greatly impact the material’s ﬁnal properties [ ].
X-ray diffraction is used to distinguish between metallic cobalt phases (hcp vs. fcc) and cobalt
oxides. For instance, peaks at 2 θ  ≈ 44.2° and 51.5° indicate fcc Co, while peaks at 31.3°, 36.8°, and
59.3° correspond to CoO. XRD also reveals crystallite size (via the Scherrer equation), strain,
and phase purity.
Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) is a technique that allows for the identiﬁcation of
localized crystalline phases within a nanomaterial. Selected area electron diffraction patterns
can be used to identify crystal structures and measure lattice parameters [ ]. Selected area
electron diffraction patterns help identify localized phase distributions, which is important
because Co-NPs often develop oxide shells during synthesis or storage. Selected area electron
diffraction can distinguish between Co ⁰  cores and CoO/CoO shells, providing insight into
oxidation pathways and interface structures [ , ].

5.3 Surface chemistry and composition
The surface of cobalt nanomaterials is highly reactive and prone to oxidation, forming CoO or
CoO shells that signiﬁcantly alter magnetic and catalytic behaviour. Therefore, surface
characterization is crucial [ ]. The surfaces of cobalt nanomaterials have been analysed by X-
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy can differentiate
between Co  and Co  species, allowing for the identiﬁcation of CoO versus CoO. For
metallic Co-NPs, XPS often reveals a thin oxide layer, which inﬂuences magnetic relaxation,
catalytic activity, and biocompatibility [ ]. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy provides
information on the elemental composition and chemical states of nanomaterials’ surfaces. By
measuring the binding energies of core electrons, XPS provides detailed information about the
surface chemistry and oxidation states of the elements present [ , ]. The characterization of
cobalt nanomaterials’ surfaces has also been achieved using the energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopic (EDX) technique. This technique reveals information about the elemental
composition and has been used to determine the amount of cobalt in cobalt nanomaterials [ ].
Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopic conﬁrms elemental composition and is commonly used
to verify cobalt content in Co-based alloys, doped cobalt oxides (e.g., Ni-doped CoO), and
composite materials. In cobalt oxide nanoparticles, EDX typically shows strong Co and O peaks,
conﬁrming successful oxidation.
Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) has also been used to identify functional
groups and molecular bonding within nanomaterials. The technique involves measuring the
absorption of infrared light by the material, which provides information about its chemical
structure and interactions [ ].
5.4 Analyses of magnetic properties
Cobalt is inherently ferromagnetic, and its nanoscale forms exhibit size-dependent magnetic
behaviours such as superparamagnetism, enhanced coercivity, and tunable anisotropy.
Superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) magnetometry and/or vibrating
sample magnetometry (VSM) are primary high-sensitivity techniques for characterizing the
magnetic properties of cobalt nanomaterials across varying temperatures, ﬁelds, and
orientations. These methods measure magnetic moments to determine key parameters like
saturation magnetization, blocking temperature, and Curie temperature, which differ from bulk
behaviour due to nanoparticle size [ , ].

6. Applications of cobalt nanomaterials
Cobalt-based nanomaterials exhibit unique magnetic, electronic, and catalytic properties,
which make them highly versatile for energy, environmental, biomedical, and technological
applications.
6.1 Energy storage and conversion
Cobalt-based nanomaterials (oxides, sulﬁdes) play important roles as high-performance energy
storage and conversion materials, offering high-capacity anodes for lithium-ion batteries and
cathode materials in supercapacitors. Cobalt oxides (CoO, CoO) and cobalt-containing-
layered oxides (e.g., LiCoO) are widely used in lithium-ion batteries due to their high
theoretical capacities, stable cycling performance, and favourable redox behaviour. Nanoscale
cobalt oxides offer enhanced lithium diffusion and improved structural stability during charge-
discharge cycling. CoO nanostructures, such as nanowires, nanosheets, and hollow spheres,
exhibit high reversible capacities and reduced volume expansion, making them promising
anode materials [ , ]. Cobalt-based nanomaterials, particularly, CoO and Co(OH) exhibit
excellent pseudocapacitive behaviour. Their redox-active surfaces enable rapid charge transfer
and high speciﬁc capacitance. Nano structuring improves electrolyte accessibility and electron
transport, leading to enhanced energy and power densities [ , , ].
Cobalt nanoparticles and cobalt nitrogen-doped carbon materials serve as cost-effective
alternatives to platinum catalysts in fuel cells. Their catalytic activity toward ORR is attributed
to Co–N ₓ  active sites and high conductivity. Cobalt oxides also function as catalysts for OER in
alkaline fuel cells [ ]. Cobalt-based electrocatalysts are widely studied for HER, OER, and

6.2 Catalysis
Cobalt-based nanomaterials, with their high surface area and magnetic properties, have been
known to function as cost-effective and durable catalysts in a number of chemical conversions,
energy storage, water splitting, CO hydrogenation, and pollutant degradation.
Cobalt nanoparticles supported on oxides (Al O, SiO, TiO) are key catalysts for Fischer–
Tropsch synthesis, converting syngas (CO + H) into long-chain hydrocarbons. Their high
activity, selectivity toward linear alkanes, and resistance to deactivation make cobalt catalysts
superior to iron for low-temperature FT processes [ ]. Additionally, cobalt nanocatalysts
efficiently catalyse the hydrogenation of nitriles, oleﬁns, and carbonyl compounds. Their high
surface area and tunable oxidation states facilitate selective transformations. Cobalt oxides and
cobalt phosphides also show promise in dehydrogenation reactions, including ammonia borane
decomposition for hydrogen storage [ ].
Cobalt oxides are effective catalysts for CO oxidation, volatile organic compound (VOC)
degradation, and the removal of nitrogen oxides. Their redox ﬂexibility (Co⁺ /Co⁺ ) enhances
oxygen mobility and catalytic turnover. Nanostructured CoO, in particular, exhibits high
activity at low temperatures [ ].
6.3 Biomedical applications
Cobalt-based nanomaterials are ﬁnding applications in biomedicine for cancer therapy,
antimicrobial agents, drug delivery, and diagnostic imaging due to their magnetic and
photocatalytic properties.
Surface-functionalized Co-NPs can be engineered for targeted drug delivery. Their magnetic
properties enable magnetic guidance and controlled release, while coatings such as polyethylene
glycol (PEG) or silica improve biocompatibility [ ]. Cobalt ferrite (CoFeO) nanoparticles
exhibit strong magnetic anisotropy and high relaxivity, making them promising T MRI
contrast agents. Their stability and tunable magnetic properties offer advantages over
conventional iron oxide nanoparticles [ ]. Cobalt-based magnetic nanoparticles can generate
heat under AMFs, enabling magnetic hyperthermia for cancer treatment. Their high saturation
magnetization allows efficient heat generation at low doses [ ]. Cobalt oxide nanoparticles
exhibit antimicrobial properties against bacteria and fungi due to reactive oxygen species (ROS)
generation and membrane disruption. Their activity can be enhanced through doping or surface
modiﬁcation [ ].

6.5 Magnetic nanomaterials and electronic devices
Cobalt nanoparticles have been shown to exhibit strong ferromagnetic behaviour, making them
excellent candidates for advanced magnetic and electronic applications, especially in high-
density data storage, spintronics, and as cathode materials in lithium-ion batteries due to their
electrochemical properties. Cobalt-based magnetic nanoparticles and thin ﬁlms are essential in
high-density data storage due to their high coercivity and thermal stability. CoPt and CoCr
alloys are widely used in magnetic recording media [ ]. Cobalt nanomaterials exhibit strong
spin polarization, making them suitable for spintronic devices such as magnetic tunnel
junctions and spin valves. Their integration into nanoscale architectures enables faster and more
energy-efficient electronics [ ]. Cobalt oxides and cobalt-based composites are used in gas
sensors, strain sensors, and magnetic actuators. Their sensitivity to gases such as CO, H, and
ethanol arises from surface redox reactions and conductivity changes [ , ].
7. Toxicity, environmental impact, and safety considerations of
cobalt nanomaterials
Cobalt nanomaterials have gained signiﬁcant attention due to their multiple applications in the
food industry, cosmetics, medicine, and agriculture. However, there is signiﬁcant concern about
their safety [ ]. Considering the various biomedical applications, it is crucial to evaluate the
biocompatibility of cobalt nanomaterials to ensure they are safe for use in the food industry and
medicine for therapeutic and diagnostic purposes.

Although cobalt is an important metal in the human body, it has systemic toxicity, including
neurological, cardiovascular, and endocrine disorders, mainly attributable to free ionic Co (II)
(with blood concentrations of more than 300 µg/L), which is assumed to cause anxiety. It has
been found to have the ability to replace iron in metalloenzymes with the formation of
substituted-inert complexes. Cobalt has been reported to induce mitochondrial impairment,
including the inhibition of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, where cobalt enters
mitochondria and reduces the activity of respiratory chain enzymes, causing the mitochondrial
membrane to collapse [ , ]. Cobalt has also been reported to show inhibitory effects on the
precursor processing of a single cytochrome c oxidase (COX) subunit and COX in mitochondria
[ , ]. In particular, the replacement of iron with cobalt in prolyl-4-hydroxylase suppresses the
regular activity of the enzyme, which activates transcription sensitive to hypoxia, thereby
causing hypoxia in mammalian cells [ ]. Furthermore, due to their unique physicochemical
properties, such as composition, morphology, charge, and size, they can adsorb various
substances from biological media onto their surface. It has long been known that nanoparticles
of a speciﬁc size, shape, and charge can pass through the blood-brain barrier and interact with
the central nervous system [ , ].
Also, exposure to Co-NPs has been associated with neurodegenerative disorders, while the
mitochondrial-associated mechanisms that mediate their neurotoxicity have yet to be fully
characterized. A report showed that exposure to cobalt nanomaterials reduces survival and
lifespan in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans. It has also been shown that exposure to Co-NPs
aggravates the induction of paralysis and the aggregation of β -amyloid (A β ) [ ]. These
effects are accompanied by ROS overproduction, ATP reduction, as well as mitochondrial
fragmentation [ ].
In addition, important cellular mechanisms, such as ROS generation, superoxide dismutase, and
catalase activity, have been directly linked to the widespread mechanism of cytotoxicity
responsible for causing damage to DNA during the cellular uptake of CoO nanoparticles. The
cellular stress also initiates DNA damage. Nanoparticle toxicity begins with ROS generation,
which leads to the onset of oxidative stress [ ].
In occupational settings, exposure to cobalt, Co-NPs, and its compounds may lead to various
lung diseases, including interstitial pneumonitis, ﬁbrosis, and asthma. Research has
demonstrated that there is a high carcinogenic potential of cobalt and its compounds in
experimental animals [ ].
While relatively few details are known about the toxicity of Co-NPs, studies have shown that
nanoparticles transported through sensory nerves can reach various brain regions, including the
olfactory bulb, cortex, striatum, hippocampus, cerebellum, and brainstem. As these nerve
pathways traverse the brain, nanoparticles are deposited across multiple brain areas. Notably,
nanoparticles entering the brain through the olfactory nerve accumulate signiﬁcantly in the
olfactory bulb. The initial localization of the nanoparticles often coincides with their functional
target sites in the brain. Once inside the central nervous system, Co-NPs can induce various
ultrastructural alterations. These include mitochondrial dysfunction and damage to the nuclear
architecture [ ].

7.2 Environmental persistence of Co-NPs
There has been intensive development in nanotechnology and a rapid increase in the production
of nanoscale systems, increasing the rate at which nanoparticles enter soil, air, and water,
directly affecting plants. The accumulation of cobalt nanomaterials in soil can pose an
environmental risk, with potential long-term effects on soil health, plant life, and the broader
ecosystem. As Co-NPs accumulate in the soil, they can persist for extended periods due to their
stability and resistance to degradation [ ]. Co-NPs can accumulate in the soil through various
pathways, including the disposal of industrial waste, the use of nanoparticle-containing
fertilizers or pesticides, and atmospheric deposition. Once in the soil, the Co-NPs can be taken
up by plants and enter the food chain. This could have cascading effects on the entire ecosystem,
including the potential for bioaccumulation in higher trophic levels [ , ].
7.3 Safety, handling, disposal, and regulatory perspectives
Due to the extensive applications of cobalt nanomaterials, there are substantial concerns
regarding their safe use, necessitating more stringent nanotoxicity regulations. One very
important aspect of product safety is proper labelling, which is an essential tool to provide
complete and accurate information to consumers related to ensuring safety and health,
compliance with regulations, and enabling informed decision-making [ , ]. Because of the
toxicity risk, it is, therefore, important to always handle cobalt nanomaterials in a certiﬁed
chemical fume hood to prevent the inhalation of toxic dust and to ensure the mandatory use of
personal protective equipment.
Traditional waste disposal methods are not always effective for nanomaterials. New technologies
and methodologies need to be developed to cope with these unique substances. Considering the
complex nature of cobalt nanomaterials, combined with the relatively new ﬁeld, effective and
safe disposal guidelines for nanomaterials involve a multidisciplinary approach, combining
rigorous scientiﬁc research with practical waste management techniques. So far, the disposal of
Co-NPs has been carried out through landﬁlls and incineration, although there are fears of
releasing some nanoparticles into the atmosphere during incineration [ ].
A crucial obstacle hindering nanotoxicology’s progress is the absence of uniﬁed and deﬁned
methodologies for characterizing NPs and assessing their associated risks. The US Food and
Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have established
detailed regulatory frameworks and guidance documents to approve drugs and medical devices,
offering a foundation that could be adapted to regulate biomedical nanomaterials. Global
regulatory bodies are actively addressing the multifaceted challenges posed by the unique

8. Challenges and future perspectives
One of the major challenges associated with cobalt nanomaterials is their susceptibility to
oxidation and structural degradation under operational conditions, which can signiﬁcantly alter
their magnetic, catalytic, and electronic properties. Although numerous laboratory-scale
synthetic methods have been developed for cobalt nanomaterials, including hydrothermal,
solvothermal, organometallic decomposition, and template-assisted approaches, scaling these
methods to industrial production remains challenging. Batch-to-batch variability and
difficulties in controlling morphology at large scale further complicate commercialization.
Green synthesis approaches using biological templates or low-energy processes offer promising
alternatives but often lack precise control over particle size and crystallinity [ ]. The
integration of cobalt nanomaterials into practical devices presents additional challenges related
to material compatibility, long-term reliability, and manufacturing constraints. For example, in
energy storage systems, cobalt nanostructures must maintain structural integrity during
repeated charge–discharge cycles and operate safely under varying temperatures and
electrolytes [ ].
Despite current challenges, several emerging research directions offer exciting opportunities for
advancing cobalt nanomaterials. Single-atom catalysts, where isolated cobalt atoms are
dispersed on conductive supports such as graphene or nitrogen-doped carbon, have
demonstrated exceptional catalytic efficiency due to maximum atom utilization and unique
electronic structures [ ]. Another transformative direction is the application of machine
learning in materials discovery and optimization [ ]. Coupling computational modelling with
high-throughput experimentation is expected to signiﬁcantly reduce development time for
next-generation materials. Emphasis on environmentally responsible production, recycling
strategies, and cobalt-efficient materials will also be critical to ensure long-term technological
and societal impact.
