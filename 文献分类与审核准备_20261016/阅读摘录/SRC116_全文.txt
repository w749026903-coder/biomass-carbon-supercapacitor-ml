ABSTRACT: Pesticide residues in aquatic systems pose persistent risks to
ecosystems and public health, underscoring the need for low-cost and
sustainable remediation strategies. In this study, biochars prepared from
Conocarpus branch waste (CBB) and lemon tree branch waste (LBB) via
slow pyrolysis at 450 °C were evaluated as adsorbents for two widely
applied agrochemicals: imidacloprid (IMI) and myclobutanil (MYC).
Comprehensive physicochemical characterization (SEM, TEM, BET, and
elemental analysis) revealed mesoporous structures with distinct surface
features. Batch adsorption experiments demonstrated strong dependence
on solution pH, adsorbent dosage, contact time, and initial pesticide
concentration. Under near-neutral conditions (pH 7.0, 5 g L
−1
, 25 °C, 24
h), maximum capacities reached 8.69 mg g
−1
(IMI) and 12.31 mg g
−1
(MYC) for CBB. Adsorption kinetics followed a pseudo-second-order
model (R
2
> 0.98), while equilibrium data fit both Langmuir and the Freundlich isotherms (R
2
≥ 0.99). Thermodynamic analyses
(ΔG° = −1.3 to −4.5 kJ mol
−1
; ΔH° = −16.4 to −29.8 kJ mol
−1
) indicated spontaneous, exothermic processes dominated by
physisorption. The reusability of both biochars across multiple cycles further confirmed their practical applicability. These results
highlight pruning-waste biochars as effective and sustainable alternatives to conventional adsorbents for mitigating pesticide
pollution in water.

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Preparation and Characterization of Biochar. Air-
dried pruning residues from Conocarpus and lemon trees were
sorted to remove dust, bark fragments, and leaves; the branch
fractions only were retained for pyrolysis. Sorted material was
washed with deionized water, oven-dried at 105 °C to constant
mass, cut to 2−3 cm, and then pyrolyzed at 450 °C for 4 h

ground and sieved to <190 μm. After cooling to room
temperature, the resulting biochars were mechanically ground
in a clean stainless-steel grinder and sieved to <190 μm to
standardize particle size for adsorption tests (details mentioned
in Supporting Information 2.2). Then subjected to phys-
icochemical characterization. Surface morphology was exam-
ined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (FEI Inspect
S50, Netherlands), elemental composition by energy-dispersive
X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Bruker, Germany), and surface
area and porosity by Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET)
nitrogen adsorption−desorption analysis (Belsorp Max II
Microtrac, Japan).
2.2. Batch Adsorption Experiments. Batch adsorption
experiments were conducted to evaluate the removal of
imidacloprid (IMI) and myclobutanil (MYC) from aqueous
solutions. Parameters examined included pH (4−10),
adsorbent dosage (1−10 g L
−1
), contact time (10 min−24
h), and initial pesticide concentration. All experiments were
performed in triplicate at 25 ± 1 °C with continuous shaking
to ensure equilibrium conditions.
2.3. LC-MS/MS Quantitation and Method Validation.
Quantification was performed by UHPLC−MS/MS (triple
quadrupole, MRM) on a C18 column with water/methanol
(each containing 1% formic acid + 5 mM ammonium formate)
under gradient elution. Matrix-matched calibration and quality
controls were used to correct for matrix effects. Method
validation (linearity, LOD/LOQ, precision) followed standard
acceptance criteria aligned with SANTE/11312/2021. Full
operating conditions and MRM transitions are provided in the
Supporting Information (2.10. LC-MS/MS). Method valida-
tion metrics are presented in Table 1.
2.4. Data Analysis and Modeling. Removal efficiency
was calculated based on the difference between initial and
equilibrium concentrations, and adsorption data were fitted to
kinetic (pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order) and equili-
brium isotherm (Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubi-
nin−Radushkevich) models. Thermodynamic parameters
(ΔG°, ΔH°, ΔS°) were derived from temperature-dependent
adsorption experiments. Comprehensive experimental proto-
cols, instrument settings, calibration data, and model-fitting
procedures are provided in the Supporting Information.

The particle size distribution, determined by laser diffraction
(Malvern Mastersizer 2000E) after ultrasonic dispersion in
deionized water, indicated mean particle diameters of
approximately 8 μm for CBB and 5 μm for LBB, confirming
that LBB consisted of slightly finer particles. In contrast, the
pore size distribution derived from N
2
adsorption−desorption
data using the Barrett, Joyner, and Halenda (BJH) method

both materials (Figure 2). Thus, “particle size” refers to the
external physical dimensions of ground biochar particles,
whereas “pore size” describes the internal porous network.
Nitrogen adsorption−desorption isotherms (Figure 2)
confirmed the presence of mesoporosity in both materials,
displaying Type IV curves with H3 hysteresis loops. CBB
showed a sharper increase in nitrogen uptake at high relative
pressures (P/P
0
> 0.8), indicating the presence of larger
mesopores and a broader distribution. LBB, in contrast,
exhibited a smoother uptake profile, consistent with narrower
pores. These textural variations highlight that adsorption
efficiency will differ depending on pesticide molecular size.
SEM micrographs (Figure 3) provided additional insights
into morphology. CBB displayed a rough, porous surface with
interconnected voids, consistent with its larger pore volume.
LBB surfaces appeared smoother with smaller, rounded pores
at low magnification, and fibrous, compact features at higher
magnification. These differences underline the suitability of
CBB for the adsorption of bulky molecules, while LBB’s denser
morphology may favor applications involving smaller species or
catalysis.

surface deprotonation or reduced pesticide affinity at higher

higher pH.

3.3.3. Contact Time. The influence of contact time on the
adsorption of imidacloprid (IMI) and myclobutanil (MYC)
was evaluated under controlled conditions (initial pesticide
concentration: 10 mg L
−1
; biochar dosage: 5 g L
−1
; pH 7). The
results are presented in Figure 9. Adsorption occurred rapidly
within the first 40 min, after which the rate plateaued,
indicating near-saturation of the available active sites.
Equilibrium was reached within 24 h, with maximum removal
efficiencies of 89% for IMI and 90% for MYC.
The rapid initial uptake is attributed to the abundant
availability of accessible binding sites on the biochar surface. As
adsorption progressed, site occupancy and repulsive inter-
actions between sorbed molecules slowed the rate of uptake,
leading to equilibrium. This trend aligns with previous studies
suggesting that sorbent−pollutant interactions at optimal pH
are facilitated by the presence of active chelating or functional
sites on biochar surfaces.
50
These findings demonstrate that biochar can achieve high
removal efficiencies for both pesticides within relatively short
timeframes, making it suitable for practical water treatment
applications. The equilibrium time of ∼40 min can thus be
considered optimal for subsequent kinetic modeling and
process design.
3.3.4. Initial Pesticide Concentration Influence. Figure 10
illustrates the effect of initial pesticide concentration on the
adsorption of imidacloprid (IMI) and myclobutanil (MYC)
onto CBB and LBB. At lower initial concentrations, removal
efficiency was high due to the abundance of vacant active sites
relative to the number of pesticide molecules. As concen-
trations increased, adsorption capacity (mg g
−1
) rose for both
pesticides, reflecting enhanced mass transfer driving forces
between solution and adsorbent.
For CBB, adsorption of IMI increased from 1.40 to 3.75 mg
g
−1
as the initial concentration rose, while MYC adsorption
increased from 0.80 to 2.75 mg g
−1
. LBB exhibited comparable
trends, with IMI and MYC adsorption capacities reaching ∼4.4
mg g
−1
at higher initial concentrations. These results
demonstrate that both biochars maintain good sorption
performance as pollutant loading increases.
However, beyond a threshold concentration, removal
efficiency (%) began to decline. This decline is attributed to
the saturation of biochar active sites and reduced mass transfer
efficiency at higher pesticide loadings, leading to the
accumulation of unadsorbed molecules in solution.
These findings indicate that both CBB and LBB are effective
at low-to-moderate pesticide concentrations, but optimization
of adsorbent dosage relative to pollutant load is essential to
maximize performance in practical water treatment scenarios.
3.3.5. Adsorption Mechanism. Adsorption is a key process
governing the retention of pesticide residues in environmental
systems. It is a combined physicochemical phenomenon
involving both physical adsorption (physisorption) and
chemical adsorption (chemisorption), where pesticide mole-
cules interact with the surfaces of adsorbent particles through
van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic
attraction, π−π interactions, and, in some cases, covalent-like
bonding.
51,52
For CBB and LBB, adsorption occurs via multiple pathways.
Electrostatic interactions arise between the charged functional
groups on biochar surfaces (e.g., −OH) and the ionizable
groups of pesticide molecules. Size exclusion also plays a role,
as the pore dimensions of biochar can restrict the entry of
larger molecules, favoring adsorption at accessible mesoporous
sites. The porous structure and surface area of the biochars
thus enhance physisorption by providing abundant sorption
sites.

Chemical adsorption is driven by the oxygen-containing
functional groups on biochar, such as hydroxyl, carboxyl,
amide, and aromatic moieties, which engage in hydrogen
bonding with pesticide molecules. In particular, the hydroxyl
groups of biochar can form stable hydrogen bonds with the

donor−acceptor interactions further contribute, as the
aromatic structures of biochar can interact with aromatic
rings and electron-deficient regions of pesticide molecules.

The relative adsorption performance of MYC and IMI can
be explained by their molecular structures. MYC, which
contains a triazole ring and a chlorophenyl moiety, exhibits
stronger π−π stacking and hydrogen bonding with biochar’s
aromatic and oxygenated functional groups. Its lower water
solubility and higher log P value also enhance its affinity for
biochar surfaces. In contrast, IMI is more soluble in water and
less hydrophobic, which reduces its interaction with the
carbon-rich biochar surfaces under comparable conditions.
The adsorption of pesticides onto CBB and LBB involves
synergistic physisorption and chemisorption mechanisms. The
higher adsorption of MYC relative to IMI reflects differences in
their hydrophobicity, aromaticity, and functional group
chemistry, which influence the strength and stability of their
interactions with biochar.

3.6. Effects of Temperature and Thermodynamic
Analysis. The influence of temperature on pesticide
adsorption was investigated to evaluate the thermodynamic
feasibility of the process (Figure 15a). Adsorption efficiency
decreased with increasing temperature for both CBB and LBB,
confirming that the process is exothermic. This decline can be
attributed to (i) increased molecular mobility of imidacloprid
(IMI) and myclobutanil (MYC) in solution, (ii) possible
structural changes or reduced activity of adsorption sites at
elevated temperatures, and (iii) enhanced desorption of
pesticide molecules back into the aqueous phase.
Thermodynamic parameters (ΔG°, ΔH°, ΔS°) were
calculated using the Van’t Hoff equation (Figure 15b; Table
7). The ΔG° values for all systems were negative across the
studied temperature range, confirming the spontaneous nature
of adsorption. For CBB, ΔG° values ranged from −1.315 to
−2.483 kJ mol
−1
(IMI) and −3.040 to −4.462 kJ mol
−1
(MYC). For LBB, ΔG° ranged from −2.849 to −4.002 kJ
mol
−1
(IMI) and −1.521 to −3.706 kJ mol
−1
(MYC).
The enthalpy changes (ΔH°) were −16.39 and −21.42 kJ
mol
−1
for IMI adsorption on CBB and LBB, respectively, and
−17.74 and −29.76 kJ mol
−1
for MYC adsorption. These
negative values confirm that the process is exothermic and
predominantly physical in nature, as adsorption enthalpies <40
kJ mol
−1
are typically associated with physisorption.
63,64
Entropy changes (ΔS°) were negative for all systems,
suggesting reduced randomness at the solid−liquid interface as
pesticide molecules became more ordered upon adsorption.
Taken together, the thermodynamic results demonstrate that
pesticide adsorption onto CBB and LBB is spontaneous,
exothermic, and primarily governed by physical interactions,
with MYC showing a stronger affinity than IMI.

3.8. Removal Efficiency of Imidacloprid and Myclo-
butanil Formulations. To evaluate the real-world applic-
ability of the biochars, adsorption experiments were conducted
using pesticide formulation solutions at a sprayer concen-
tration of 50 mg L
−1
, the recommended application rate for
both imidacloprid (IMI) and myclobutanil (MYC). Such
formulations represent realistic pesticide wastes, including
leftover spray mixtures and sprayer wash solutions, which
require treatment before disposal. Unlike pure active
ingredient solutions, formulations also contain adjuvants that
may reduce adsorption efficiency by competing for active sites
or by enhancing pesticide solubility.
The results showed that removal efficiency increased with
biochar dosage for both pesticides (Table 8). CBB consistently
outperformed LBB, likely due to its larger pore diameter
(17.26 nm vs 14.99 nm), which provides greater accessibility
for pesticide molecules. Across both adsorbents, MYC removal
was higher than IMI removal, attributable to MYC’s lower
water solubility and higher log P value, which favor sorption
onto carbon-rich surfaces.
These findings demonstrate that biochars derived from CBB
and LBB are capable of effectively removing pesticide
formulations under realistic agricultural waste conditions.
The superior performance of CBB, combined with the higher
affinity of MYC, highlights the importance of tailoring biochar
selection to specific pesticide properties and waste manage-
ment needs.
3.9. Rapid Removal at High Biochar Dosage. A rapid
adsorption experiment was conducted to evaluate the efficiency
of CBB and LBB in removing a mixed solution of imidacloprid
(IMI) and myclobutanil (MYC) under conditions simulating
urgent water treatment scenarios. The test used a pesticide
concentration of 50 mg L
−1
for each compound and a high
biochar dosage of 100 mg L
−1
, with a short contact time of 30
min.
As shown in Table 9, removal efficiencies followed the same
trends observed under equilibrium conditions (24 h, lower
dosage). CBB exhibited higher adsorption capacity than LBB
for both pesticides, consistent with its larger pore size and
greater pore volume. MYC removal exceeded IMI removal
across both biochars, reflecting its lower solubility and greater
hydrophobicity, which enhance sorption affinity.
These findings demonstrate that effective pesticide removal
can be achieved within 30 min at higher biochar dosages,
highlighting the feasibility of using CBB and LBB for rapid-
response water treatment applications. Such performance is
especially relevant for agricultural runoff or accidental pesticide
spills, where immediate mitigation is required.

4. CONCLUSION
This study demonstrated the potential of biochars derived
from Conocarpus branch (CBB) and lemon branch (LBB)
wastes as efficient, low-cost adsorbents for removing the
pesticides imidacloprid (IMI) and myclobutanil (MYC) from
aqueous solutions. Both biochars were successfully synthesized
and characterized, revealing distinct physicochemical proper-
ties that influenced their adsorption performance.
Adsorption equilibrium data fitted well to the Langmuir,
Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubinin−Radushkevich models, with
the Langmuir isotherm providing the best description,
indicating predominantly monolayer adsorption. Kinetic
studies showed that adsorption followed a pseudo-second-
order model, suggesting chemisorption as the rate-limiting
step. Thermodynamic analysis confirmed that the process was
spontaneous and exothermic, with negative ΔG° and ΔH°
values consistent with physisorption−chemisorption interac-
tions.
Importantly, both CBB and LBB exhibited strong removal
efficiencies under realistic conditions, including pesticide
formulations and rapid adsorption scenarios, with CBB
generally outperforming LBB due to its larger pore structure.
Reusability tests confirmed that both biochars retained
significant adsorption capacity after multiple cycles, supporting
their potential for sustainable application.
These findings highlight pruning waste-derived biochars as
promising alternatives to conventional adsorbents for pesticide
remediation in contaminated water systems. Future studies
should focus on scaling these findings to pilot-scale treatment
systems, optimizing regeneration protocols, and evaluating
performance in complex natural water matrices.
