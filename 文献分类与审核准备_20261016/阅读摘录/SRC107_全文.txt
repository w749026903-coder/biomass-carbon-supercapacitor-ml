ABSTRACT
Over the years, porous carbons have garnered immense research interest due to their abundance, 
cost-effectiveness, and promising performance across a range of applications, including energy 
storage, gas separation, and catalysis. With growing demand for eco-friendly materials, biomass-
derived hard carbons have gained increasing attention. This study investigates the potential of 
porous carbons synthesized from locally sourced bay leaves by carbonizing at varying 
temperatures for batteries and supercapacitor applications. The porous carbon produced at 800 ℃ 
demonstrated an impressive specific capacitance of 689 F g−1 at a current density of 1 A g−1 in a 2 
M NaOH electrolyte. The device attained an energy density of about 33.3 Wh kg −1 at a power 
density of 1098.8 W kg −1. Moreover, porous carbon synthesized at a mild carbonization 
temperature exhibits excellent battery performance, delivering a reversible capacity of 310 mAh 
g−1 at 30 mA g−1, with high capacity retention and rate capability. This work presents a viable path 
towards low-cost, scalable, and sustainable waste (bay leaves) for producing porous hard carbon 
and demonstrates its use for energy storage applications.
Keywords: Biomass, hard carbon, supercapacitor, sodium-ion battery

1. Introduction
In response to interrelated issues of excessive fossil fuel depletion, climate change, and population 
growth, increasing attention is being drawn to sustainable and green energy sources for future 
energy storage, conversion, and utilization. In this regard, high-performing energy storage devices 
with substantial energy and power density are important for the development of compact gadgets 
and e-vehicles in the modern era. Among numerous electrical energy storage systems, batteries 
and supercapacitors (SCs) have been of particular significance for these applications. 1 SCs are 
promising technologies due to their improved safety, fast charge and discharge times, high power 
density, and extended lifespan. However, SCs have low energy densities.2 On the other hand, high-
energy-density batteries with extended cycle life and acceptable rate efficiency are crucial for 
advancing durable portable electronics and electric cars. These essential properties of lithium-ion 
batteries (LIBs) have led to their widespread commercialization. 3 Nevertheless, their high cost, 
due to their low abundance and restricted distribution, has put their use at risk. As a result, similar 
alternatives, such as sodium-ion batteries (SIBs), are more cost-effective due to their plentiful 
supply and favorable outcomes.4,5 
As electrode materials constitute the core of energy-storing devices, developing efficient, 
cost-effective electrode materials is the prime challenge in energy storage applications. Numerous 
materials studied and used in the energy storage devices to date include carbon-based materials, 
metal oxides and hydroxides, composite materials, and conducting polymers. Among these, 
polymers and metal oxides/hydroxides involve high costs and are detrimental to the environment 
because they are not sustainable.6,7 Though graphite is being utilized widely in commercial LIBs, 
its limiting thermodynamic compatibility and inefficient interlayer spacing to accommodate Na⁺ 
ions make it undesirable for SIBs. 8,9 Similarly, numerous carbonaceous materials such as carbon 
nanotubes, carbon spheres, graphene, hollow carbon particles, carbon black have been taken into 
account for SCs application, but the poor cost-performance ratio, challenging synthesis 
procedures, and their procreant intermediates restrict their commercial use. 10 Therefore, it is 
imperative to develop porous carbons with easy availability, renewable, low cost, and promising 
performance for energy storage applications. 11,12 For instance, disordered carbons such as hard 
carbon (HC) and soft carbon have been extensively studied as alternatives to conventional 
materials. One of the most apparent aspects of HC is its cost-effectiveness as it can be generated 
from easily available precursors, including used wastes, commercial polymers, dumped electronic

Biomass has innately good molecular and pore structures that are good for charge 
storage/transportation by reducing the ion diffusion pathways and preventing a significant 
irreversible capacity. Unlike the graphite anode used in batteries, HC electrodes exhibit desirable 
characteristics, including enhanced safety, higher rate performance, longer lifespan, and increased 
capacity.16 Darjazi et al. developed a promising candidate for both LIB/SIBs and SCs via pyrolysis 
of olive leaves followed by activation. They obtained a specific capacitance of 169.6 F g −1 at 0.5 
A g−1 as SC, and specific capacities of 265 and 150 mAh g−1 at 300 and 1500 mA g−1, respectively, 
with excellent cyclic stability. 17 Similarly, another waste-derived activated carbon from cherry 
flower (CFAC) prepared by Bhattarai et al. was demonstrated as an electrode material for both SC 
and SIB applications. The developed CFAC electrode material delivered 333.8 F g−1.  Furthermore, 
the same materials showed an average specific capacity of 150 mAh g −1, maintaining a high 
retention rate of> 84% over 200 charge/discharge cycles. 18 Similarly, Zhao et al. reported that a 
3D hierarchical porous carbon with a nanocage-like structure, derived from spring onion peel, 
could attain a high specific capacitance of 490.7 F g −1 at 1.0 A g −1 in a 3-electrode setup as well 
as an energy density (ED) of 92.0 Wh kg −1 at a power density (PD) of 1800 W kg −1.19 They have 
also demonstrated a high reversible capacity of 318.2 mAh g −1 at 50 mA g −1 in  SIBs using the 
same electrode material. Chen et al. developed a scalable, eco-friendly route to produce carbon 
from biomass by pyrolyzing shiitake mushrooms, achieving 354.8 F g −1 at 1.0 A g−1. Further, the 
evaluated ED was about 55.2 Wh kg−1 at a PD of 1250 W kg−1. In addition, the same material was 
evaluated as an anode material for SIBs, producing excellent Na+ storage performance.20 
Herein, bay leaves are demonstrated for the first time as a precursor for producing porous 
hard carbon, i.e., bay leaves-derived porous carbon (BLPC), via a single-step carbonization at a 
mild temperature without activation. With a high carbohydrate content, dry dead bay leaves are an 
inexpensive biomass source of carbon with potential energy-storage applications. The synthesized 
porous carbon, i.e., BLPC-800, exhibits an exceptionally high specific capacitance of 689 F g−1 at 
a current density of 1 A g −1 in 2 M NaOH electrolyte. The symmetric SCs fabricated with BLPC

2. Materials and methods
2.1 Chemicals
Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), polyvinylidenedifluoride (PVDF), and hydrochloric acid (HCl), were 
procured from Merck (India). Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) were 
obtained from Loba Chemie. Sodium perchlorate (NaClO 4), ethylene carbonate (EC), and 
propylene carbonate (PC) were procured from Sigma Aldrich. All chemicals were used as received 
without further purification. 
2.2 Materials synthesis
The sweet bay tree's leaf (Laurus nobilis or Bay leaves) is a native of nations that lie along the 
Mediterranean Sea. Bay leaves are used extensively in various applications, including food and 
medical treatment. The following nutrients are found in one ounce of bay leaf: 12–13 g 
carbohydrates, 1–1.2 g protein, 1–1.5 mg iron (Fe), 51–53 mg calcium (Ca), a little fat, potassium, 
and vitamins. It is known that one of the main sources of functional carbon is carbohydrates. As a 
result, the minerals found naturally in dead bay leaves ought to aid in the creation of pores in the 
carbon without activation, as will be covered later.21,22 The fallen dried bay leaves (Fig. 1a) were 
collected, cleaned, and then dried in an oven at 70 °C to remove residual moisture. The dried leaves 
were then ground to obtain a powder (Fig. 1b) for carbonization. Specifically, 10 g of bay leaves 
were carbonized for 3 h in a tube furnace at three varying temperatures, i.e., 700 ℃, 800 ℃, and 
900 ℃, keeping the 5 °C min−1 for heating under an Ar flow to have an inert atmosphere, and the 
obtained products are labeled as BLPC-700, BLPC-800, and BLPC-900, respectively. Scheme 1 
presents the schematic of the carbonization process. The porous carbon obtained was subsequently 
washed multiple times with acid, deionized water, and pure ethanol until the mixture reached pH 
7, ensuring the removal of any contaminants. The final products were dried in a vacuum oven at 
80 ℃ for 8 h to obtain porous carbon powders (Fig. 1c).

2.3 Materials characterization 
The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the samples were obtained using a diffractometer 
(Bruker, D8 – Advance XRD, Cu-Ka, λ = 1.5406 Å) to determine the structural properties of the 
synthesized carbon products. The morphologies and microstructures of the samples were examined 
with a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) (GEMINI 500, Zeiss) and a 
transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM- 2010HR), respectively. An iQ2 autosorb 
automated gas sorption system (Quantachrome, USA) was used to determine the surface area of 
the hierarchical carbon products using nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms at 77 K. Raman 
spectroscopy was used to examine structural imperfections and the degree of graphitization. A 
Horiba T6400 with an Ar-Krypton mixed ion gas laser of λ = 514.5 nm served to record the Raman 
spectra at room temperature between 500 cm−1 and 1800 cm−1.
2.4 Electrochemical measurements
All electrochemical investigations for supercapacitors were conducted using a CHI 760D 
electrochemical analyzer (CH Instruments, Inc., USA) in both 3-electrode and 2-electrode 
configurations. Testing was performed using a 3-electrode setup in 2 M NaOH solution, with 
chronopotentiometry, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and cyclic voltammetry 
(CV). The synthesized carbon materials were used as active materials for electrode fabrication. To 
fabricate the working electrode, a slurry was prepared by mixing 80 wt% BLPC (active

3. Results and discussion
3.1 Morphology
FESEM was used to examine the morphology of the synthesized BLPCs. Fig. 2a depicts the 
layered features of the product (BLPC-700) obtained by carbonization at 700 ℃. On the other 
hand, a more porous-like structure is seen for BLPC-800 and BLPC-900, as shown in Fig. 2b and 
Fig. 2c, respectively. At higher magnification, BLPC-700 (Fig. 2d) shows a mostly aggregated 
carbon structure, denser than those of other samples. In contrast, fine open pores are observed in 
BLPC-800 (Fig. 2e) and BLPC-900 (Fig. 2f) at higher magnification. These pores provide ion 
transport easily in electrochemical energy storage applications, thereby enhancing the overall 
functionality of the active materials.

3.2 Crystal structure 
The crystal structure and degree of graphitization of the synthesized carbon products are examined 
using XRD and Raman spectroscopy. Fig. 3a shows the XRD patterns of all the carbonized 
samples, which exhibit two broad peaks. The broad peaks at two theta ~23 and ~43 are assigned 
to the (002) and (100) reflections of graphite planes, suggesting the amorphous nature of the carbon 
species. The broad and low-intensity diffraction peaks represent a disordered turbostratic structure, 
which is a characteristic of carbon materials with an amorphous structure. 23,24 The average 
graphitic stacking height (Lc) representing the crystallite/domain height along the c-axis was 
determined using the Scherrer equation from the (100) peak. Subsequently, the average number of 
stacked graphene layers (N) was estimated using the relation N=[(Lc/d002)+1], where d002 denotes 
the interlayer spacing (Table 1). Lc of the domain is 1.147 nm for BLPC-800, suggesting nearly 4 
stacked graphene layers with an interlayer spacing of 0.379 nm, which favors a more reversible 
intercalation process. An increased interlayer spacing promotes sodium ion insertion, whereas a 
greater number of stacked graphene layers improves interlayer sodium storage by offering 
additional accessible intercalation sites.25 The Raman spectra pertaining to the produced samples 
are displayed in Fig. 3b. The spectra show two notable peaks at around 1350 and 1585 cm−1, which 
correspond to the D-band and G-band of carbonaceous materials, respectively. In general, the

3.5 Sodium-ion battery 
Batteries are another important energy storage technology, alongside supercapacitors. Recently, 
SIBs have attracted global interest, particularly because of their high storage capacity and lower 
cost of sodium.43 Carbons with the capacity to store sodium ions are being actively investigated as 
promising negative electrode materials. 44 In this work, the synthesized carbon materials (BLPC) 
are investigated as a potential anode component for SIBs due to their distinct structure and high 
SSA. The GCD profiles of BLPCs are shown in Fig. 6a at a current density of 30 mA g⁻ 1 (1C = 
300 mA g⁻1) in the voltage range of 1.5 – 0.005 V. The BLPC-800 delivers a superior reversible 
capacity of ~310 mAh g⁻1, with an excellent capacity retention of 87.4% after 50 cycles (Fig. 6b). 
On the other hand, BLPC-700 and BLPC-900 attain reversible capacities of ~265 and 295 mAh 
g⁻1, respectively, retaining specific capacities of about 84.2% and 88%, after 50 cycles. Moreover, 
BLPCs show excellent rate performance at varying C-rates ranging from 0.1C to 10C, as illustrated 
in Fig. 6c. The specific discharge capacities recorded for BLPC-800 were 246, 191, 155, 110, 62, 
and 24 mAh g⁻1 at the current rate of 0.2C, 0.5C, 1C, 2C, 5C and 10C, respectively. Interestingly, 
the specific discharge capacity recovered to 321 mAh g⁻¹ at 0.1C, or 103.5% of its initial value, 
after 35 cycles of the rate performance test. In addition, the repetitive cycling of BLPC-800 at 1C 
was performed (Fig. 6d) for 1000 long cycles, leading to the reversible capacity retention of 74%

nanopores in the porous carbon anodes (Fig. 6e).25 Moreover, a broader hump near 0.7 V suggests
