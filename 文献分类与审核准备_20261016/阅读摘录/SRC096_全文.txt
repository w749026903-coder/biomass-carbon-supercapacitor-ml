Recovery of Cathode and Solid Electrolyte in All-Solid-State Sodium-

ABSTRACT: Metals in spent all-solid-state sodium-ion batteries
(ASIBs) pose severe environmental pollution, resource depletion,
and health risks. However, selective and sustainable recovery of
these metals from ASIBs remains highly challenging. Herein, we
introduce a coordination-solvation dual-regulation (CSDR) mech-
anism that enables, for the first time, the recovery of both the
cathode and solid electrolyte from ASIBs with exceptional
selectivity and high sustainability. Guided by this CSDR
mechanism, amino acid−based green solvents are employed with
a dual function: abundant functional groups provide coordination
sites for target metal ions while facilitating favorable solvation
interactions, collectively enabling metal leaching. Mechanistically,
the superior Na over Zr selectivity originates from differential
binding energetics, Na
+
forms stable chelate complexes with amino acid ligands with lower binding free energies, whereas Zr
4+
,
owing to its higher charge density and stronger hydrolysis tendency, exhibits weaker coordination affinity and poorer solvation
compatibility, thus remaining preferentially in the solid residue.

Herein, we propose a coordination-solvation dual-regulation
(CSDR) mechanism using amino acid−based LoMMSs for the
selective recovery of metals from spent ASIBs. NMF and
NZSP were selected as the typical cathode and solid
electrolyte, respectively. Amino acids (Figure 1a) are chosen
as the hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs) for LoMMSs due to
their green and biodegradable nature, as well as the ability of
their amino and carboxyl groups to coordinate effectively with
metals. Notably, the functional amino and carboxyl groups in
HBAs not only offer strong coordination sites for metals but
also influence the solvation effect, enabling fine-tuning of metal
ion solubility and selectivity. By balancing coordination and
solvation regulation, the CSDR mechanism achieves remark-
able metal selectivity, thereby allowing efficient separation of
target metals from both the cathode and solid electrolyte.

2.5. Antisolvent Precipitation
The antisolvent precipitation was conducted by mixing 2 mL of
leachate with 2 mL of antisolvent, allowing the mixture to stand at
room temperature for 24 h, and then centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for
20 min to collect the precipitate. The supernatant was analyzed by
ICP-OES for metal ion concentration. The reuse of leachate was
conducted under the conditions of a solid-to-solvent ratio of 1:50 and
at 80 °C for 24 h.

3.2. CSDR Mechanism
The exceptional selectivity stems from the CSDR mechanism,
wherein the coordination and solvation effects of the amino-
acid−based LoMMSs exert a dual-regulatory influence on the
metal ions within ASIBs.
The molecular-level basis of the enhanced selectivity for Na
over transition metal Zr via the proposed CSDR mechanism is
investigated by using QM simulations of the metal ion,
LoMMSs, ion−LoMMSs, and ion−O. A lower ΔG value for
Zr
4+
−LoMMSs than for Na
+
−LoMMSs demonstrates that
LoMMSs coordinate more strongly with Zr
4+
than with Na
+
(Table 2). Theoretical calculation in Figure 2 also illustrates
that LoMMSs exhibit strong coordination interactions with
both Zr
4+
and Na
+
. Previous studies on both sodium-ion
(SIBs/ASIBs)
46−51
and lithium-ion batteries
53−58
further
support the role of coordination interactions in governing
metal leaching.
However, although Zr
4+
has a high theoretical affinity for
coordination, its solvation at the solid−liquid interface is
blocked by strong lattice confinement and steric hindrance,
allowing Na
+
to dominate the solvation process due to its low
desorption energy, high ionic mobility, and lower charge
density. Furthermore, a significantly lower ΔG value for Zr
4+
−
O than for Na
+
−O in NZSP implies a much stronger Zr
4+
−O
bond than Na
+
−O bond. Considering the ion−LoMMSs
coordination, solvation effect, and ion−O interactions in
NZSP, it can be concluded that Zr is much more difficult to
release from NZSP than Na, resulting in high Na/Zr selectivity
shown in Table 1. Consequently, this rational dual regulation
of coordination and solvation effects determines the metal
selectivity in NZSP, which constitutes the core of the proposed
CSDR mechanism.

CSDR mechanism could be further validated by changes in the
physical properties (e.g., density, conductivity, pH, solvato-

tension, and viscosity in Figure 3a−h) of LoMMSs and the
leachate. For example, the increased viscosity after leaching
reflects the disruption of the original hydrogen-bond network

The subsequent separation and recovery of metals from the
leachate are achieved through an antisolvent precipitation
strategy. Briefly, this technique involves adding an antisolvent
to the leachate to lower the solubility of the target metals,
thereby inducing the formation of insoluble solids for effective
separation.

system; the remaining 38 antisolvents produce no precipitation
at all. While TBT exhibits a higher Na precipitation efficiency
than FA (53.9% vs 46.6%), it demonstrates a lower efficiency
for Zr precipitation (49.0% vs 68.6%) (Figure 5b).
The reuse of the leachate shows a decline in leaching
efficiency over three cycles, with Na leaching efficiency
following the order of cycle 1 (90.0%) > cycle 2 (43.1%) >

attributed to the consumption of coordination sites in

5d, the highly consistent UV−vis characteristic peaks over

LoMMSs, indicating negligible compositional alteration and
degradation upon reuse. The IR spectra exhibit no obvious
changes in shape, which further verifies the negligible
compositional changes and degradation of the system (Figure
5e).
4. CONCLUSION
In summary, we propose a CSDR mechanism driven by
coordination and solvation effects to achieve the highly
selective and sustainable recovery of both the cathode and
solid electrolyte from spent ASIBs utilizing amino acid−based
LoMMSs. The maximum separation selectivity reaches 186.5
for Na/Mn (NMF cathode using LA:Trp) and 1990.7 for Na/
Zr (NZSP solid electrolyte using FA:Arg) from spent ASIBs.
The effect of the HBA on the Na leaching efficiency from the
NZSP solid electrolyte follows the order of LA:Arg (97.8%) >
LA:Pro (97.1%) > LA:His (95.1%) > LA:Val (94.9%) >
LA:Met (91.8%) > LA:Glu (91.1%) > LA:Trp (89.4%) >
LA:Cys (89.2%) > LA:Lys (87.0%) > LA:Leu (86.1%) >
LA:Gly (82.2%). Conversely, the corresponding Na/Zr
separation selectivity exhibits a distinct trend, decreasing in
the sequence of LA:Glu (45.6) > LA:Pro (15.4) > LA:Gly
(14.4) > LA:Leu (13.7) > LA:Trp (10.6) > LA:Lys (10.5) >
LA:Met (8.5) > LA:Val (8.3) > LA:Arg (7.5) > LA:His (6.1) >
LA:Cys (5.7). As a function of the HBD, the Na leaching
efficiency from the NZSP solid electrolyte decreases in the
order of FA:Arg (99.8%) > LA:Arg (97.8%) > PHA:Arg
(92.4%) > PA:Arg (73.1%) > Gly:Arg (17.0%) > EG:Arg
(15.3%); meanwhile, the corresponding Na/Zr selectivity
displays a modified ranking of FA:Arg (1990.7) > PA:Arg
(425.6) > EG:Arg (25.2) > Gly:Arg (22.6) > LA:Arg (6.3) >
PHA:Arg (1.1). A decreased mass ratio of the NZSP solid
electrolyte to the LA:Arg solvent accelerates Zr dissolution
from 15.5% to 29.7% while leaving Na leaching minimally
unchanged, which ultimately suppresses the Na/Zr selectivity
from 6.3 to 3.3. While TBT shows a superior Na precipitation
efficiency of 53.9% compared to 46.6% for FA; however, it
displays an inferior Zr precipitation efficiency of 49.0% relative
to that of 68.6% for FA. The implications for practical recycling
applications are 3-fold: (1) existing chemical mechanisms can
be combined to boost both selectivity and efficiency during
metal leaching; (2) a multitude of factors, including but not
restricted to the HBD, HBA, molar ratio, and solid-to-solvent
ratio, act collectively to govern the selectivity and efficiency of
ASIB component recovery, extending well beyond standard
cathode and solid electrolyte materials; and (3) the CSDR
mechanism is highly applicable to other clean energy recovery,
providing a green recycling strategy for solar, biomass, and
hydrogen wastes, as well as alternative energy storage platforms
like lithium-ion batteries and supercapacitors. The main
limitations of this work include the possible presence of
secondary chemical interactions alongside the core coordina-
tion and solvation effects, as well as a relatively low efficiency
in the subsequent reuse of the solvent system.
