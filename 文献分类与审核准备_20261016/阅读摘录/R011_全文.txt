Review
Biomass‑derived activated carbon: a review 
on process parameters, material properties, 
and machine learning approaches for supercapacitor
Pranay Singh1 and Himanshu Sekhar Panda1,*
1 Department of Metallurgical and Materials Engineering, Defence Institute of Advanced Technology, Girinagar, Pune 411025, India
ABSTRACT
Carbon-based electrode materials are widely recognized for their outstanding 
electrical conductivity, large specific surface area, and robust chemical stabil-
ity, thereby rendering them highly suitable for electric double-layer capacitors 
(EDLCs) that rely on electrostatic charge storage. Among these materials, bio-
mass-derived activated carbon (AC)—especially from agroforestry waste—has 
gained prominence as a potential candidate due to its cost-effectiveness, abundant 
availability, and simple synthesis processes. Numerous experimental investiga-
tions have explored the synthesis of AC from diverse biomass sources. Also, the 
research is now increasingly shifting toward predictive modeling to understand 
and optimize AC properties for supercapacitor applications. Recent efforts focus 
on forecasting key performance metrics such as yield, heteroatom content, and 
specific capacitance based on structural and compositional parameters, including 
pore architecture, chemical constituents, graphitization level, testing conditions, 
and electrolyte choice. This review presents a detailed analysis of AC fabrication 
techniques, the influence of processing variables on material performance, and 
the growing role of machine learning (ML) in predicting electrochemical behav-
ior. Particular emphasis is placed on linking synthesis conditions with structural 
characteristics and their impact on specific capacitance.
1 Introduction
The depletion of natural resources, driven by 
rapid urbanization, industrialization, and popula-
tion growth, has intensified the requirement for 
energy storage technologies that are both efficient 
and environmentally sustainable. Supercapaci-
tors (SC) offer efficient energy storage with rapid 
charge–discharge capability and long operational life 
[1–6]. Consequently, research efforts have focused 
on the development of advanced electrode materials 
designed to enhance the electrochemical performance 
of SC, which exhibit high specific capacitance  (Csp), 
superior power density  (DP), and exceptional long-
term stability, effectively bridging the performance 
disparity in energy storage characteristics between 
traditional capacitors and batteries. In terms of classi-
fication, SC fall into three main types: EDLCs, pseudo-
capacitors, and hybrid capacitors. EDLCs store charge 
electrostatically, much like traditional capacitors,

while pseudocapacitors rely on fast, reversible redox 
reactions to store charge. Hybrid capacitors combine 
the advantages of both systems [7].
Pseudocapacitors offer high  Csp and energy den-
sity  (DE), but they suffer from low cyclic stability and 
 DP [8]. Materials like transition metal oxides, nitrides, 
and sulfides are commonly used as electrode materi-
als in this system, with spinel-structured compounds 
such as cobaltite and layered double hydroxides 
demonstrating pronounced pseudocapacitive behav-
iour [9 –13]. In contrast, EDLCs are typically based 
on carbon-based materials, which provide excellent 
cyclic stability and high  DP. These materials exhibit 
excellent electrical conductivity and are more resistant 
to degradation compared to those used in pseudoca-
pacitors [14, 15]. Common carbon materials used in 
EDLCs include graphene, carbon aerogels, reduced 
graphene oxide (rGO), carbon nanotubes (CNTs), and 
AC. Among these, AC stands out for being low-cost, 
highly porous, environmentally friendly, and easy to 
synthesize, making it more scalable than other carbon 
materials. Analysis of Scopus data reveals a steady 
growth in the number of publications on “Biomass-
derived AC for Supercapacitors” over the past decade 
(Fig. 1). This trend highlights the increasing impor -
tance of biomass-derived carbon materials and under-
scores the expanding research focus on their synthesis, 
structural optimization, and application in high-per-
formance supercapacitors.
AC offers a wide variety of applications across 
numerous domains, including environmental reme-
diation (e.g., air and water purification), medical and 
pharmaceutical uses (such as the treatment of poi-
soning and drug overdoses, and filtration in medical 
devices), the food and beverage industry, and several 
industrial processes beyond energy storage systems 
[16–18]. Among these, water purification is identified 
as a prominent application due to AC’s high, large sur-
face area, high adsorption capacity, and the presence 
of surface functional groups, which enhance its ability 
to remove chemical pollutants from wastewater [19]. 
These same properties also make it effective for air 
purification, where gaseous pollutants are adsorbed 
onto the porous carbon surface.
The synthesis of AC is achieved through physical 
or chemical activation techniques, with processing 
conditions strongly influencing its surface area, pore 
structure, and functionality. These factors directly 
affect  Csp, as confirmed by various studies. In the pre-
sent research landscape, a substantial volume of data 
is available in technical literature, which can be effec-
tively utilized for ML-driven applications. Within the 
experimental framework of SC, ML plays a vital role 
in reducing the dependency on extensive experimental 
trials to attain optimal outcomes for specific proper-
ties. By employing prior domain knowledge, relevant 
input parameters correlated with the desired output 
can be systematically selected to enhance predictive 
accuracy. ML not only streamlines the research pro-
cess by minimizing time and effort but also facilitates 
the determination of optimal predicted values based 
on specific parameters, thereby improving efficiency 
and reliability in experimental studies.
Yogesh et al. implemented an ML-based predic-
tive model to estimate the  Csp of graphene oxide 
nano-ring-based electrodes. The study involved data 
extraction from 64 research articles, utilizing potential 
window, current density, electrolyte, and its respec-
tive concentration as input parameters, with  Csp as 
the target variable. Among the various ML models 
employed, the Support Vector Machine (SVM) exhib-
ited the highest predictive accuracy, achieving a root 
mean square error (RMSE) of 52.71, outperforming the 
Regression Tree (55.98), Neural Network (NN) (59.02), 
and Linear Regression (LR) (59.41) models. The  Csp 
predicted by the SVM model was 221.6 F/g, whereas 
the experimentally obtained value was 110 F/g [20].

Numerous review studies have investigated AC 
derived from a variety of biomass precursors, each 
highlighting different facets of its development. Some 
reviews focus on the selection of raw biomass materi-
als and how these precursors influence the resulting 
electrochemical properties, alongside the synthesis 
strategies employed. Others concentrate specifically 
on activation techniques or the effects of heteroatom 
doping in modifying the structure and performance of 
AC. For instance, Shanmuga Priya et al. [22] reviewed 
the physicochemical characterization and electrochem-
ical behavior of biomass-derived AC. Rashidi et al.[23] 
place special emphasis on the ideal properties of AC 
along with its characterization. Bedia et al. [24] dis-
cussed the production of AC through  FeCl3-mediated 
chemical activation. Das et al. [25] reviewed bamboo-
based AC and its suitability for energy storage devices. 
Similarly, Tomy et al.[26] highlighted advancements 
in generating AC from coconut biomass for improved 
energy storage performance. Distinct from previously 
published reviews, this work offers a thorough and 
cohesive examination of AC produced from agro-
forestry biomass. It systematically covers synthesis 
routes, the impact of crucial processing variables on 
structural and functional properties, the role of differ-
ent activating agents, and the resulting electrochemi-
cal behavior supported by detailed physicochemical 
characterization. Furthermore, it underscores the sig-
nificance of heteroatom incorporation in enhancing 
electrochemical performance.
A major contribution of this review lies in its in-
depth focus on ML-driven prediction frameworks—an 
emerging yet underexplored dimension in activated 
carbon research. With the growing shift toward data-
centric methodologies for forecasting key performance 
indicators of supercapacitors, particularly  Csp, as well 
as secondary performance-supporting attributes such 
as SSA and yield that are essential for commercial 
viability. This article critically evaluates the selection 
of input parameters used in ML models and their 
physical significance in predicting  Csp. Accordingly, 
the review is organized as follows. Section 2 provides 
a comprehensive overview of biomass resources and 
their key characteristics. Section 3 outlines the syn-
thesis strategies for AC, with particular emphasis on 
commonly employed activating agents. Section 4 sys-
tematically evaluates the effects of synthesis param-
eters, precursor selection, proximate analysis, and het-
eroatom doping on the  Csp of AC materials. Section 5 
focuses on the structural and morphological charac -
terization of AC. Finally, Sects. 6 and 7 present super-
vised ML frameworks for supercapacitors, detailing 
the prediction of key electrochemical properties using 
ML approaches, and conclude with future prospects, 
critical challenges, and concluding remarks.
2  Biomass
Biomass refers to organic matter derived from plant 
and animal sources that can be utilized as an energy 
resource. Due to its low price value, abundant avail-
ability, and environmental compatibility, biomass 
plays a significant role in energy storage applications, 
making it a promising alternative for sustainable 
energy production. Among the various categoriza-
tion schemes proposed, Roberts et  al. classified bio-
mass into two fundamental categories: plant-derived 
and animal-derived sources. Within the plant-derived 
group, biomass is further divided into natural vegeta-
tion, energy crops, and residual biomass. The residual 
fraction includes by-products generated from agricul-
tural practices, forestry operations, industrial activi-
ties, and urban solid waste (Fig. 2) [27]. Agroforestry 
activities generate substantial quantities of biomass 
residues, which are often left to decompose in the field, 
utilized as fuel for domestic combustion, or disposed

of in landfills. These conventional practices contribute 
significantly to environmental concerns, such as air 
and land degradation and greenhouse gas emissions 
[28]. However, such lignocellulosic wastes hold prom-
ise as sustainable carbon precursors for the prepara-
tion of AC, particularly for SC applications. Various 
agricultural and forestry-derived biomass residues 
commonly used for this purpose are outlined below.
2.1  Forest residue
Due to their extensive availability, low price, and high 
content of carbon and heteroatoms, forestry residues 
represent promising precursor materials for the fab-
rication of SC electrodes. Additionally, their inherent 
porous structures facilitate efficient ion transport, fur-
ther improving electrochemical performance. Typical 
tree-derived biomass sources encompass wood, leaves, 
bark, and sawdust. Soybean straw, derived from agri-
cultural biomass, is considered a viable precursor used 
for AC synthesis due to its prominent nitrogen and 
carbon content. AC was synthesized by impregnating 
carbonized soybean straw powder with KOH (1:2) 
and carbonizing it at 700 ºC, followed by HCl and DI 
water washing. The material exhibited an specific sur-
face area (SSA) of 2012.62  m2/g and a  Csp of 380.5 F/g 
at 0.5 A/g, with 73.97% capacitive retention at 10 A/g 
after 10,000 cycles [29]. Ayinla et al. synthesized AC 
using pine wood and utilized it to fabricate a sym-
metrical solid-state SC. The AC material was derived 
from pine wood, carbonized at 1000 ºC with a phos-
phoric acid (H₃ PO₄) impregnation ratio of 1.5. The 
resulting SC demonstrated a  DP of 1156.04 W/kg at a 
 DE of 14.75 Wh/kg, with  Cu2⁺-CNF hydrogel serving 
as the electrode material [ 30]. Paul et al. synthesized 
AC from birch sawdust using a two-step KOH acti-
vation process. The raw material was carbonized at 
500 °C under an Inert  N2 atmosphere for one hour, 
impregnated with KOH (biochar: KOH = 1:8 wt%), 
and pyrolyzed at 900 ºC in nitrogen. The product was 
washed until pH stabilization. The AC exhibited small 
and elongated particle morphology and a Type I iso-
therm, indicating microporosity. It had a high SSA 
(3029  m2/g) and an  ID/IG ratio of 1.4, suggesting sig -
nificant structural defects. Electrochemical analysis of 
birch-based electrodes in symmetric SC showed a  Csp 
of 143 F/g at 0.1 A/g [31]. Table 1 provides an overview 
of various AC materials synthesized from tree-derived 
biomass wastes, along with their respective  Csp values. 
An overview of typical forestry-derived byproducts is 
provided below.
2.1.1  Mulberrytree
Mulberry tree waste, with its high lignocellulosic con-
tent, is a suitable precursor for AC, yielding a porous 
structure ideal for energy storage applications. Mul-
berry branches possess substantial amounts of pro-
tein, cellulose, and lignin, with cellulose and lignin 
accounting for 54.3% and 28.9% of the composition, 
respectively [37]. Ma et al. employed a two-step 
pyrolysis strategy integrated with chemical activa -
tion to preserve the hierarchical porous architecture 
of mulberry wood-derived carbon. Among the acti-
vating agents tested (KOH, ZnCl₂ , and H₃PO₄), KOH 
proved most effective, yielding a material with a high 
SSA of 424  m2/g, a microporosity of 59.1%, and a  Csp 
of 98 F/g at 5 mA/cm2 [38].
Liu et al. synthesized AC electrode material from 
mulberry tree leaves via hydrothermal carboniza-
tion, followed by chemical activation using NaOH. 
Different char-to-NaOH ratios were investigated, 
with the optimal performance observed at a 1:3 ratio, 
resulting in a high SSA of 2662  m2/g and a total pore 
volume (TPV) of 1.79  cm3/g. Electrochemical evalu-
ation in a symmetric two-electrode system with the 
electrolytic concentration of 6 M KOH demonstrated 
a   Csp of 330 F/g at 0.1 A/g. The CV and GCD plots for 
all the samples fabricated with different biochar-to-
NaOH ratios are shown in Fig. 3(1a, 1b). The elec-
trode material also exhibited excellent rate capabil-
ity, retaining 76% of its capacitance at a high specific

2.1.3  Bamboo
The cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin content of 
bamboo are 47.7, 21.9, and 24.5%, respectively, and it 
exhibits a high carbon yield because of its significant 
lignin fraction. Its well-developed porous structure 
and high carbon content facilitate efficient ion diffu-
sion, highlighting its potential as a sustainable pre-
cursor for SC applications [43]. Qiu et al. studied the 
impact of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin derived 
from bamboo on the electrochemical properties of the 
resulting AC. A  CO2-catalyzed  K2CO3 activation tech-
nique was employed to produce hierarchical porous 
carbon. The results indicate that the presence of lignin 
promotes the development of numerous micropores 
alongside a large fraction of mesopores in the bamboo-
based hierarchical porous carbon, while cellulose and 
hemicellulose support micropore formation. Lignin 
enhances  Csp and rate performance due to its high sur-
face area and the micro- and mesopore-associated pore 
volumes, whereas cellulose provides excellent cycling 
stability by forming a stable carbon framework and 
excellent conductivity [44].
Zhang et al. produced AC from bamboo via a 
straightforward carbonization process, succeeded 
by chemical activation using KOH at various activa-
tion temperatures. The ratio of carbonized material to 
KOH was maintained at 1:3. Among the samples, the 
one activated at 900 ºC exhibited the highest  Csp of 
293 F/g at 0.5 A/g in a 3 M KOH solution. This supe-
rior capacitance is attributed to the synergistic effect of 
its mesoporous structure and large SSA (2221.1  m2/g) 
[45]. Gunasekaran et al. developed nitrogen-doped 
AC material from bamboo through a one-step pyroly-
sis and activation process using a urea-to-KOH ratio 
of 3:1, exhibiting a wrinkled nanosheet morphology 
with porous structure, as confirmed by FESEM analy-
sis. Electrochemical characterization in a 6 M KOH 
electrolyte using a tri-electrode set-up yielded a high 
 Csp of 475 F/g at 10 mV/s, highlighting the enhanced 
performance due to nitrogen doping [46].
2.2  Agricultural waste
Agricultural waste primarily consists of biomass 
residues derived from crop and tree cultivation, and 
agro-industrial processes. These residues include 
sugarcane bagasse, peanut shells, wax gourd peels, 
garlic peels, mango peels, corncobs, onion peels, and 
paddy straw, among others. It can be biocompatible 
or non-biodegradable, hazardous or non-hazardous. 
A range of agricultural waste materials employed for 
AC generation is summarized in Table 2. The follow-
ing agricultural residues are commonly employed as 
precursors to produce AC.
2.2.1  Sugarcanebagasse
Sugarcane bagasse, a lignocellulosic byproduct 
(composed of 20–25% lignin, 25–30% hemicellulose, 
and ~ 40–50% cellulose) from agro-industries, serves 
as a cost-effective precursor for AC. Its utilization

reduces waste-related pollution while yielding porous 
carbon materials [ 57]. Phakkhawan et al. demon -
strated the production of AC from sugarcane bagasse 
via a sequential carbonization and activation process. 
Various activating agents were used, including basic 
(KOH, NaOH) and acidic  (HNO3,   H2SO4) types. SEM 
images reveal that samples synthesized without acti-
vation display a smooth and dense surface, whereas 
those treated with activating agents show a higher 
density of macropores due to the etching effect of the 
different agents (Fig. 4). The use of alkaline activa-
tors resulted in significantly enhanced SSA, increased 
TPV, and superior surface wettability relative to acidic 
activators. Among the activating agents tested, KOH 
yielded the best performance, providing an SSA 
of 1058.53  m2/g and a  Csp of 138.61 F/g at 0.5 A/g in 
a three-electrode system [58]. Wei et al. developed 
AC from sugarcane tips through a dual-step process 
involving carbonization and chemical activation using 
KOH. Initially, the sugarcane tips were washed, dried, 
and ground into a fine powder, which was carbonized 
at 500 ºC for 2 h. The carbonized product was then 
mixed with KOH at different ratios and calcined at 
600 ºC for 2 h under vacuum conditions. After cooling 
to room temperature, the material was acid-treated 
with 2 M HCl and washed to neutral pH. The optimal 
activation was obtained with a 1:2 weight ratio of pre-
carbonized biomass to KOH, resulting in a  Csp of 259 
F/g at 0.5 A/g. This superior performance is attributed 
to the relatively high effective surface area, which pro-
vides abundant ion-exposed adsorption sites [59].
Further, Sarkar et al. synthesized AC from sugar-
cane bagasse by pre-treating it with various alkaline 
activating agents such as KOH,  K2CO3, and NaOH. 
The raw bagasse was thoroughly washed, dried, and 
chopped into small pieces before Thereafter, 30 g of 
dried bagasse was combined with 150 g of an aque-
ous solution containing 80 wt% of the corresponding 
activating agent and allowed to impregnate for 24 h. 
After pretreatment, the material was dried for 12 h at 
120 ºC in a hot air oven, followed by carbonization at 
650 ºC for 2 h in a muffle furnace. This was followed 
by thorough washing until a neutral pH was achieved. 
Among the activating agents tested,  K2CO3 demon-
strated the most favorable electrochemical perfor-
mance, yielding a  Csp of 265 F/g at 0.2 A/g [60].

3  Methods for the synthesis of activated 
carbon
AC is prepared from a carbonaceous precursor either 
through a two-stage procedure: carbonization fol-
lowed by activation, or just carbonization.
3.1  Carbonization
Carbonization refers to the thermal breakdown of bio-
mass under oxygen-restricted atmosphere to generate 
char. During this process, volatile components of the 
precursor are released, leading to the formation of a 
porous carbon structure. Carbonization can be carried 
out using various methods, with hydrothermal car-
bonization and pyrolysis being the predominant and 
widely used techniques.
3.1.1  Pyrolysis
Pyrolysis is an irreversible thermochemical mecha-
nism in which organic material undergoes thermal 
degradation at high temperatures in an inert atmos -
phere, producing carbon-rich solids, gases, and liquid 
byproducts. Zhang et al. studied the impact of varying 
pyrolysis temperatures on AC derived from reed resi-
due waste. The precursor material was pyrolyzed at 
different temperatures ranging from 500 to 800 ºC, fol-
lowed by chemical activation using KOH at a weight 
ratio of 1:5 (AC: KOH). Among the tested conditions, 
600 ºC was identified as the optimal pyrolysis temper-
ature, yielding the SSA of 2074.72  m2/g. Increasing the 
pyrolysis temperature beyond 600 ºC led to a reduc -
tion in micropore volume, likely due to structural 
collapse or merging of micropores. Electrochemical

3.1.2  Hydrothermalcarbonization
Hydrothermal carbonization is a thermochemical 
process in which biomass is treated in an aqueous 
environment at temperatures between 180 and 300 ºC 
to produce hydrochar[106]. A primary advantage 
of using this process is its potential to convert wet 
biomass into carbonaceous material without requir -
ing a prior drying step [107]. Researchers employed 
hydrothermal carbonization in combination with an 
activation step for the production of AC. Sandeep 
et al. synthesized AC from corncob through a two-
step process involving hydrothermal carbonization 
at 200 ºC for 7 h, followed by carbonization at 400 ºC 
for 3 h in a muffle furnace. The corncob powder, acti-
vating agent (KOH,  ZnCl2, or  H3PO4), and distilled 
water were combined in a specific ratio within a 
Teflon-lined autoclave, and the resulting hydrochar 
was subsequently carbonized. Among the activating 
agents,  H3PO4 produced the most effective AC, exhib-
iting a surface area of 68.54  m2/g, which is the highest 
among them and delivering superior electrochemi-
cal performance, with a  Csp of 41 F/g at 1 A/g in 1 M

KOH displayed lower surface areas of 54.12  m2/g and 
34.58  m2/g, and correspondingly lower capacitances of 
9.5 F/g and 2.5 F/g, respectively. The  H3PO4-activated 
sample demonstrated favorable energy storage char-
acteristics, achieving a  DE of 5.7 Wh/kg and a  DP of 
500.4 W/kg in a three-electrode configuration [108].
3.2  Activation process
Activation improves the surface area and pore volume 
of the material through the formation of additional 
pores and the expansion of pre-existing pores. In addi-
tion, activation alters AC’s surface chemical nature, 
giving it unique characteristics [109]. Activation may 
be carried out using a single-step approach, where 
carbonization and activation take place concurrently, 
or a two-step process, in which the carbonaceous 
intermediate, typically derived from pyrolysis or 
hydrothermal carbonization, is subsequently activated 
through chemical treatment. AC can be achieved using 
physical, chemical, or a combination of both methods.
3.2.1  Physicalactivation
Physical activation is a two-step process consisting of 
carbonization followed by activation. During carboni-
zation, the raw material is heated in an inert atmos-
phere (like argon or nitrogen) at temperatures ranging 
from 400 to 700  ºC. Subsequently, the resulting car-
bonized material is subjected to oxidizing gases, such 
as carbon dioxide or steam, at elevated temperatures 
of 800 ºC to 1000 ºC to create porosity and enhance 
its surface area. During the activation process, gasifi-
cation reactions between the oxidizing gases and the 
carbonized material promote the removal of carbon 
atoms as gaseous products such as CO, leading to the 
development of a well-defined porous structure in the 
AC according to the following reaction (Eq. 1-2) [110, 
111].
CO2 is generally preferred due to its high purity 
and ease of flow control [112]. AC produced using  CO2 
as the oxidizing agent exhibits significantly enhanced 
microporosity, along with higher BET surface area 
and micropore volume, compared to AC derived from 
steam activation, which often results in insufficient 
micropore development [113].

Chemical activation is done using a chemical agent for 
the treatment of carbonaceous material to produce AC. 
It can be a one-step process influenced by the degree of 
impregnation and the chemical agent-to-dry precursor 
ratio. Compared to physical activation, chemical acti-
vation presents several advantages, including higher 
carbon yield, shorter activation time, and reduced 
processing cost. Additionally, it produces AC with a 
more developed and well-defined porous structure 
[109, 111]. However, a drawback of this approach is 
the extensive and repetitive washing required for the 
AC, as well as its severe corrosiveness [109]. A detailed

4  Factors influencing the properties of AC
During synthesis, certain processing parameters have 
a significant impact on the physicochemical character-
istics of AC. These include the impregnation ratio, acti-
vation temperature, and activation period. A detailed 
examination of the influence of these parameters is 
presented below.
4.1  Activation temperature
Activation temperature strongly impacts the devel-
opment of AC, as it significantly affects key proper-
ties such as SSA, pore size distribution, and overall 
porosity. In many cases, increasing the carbonization 
temperature enhances the surface area, reduces vola-
tile content, refines the pore structure, and improves 
the mechanical strength of the material. However, 
when the temperature exceeds an optimal threshold, 
these benefits may decline due to pore collapse or 
structural degradation. The quality and effective-
ness of the AC may also be adversely affected by 
extremely high temperatures, which can result in a 
decrease in total carbon yield and an increase in ash 
content.
He et al. systematically examined the influence 
of carbonization temperature, ranging from 500 to 
700 ºC, on the textural and electrochemical properties 
of AC. An increase in activation temperature, while 
keeping other parameters constant, leads to a signifi-

4.3  Impregnation ratio (IR)
In chemical activation, the IR is described as the 
mass ratio of the carbonaceous precursor to the 
activating agent (e.g., KOH, NaOH,  K2CO3,  ZnCl2, 
or  H3PO4) and is a critical parameter. Chaisit et al. 
investigated the effect of increasing the IR of AC 
to KOH from 1:1 to 1:5 wt%. An increase in the IR 
resulted in an enhancement of the textural proper-
ties, evidenced by an increase in SSA from 1049 to 
1350  m2/g, an expansion in average pore diameter 
from 1.89 to 2.15 nm, and a growth in TPV from 0.49 
to 0.72  cm3/g. However, the  Csp values at IRs of 1:1, 
1:3, and 1:5 were recorded as 175, 153, and 152 F/g, 
respectively. The higher capacitance observed at a 
1:1 ratio is attributed to the pore size being more 
closely matched to the solvated ion size, facilitating 
efficient electrolyte diffusion into the micropores 
[145]. Huarote-Garcia et al. produced AC from Pur-
ple Corncob using varying KOH concentrations (5%, 
10%, 20%, 30%). The porous structure improved 
with increasing KOH concentration up to 20%, but 
collapsed at 30% due to burning during activa-
tion, as evident from the SEM images presented in 
Fig. 8(1a–1f). The nitrogen adsorption–desorption 
isotherms for different KOH IRs (Fig.  8(2)) reveal 
that the samples containing 5% and 10% KOH 
exhibit a steep rise in nitrogen uptake at relative 
pressures (P/P₀  < 0.1), suggesting the existence of 
micropores. The hysteresis observed at higher rela-
tive pressures (P/P₀  > 0.5) results from nitrogen con-
densation within mesopores. Based on the IUPAC 
classification, all the samples demonstrate Type IV 
isotherms. The SSA increased from 499.5  m2/g at 
5% to 965.1  m2/g at 20%, then dropped to 231  m2/g 
at 30%. The highest  Csp was observed at 10 wt%, 
linked to the highest micropore area and volume 
[146]. Generally, an increase in the activator-to-
precursor ratio leads to a higher SSA up to an opti-
mal point, beyond which the SSA may plateau or 
slightly decrease due to pore collapse or excessive.

4.4  Effect of doping
AC SC electrodes exhibit inherently low  DE due to 
their charge storage mechanism, which primarily 
relies on electrostatic interactions at the electrode/
electrolyte interface rather than within the bulk mate-
rial. As a result, charge accumulation is constrained, 
as only the accessible surface area actively participates 
in capacitance generation. To overcome this limitation, 
heteroatom doping has been employed to modify 
the carbon matrix. The introduction of heteroatoms 
enhances pseudocapacitive behavior by facilitating 
Faradaic redox reactions, thereby supplementing 
the conventional charge storage of electric double-
layer capacitors. Furthermore, heteroatom incorpora-
tion increases the packing density of AC, leading to 
improved hydrophilicity and enhanced ion diffusion 
kinetics, ultimately increasing volumetric capacitance. 
In addition, heteroatom doping induces lattice distor-
tions and alters the electronic structure of AC. The 
disparity in electronegativity between the dopant and 
carbon atoms induces charge redistribution, resulting

J Mater Sci: Mater Electron (2026) 37:143 Page 19 of 49 143
in increased surface polarity. These modifications col-
lectively enhance ion adsorption and charge storage 
capabilities, thereby significantly improving the over-
all electrochemical performance of EDLCs [152].

4.4.3  Co‑doped
Co-doping in AC refers to the concurrent introduc -
tion of multiple heteroatoms into the carbon frame -
work to improve its structural, surface, and electro-
chemical characteristics synergistically. In a study by 
Wang et al., litchi peel was used as a precursor for AC 
production. It was then combined with varying mass 
ratios of dicyandiamide (0.5, 1, and 2) for nitrogen 
doping, while oxygen was self-doped to produce N/O 
co-doped AC. SEM images of all samples are shown 
in Fig. 9(3a–3d), revealing a well-developed porous 
structure, highlighting the pore-forming effectiveness 
of  ZnCl2. Notably, the undoped sample exhibits the 
most pronounced pore development compared to the 
doped counterparts. Figure 9(3e–3g) presents the ele-
mental mapping of the co-doped sample synthesized 
with an LC/ZnCl2/dicyandiamide ratio of 1:4:1, which 
confirms the presence of nitrogen along with carbon 
and oxygen. The sample with a 1:1 mass ratio of dicy-
andiamide exhibited the highest  Csp of 320 F/g in a 
6 M KOH electrolyte, compared to 246.2 F/g for the 
undoped sample. These results suggest that dicyandi-
amide, as a nitrogen dopant, significantly enhances the 
capacitor’s performance. However, increasing the dop-
ing concentration led to a reduction in SSA, micropore 
volume, and total volume, likely due to the infiltration 
of the dopant into the micro- and mesopores, causing 
subsequent pore blockage [52].
Zheng et al. converted corn bracts into AC and 
enhanced their properties through doping with nitro-
gen, phosphorus, and a combination of both elements. 
Among the samples, the co-doped variant exhibited 
the highest  Csp, which correlated with its superior 
SSA of 286.5  m2/g and a TPV of 0.14  cm3/g [51]. In a 
separate study by Birundha et al., doping with nitro-
gen and sulfur increased the surface area but reduced 
 Csp, which was attributed to disrupting graphitic layer 
stacking and changes in surface functionality [159].
Several comparative studies have examined the 
electrochemical performance of materials with indi-
vidual dopants, such as sulfur or nitrogen, and com-
pared them with co-doped nitrogen–sulfur systems, 
specifically for supercapacitor applications [160–163]. 
Saravanan et al. investigated nitrogen- and sulfur-
doped AC derived from Gossypium hirsutum L. 
stalks, using urea as the nitrogen source and thiourea 
as the sulfur source. The SSA of undoped, N-doped, 
and S-doped AC were reported as 60.2, 365, and 382 
 m2/g, respectively. Electrochemical performance meas-
ured in 1 M  Na2SO4 at 1 A/g showed  Csp of 256, 326, 
and 424 F/g [161]. Notably, thiourea contains both 
nitrogen and sulfur, suggesting partial co-doping 
effects. Similarly, Nath et al. compared N-doped, 
S-doped, and N,S co-doped AC synthesized from 
seaweed biomass via pyrolysis using urea, elemental 
sulfur, and thiourea as dopants. The undoped sam-
ple exhibited an SSA of 362.5  m2/g, while N-doped 
and S-doped samples showed 498.7 and 435.4  m2/g, 
respectively; the co-doped sample displayed the high-
est SSA of 612.3  m2/g. Electrochemical testing in 3 M 
KOH revealed that the N,S co-doped sample showed 
the longest discharge time in GCD, followed by the 
N-doped, S-doped, and the undoped sample. The co-
doped carbon also delivered the highest  Csp of 890 F/g 
at 1 A/g, attributed to the synergistic effect of N–S dual 
doping [162]. Nazir et al. also examined the influence 
of N-, S-, and co-doping using banana-peel-derived 
biochar, with melamine, sodium thiosulfate, and their 
combination as dopants. The co-doped AC showed the 
highest SSA (2452  m2/g) and the highest  Csp of 175 
F/g at 1 A/g in a three-electrode system, compared to 
sulfur- and nitrogen-doped samples (123 and 115 F/g, 
respectively) [163]. Overall, while the relative perfor-
mance of single N- or S-doped carbons varies, multiple 
studies consistently demonstrate that N,S co-doping 
achieves superior  Csp due to synergistic heteroatom 
interactions. A comparative summary of the electro -

of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. The cellulose

tributes to the development of a mesoporous struc-
ture. Rodriguez Correa et al. investigated the effects 
of pore formation and SSA on biomass derived from 
alpha-cellulose, xylan from beech wood, and alkali 
kraft lignin. Their findings revealed that AC derived 
from cellulose and lignin achieved the highest SSA

4.6  Proximate analysis
The proximate analysis of AC reveals key components 
such as ash content, moisture content, volatile matter 
and fixed carbon percentage, all of which can influ-
ence the electrochemical performance of a SC. High 
moisture content decreases the material’s conductivity 
and may lead to pore blockage. A high level of vola -
tile matter suggests incomplete carbonization, which 
can impede the formation of micropores. Ash content 
introduces non-conductive impurities, while a higher 
fixed carbon percentage typically indicates better con-
ductivity and a higher surface area. The subsequent

5.5  Brunauer–Emmett–Teller analysis
BET analysis is a widely used technique for determin-
ing the SSA of porous materials through gas adsorp-
tion measurements, offering critical insights into their 
textural characteristics, including pore size distribu-
tion, pore types, pore volume, and overall pore struc-
ture. The surface area is known to have a direct impact 
on the performance of supercapacitors. An increase 
in SSA enhances the availability of active sites for ion 
adsorption, thereby improving electric double-layer 
capacitance. Additionally, a larger mesoporous vol-
ume facilitates efficient electrolyte ion penetration. 
These mesopores act as transport pathways, ena-
bling ions to more easily reach the micropores where 
double-layer charge storage predominantly occurs, 
resulting in improved overall electrochemical perfor-
mance [201]. To further interpret BET results, adsorp-
tion–desorption isotherms play a critical role in iden-
tifying pore characteristics. Type I isotherm follows 
the Langmuir model and reflects monolayer adsorp-
tion in microporous materials with pore sizes < 2 nm. 
Type II and Type III isotherms are observed in non-
porous or macroporous materials. Type II isotherms 
represent multilayer adsorption on the adsorbent 
surface, with the knee at point B indicating complete 
monolayer coverage. In Type III isotherms, there is no 
point B, reflecting the absence of monolayer formation. 
The adsorbent–adsorbate interactions are relatively 
weak, and the adsorbate molecules tend to cluster on 
the most favorable sites. Type IV isotherm indicates 
mesoporosity, where multilayer adsorption followed 
by capillary condensation occurs within mesopores. 
Type V isotherms, commonly observed in mesoporous 
adsorbents, are indicative of weak adsorbent–adsorb-
ate interactions. The initial region of these isotherms 
closely resembles that of Type III, while the subse-
quent pore-filling process governs their overall profile. 
Finally, the Type VI isotherm shows stepwise multi-
layer adsorption on highly uniform and nonporous 
surfaces [202] [203]. The classification of physisorption 
isotherms is shown in Fig. 12.
In addition to isotherm types, adsorption hysteresis 
loops provide essential information on pore geome-
try. According to the IUPAC classification, adsorption 
hysteresis is generally categorized into H1, H2, H3, 
and H4 types, which serve as diagnostic indicators of 
pore structure and adsorption behavior. H1 hysteresis 
is typically associated with a narrow distribution of 
uniform mesopores. In comparison, H2(a) hysteresis 
loops are generally attributed to pore-blocking or per-
colation effects occurring within a narrow distribution 
of pore necks, or to cavitation-induced evaporation. 
At the same time, H2(b) loops are also linked to pore 
blocking but involve a much broader distribution 
of pore neck sizes. H3 hysteresis loops, on the other 
hand, are characterized by an adsorption branch simi-
lar to that of a Type II isotherm, with the desorption 
branch generally terminating at the relative pressure 
corresponding to cavitation. Finally, H4 hysteresis 
displays combined features of Type I and Type II 
isotherms, reflecting the coexistence of microporous 
regions with mesoporous frameworks [202].
Several studies have demonstrated how isotherm 
behavior reflects the underlying pore structures of AC. 
Wang et al. prepared AC from bamboo waste by vary-
ing the activation temperature during the synthesis 
process. N₂ adsorption and desorption measurements 
were performed to determine the pore configuration 
and volume characteristics of the material. All samples 
exhibited Type I isotherms, as no significant hysteresis 
loop was observed, indicated by the complete overlap 
of the adsorption and desorption branches (Fig. 13a). 
In the extremely low relative pressure region, the 
curve remains approximately linear, suggesting a 
high content of micropores within the material [204]. 
Liu et al. used garlic shoots as a carbon precursor for 
AC synthesis. The precursor was pre-carbonized at 
400 ºC for 2 h, after which KOH was mixed with the 
pre-carbonized material at mass ratios of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 
5. As shown in Fig. 13b, the  N2 adsorption–desorption

of mesopores. Additionally, the pronounced adsorp-
tion capacity in the low-pressure range (P/P₀ = 0.0–0.1) 
indicates the presence of microporosity. The sample

In order to prepare labeled datasets for supervised ML

Guided by domain-specific knowledge, this process 
plays a crucial role in improving data quality as well 
as ensuring the robustness of predictive models. Typi-

6.1.1  Datasetcollection
A clearly defined problem statement is essential for 
constructing a relevant dataset in ML. It guides the 
selection of meaningful input features directly linked 
to the target variable. For instance, predicting SC 
capacitance requires the inclusion of measurement 
conditions and material properties, while fiber diam-
eter prediction demands process parameters such as 
applied voltage, feed rate, and collector speed. Feature 
selection must be based on domain knowledge, and 
data should be collected from reliable peer-reviewed 
sources with strict unit consistency. When data is 
presented graphically, digitization tools should be 
used for accurate extraction, and efforts should be 
made to maintain low missing-value ratios to improve 
model reliability. For small and imbalanced datasets, 
resampling can enhance model performance by effec-
tively expanding the available data. This involves 
generating additional instances or subsets from the 
original dataset. In a study conducted by our research 
group, a resampling-based filtering method was 
applied within the ML workflow to predict the  Csp of 
nickel cobalt phosphate. At a 300% resampling level, 
the gap between training and testing accuracies was 
minimized, indicating improved model generaliza-
tion. However, increasing the resampling ratio to 400% 
resulted in a wider accuracy gap, suggesting poten-
tial overfitting [208].These observations demonstrate 
that resampling, when optimized, can improve model 
accuracy and strengthen predictive reliability.

fill, backward fill, and k-nearest neighbors. It is essen-

6.1.4  Datasetreduction
Dataset reduction is performed to eliminate redundant 
or irrelevant information while preserving the essen-
tial structure and patterns in the data. It offers sev-
eral advantages, including improved computational 
efficiency, faster processing, reduced storage require-
ments, enhanced data analysis, and better model per-

on the other hand, involves selecting a representative

on specific criteria, depending on the practical applica-

6.1.5  Dataintegrationandstorage
Data integration is the process of combining datasets 
from different sources into one unified dataset. The 
first step is data harmonization, which involves nor-
malizing and aligning data to make it consistent across 
all sources. After these steps, the integrated dataset 
is stored and prepared for use in model training and 
prediction.
6.1.6  Modelselectionandtraining
The process of selecting an appropriate ML model 
depends on the problem’s characteristics, such as 
whether it calls for regression or classification, as well 
as the complexity of the dataset. The dataset is gener-
ally split into training and testing subsets, with com-
mon split ratios of 80:20 or 70:30 to ensure a balanced 
evaluation. The training dataset serves as input to the 
chosen model, enabling it to discover hidden patterns 
in the data. Following training, model performance is 
assessed using the test dataset to estimate its accuracy 
and generalization capability. Additionally, hyperpa-
rameter tuning is conducted to optimize model perfor-
mance by adjusting parameters that are not inherently 
learned from the data during training.

6.3.2 Decisiontree
A decision tree is another supervised ML model, con-

6.3.3 Randomforest
RF is a bagging-based ensemble learning method, 
designed for both classification and regression tasks. 
It constructs multiple decision trees by generating 
bootstrap samples from the original dataset, where 
sampling is performed with replacement. A distinct 
subset drawn from the entire dataset is utilized to 
train each individual tree, incorporating randomness 
in both sample selection and feature selection at each 
node. In regression, the output is derived by averaging 
the predictions over all trees, while in classification, 
the final outcome is decided by majority voting among 
the different trees [216, 217]. This approach enhances 
model robustness, reduces overfitting, and improves 
generalization performance. Figure 17 presents a sche-
matic diagram of the RF-based model.
6.3.4 XGBoost
XGBoost employs gradient boosting decision trees 
to perform classification and regression. It integrates 
L1 and L2 regularization to control overfitting and 
enhance model generalization [210, 218]. Engineered 
for efficiency, XGBoost effectively processes large 
datasets. Moreover, it has a built-in capability to man-
age missing values [219]. XGBoost employs gradient-
based optimization to iteratively refine predictions, 
applying first-order (gradient) and second-order (Hes-
sian) derivatives of the loss function to adjust model 
parameters [220]. Figure 18 shows a schematic repre-
sentation of the XGBoost concept.
6.3.5 Ada‑boost
AdaBoost is a boosting ensemble ML algorithm that 
combines multiple weak base learners for regression 
or classification tasks. In AdaBoost, training is per-
formed iteratively by focusing on the errors of previ-
ously trained models. Initially, weights are assigned 
to all data points. If a model predicts incorrectly, the 
weight of the misclassified samples is increased. A

7  Prediction of key properties 
of biomass‑derived carbon
AC exhibits several critical properties, including yield, 
chemical composition, and SSA, which are fundamen-
tal to its functional performance. In SC applications, 
particular emphasis is placed on  Csp, a key electro-
chemical parameter extensively studied and predicted 
across the literature. The yield, chemical component, 
and SSA of AC or biochar are strongly influenced by 
structural parameters such as pore architecture, ele-
mental composition, and the degree of graphitization, 
along with process parameters. For AC derived from 
agro-forest biomass, the relative proportions of lignin, 
hemicellulose, and cellulose have been identified as 
important determinants of these characteristics, as 
discussed in detail in the raw material section. In 
addition, the evaluation of  Csp typically involves 
both intrinsic material properties and experimental 
parameters, including current density, scan rate, and 
electrolyte composition. These properties are used 
by researchers as input features in ML frameworks 
for predicting various properties of AC. An overview 
of key parameters frequently used in predicting AC 
properties for SC performance using agro-plant-based 
biomass is provided in Fig. 20.

their structural and compositional parameters. For

models were additionally employed for the same 
external dataset. The ANN model outperformed the 
comparative models, demonstrating the lowest pre-
diction error and highlighting its superior capability

7.3.2  Energyandpowerdensity
DE quantifies the stored energy relative to the 
device’s mass or volume, while  DP defines the rate at 
which energy can be delivered per unit mass or vol-
ume. Both metrics are critical performance indicators 
for supercapacitors and are directly correlated with 
their  Csp. The development of ML models for pre-
dicting these properties is still in its early stage, and 
significantly more work is required compared to pre-
diction studies on the  Csp of AC. Oladipo developed 
a three-layer ANN architecture using five input vari-
ables—sulfur doping (at%), nitrogen doping (at%), 
electrolyte concentration, electrolyte type, and S/N 
co-doping (at%)—to predict the  DE of nitrogen–sul-
fur co-doped chicken-bone-derived biocarbon. The 
predicted results of the model closely aligned with 
the experimental values, exhibiting a desirability of 
0.96 and an R2 of 0.964. Furthermore, an RF algo-
rithm and Garson equation analysis were employed 
to evaluate the contribution of each input variable. 
Electrolyte concentration and electrolyte type had 
the most significant influence in the ANN prediction 
model[235]. Zhou et al. predicted the  DP of AC using 
mesopore surface area, micropore surface area, and 
scan rate as input features. Four ML models were 
employed—linear regression, SVR, RF, and ANN. 
Among them, the ANN model outperformed the oth-
ers, exhibiting the highest R2 and the lowest RMSE 
values [236]. Lu et al. employed ML approaches to 
systematically predict the  DE and  DE of superca-
pacitors. The dataset used in this study comprised 
multiple input parameters, including the elemental 
composition of biomass precursors, industrial (prox-
imate) analysis, activation conditions, and applied 
current density, while  DE and  DE served as the target 
outputs. Several predictive models, namely Light-
GBM, XGBoost, and deep neural networks, were 
developed and evaluated. Among these, the Light-
GBM model demonstrated superior performance in 
estimating  DE, achieving a training R2 value of 0.922 
and a test R2 value of 0.916. In contrast, the XGBoost 
model exhibited the highest predictive accuracy for 
 DP, with a training R2 of 0.984 and a test R2 of 0.921. 
Furthermore, SHAP-based interpretability analysis 
revealed that residence time was the most influen-
tial parameter governing  DE, whereas volatile matter 
content exerted the strongest influence on  DP [237].
8  Prospects and challenges
Despite substantial progress in the synthesis of AC 
and an improved understanding of its effects on  Csp, 
graphitization, and SSA, several critical challenges 
persist. Enhancing the energy density of AC-based 
supercapacitors remains difficult, and issues related 
to the selection of raw materials further limit per-
formance consistency and large-scale application. 
In parallel, although ML has emerged as a promis -
ing tool for predicting key electrochemical proper-
ties, its integration is still limited by data scarcity and 
incomplete feature–property correlations. As shown 
in Fig. 25, key limitations and future perspectives for 
AC-based supercapacitors remain. The detailed chal-
lenges described below highlight the need for contin-
ued systematic experimental studies to improve  DE, 
ensure reliable raw-material utilization, and advance 
the scalability of AC materials, alongside the develop-
ment of more robust ML models supported by com-
prehensive and high-quality datasets.
1. The electrochemical performance of AC is influ-
enced not only by synthesis parameters but also criti-
cally by the choice of precursor material. A key advan-
tage of AC production lies in the broad availability 
of potential carbon sources, highlighting the need for 
careful precursor selection. An ideal precursor should 
have high carbon content, low levels of inorganic ash-
forming components, high activation potential, mini-
mal degradation during storage, appropriate density 
with sufficient volatile matter, a stable regional sup-
ply, and cost-effectiveness [238]. Certain biomass pre-
cursors are rich in heteroatoms such as nitrogen and 
oxygen, which can be retained during carbonization. 
These heteroatoms induce pseudocapacitive behavior 
in the resulting EDLC behavior of AC, thereby enhanc-
ing Csp. Conversely, some biomass contains inorganic 
salts that require acid pre-treatment to remove, ensur-
ing that the precursor composition positively contrib-
utes to electrode performance without compromis-
ing the intrinsic properties of the AC. Therefore, the 
precursor must be selected carefully, as its intrinsic 
properties directly influence the quality and perfor-
mance of the resulting AC. The resulting AC materials 
can be effectively characterized using techniques such 
as XRD, SEM, TEM, and BET surface area analysis to 
understand their intrinsic characteristics when derived 
from various biomass sources.
2. Chemical activation typically yields a higher 
amount of AC than physical activation, largely due 

===== PDF PAGE 43 =====
J Mater Sci: Mater Electron (2026) 37:143 Page 43 of 49 143
to enhanced oxidation of the carbonaceous precursor 
during the carbonization and activation stages of the 
physical activation route. In addition, chemical acti-
vating agents inhibit the formation of tar and volatile 
matter, thereby improving the yield of AC [239]. Yield 
is a key parameter in assessing the commercial feasi-
bility of AC production. In chemical activation, appro-
priate optimization of process conditions can signifi-
cantly increase productivity, whereas suboptimal 
conditions may compromise both material yield and 
chemical properties. In batch-scale AC production, 
variations in porosity, specific SSA, and yield remain 
a major limitation for consistent characterization. Such 
inconsistencies directly influence the electrochemical 
performance, particularly the capacitance of superca-
pacitors, underscoring the need for uniformity across 
production batches to enable reliable performance 
scaling.
3. Advance the development of innovative, 
resource-efficient synthesis strategies for activated 
carbon that significantly reduce chemical waste, max-
imize product yield, achieve high surface area with 
finely optimized pore architecture, and minimize 
the need for complex machinery and labor-intensive 
washing steps.
4. A major drawback of carbon-based electrodes 
is their inherently low Cₛₚ, which in turn limits the 
achievable energy density of the device. To overcome 
these performance constraints, several enhancement 
strategies have been investigated. These include 
heteroatom doping to modify surface chemistry, the 
development of composites incorporating transition 
metal oxides to introduce pseudocapacitive behav-
ior, and the integration of conducting polymers to 
improve charge storage capability. New classes of 
materials can also be integrated with AC to enhance 
its electrochemical performance. The incorporation 
of heteroatoms such as O, N, and S improves wetta -
bility, increases conductivity, and enhances charge-
transport sites, thereby improving the  Csp of super-
capacitors. Similarly, compositing AC with transition 
metal oxides can further increase capacitance through 
synergistic effects.
5. Systematic comparative studies examining dif-
ferent sources of the same dopant for AC doping in 
supercapacitor applications are currently scarce. 
Consequently, the influence of these variations on 
key material properties such as morphology, degree 
of graphitization, surface characteristics, and electro-
chemical performance remains poorly understood. 
Addressing this knowledge gap through detailed 
investigations is crucial to establishing clear cor -
relations between dopant source and AC behavior, 
thereby guiding the rational design of high-perfor-
mance supercapacitor electrodes.
6. A limited dataset restricts the model’s generali-
zation capability, thereby reducing its effectiveness 
in accurately learning the underlying relationships 
among input variables. To address this limitation, 
efforts should focus on expanding the dataset while

ensuring high data quality. Additionally, approaches 
for generating synthetic data—such as data aug-
mentation and transfer learning can be employed 
to effectively enrich the dataset and enhance model 
robustness.
7. Dataset collection in a supervised ML framework 
is a critical and time-intensive task. Different prepa-
ration methods impart distinct properties to AC, and 
variations in these processes significantly influence its 
physical and chemical characteristics. Physical activa-
tion yields materials with specific features, whereas 
chemical activation produces different characteris-
tics. In chemical activation, whether performed as a 
one-step or two-step process, the initial carbonization 
step, carried out through pyrolysis or hydrothermal 
treatment, is particularly crucial. While collecting such 
data, these variations must be carefully addressed, as 
they make dataset compilation challenging. Insuffi-
cient or non-representative data can ultimately limit 
the ability of ML models to accurately predict AC 
properties.
8. Existing studies predominantly focus on ML-
based prediction of the  Csp of AC, while other criti-
cal performance metrics, such as  DE,   DP, and cycling 
stability, receive limited attention. Furthermore, the 
scarcity of experimental validation using realistic and 
representative datasets constrains the practical appli-
cability and broader adoption of these ML models.
9  Conclusion
Agroforestry residues are promising sustainable pre-
cursors for AC in supercapacitors. Their electrochem-
ical performance depends on the interplay between 
precursor chemistry and processing conditions, which 
govern pore structure and surface properties. Based on 
an extensive review of the literature, the key findings 
highlighted in this review are as follows:
1. When selecting a precursor material, a high heter-
oatom content is desirable, as these heteroatoms 
can function as intrinsic dopants, facilitate the 
formation of redox-active sites, induce pseudoca-
pacitance, and enhance surface wettability, thereby 
significantly improving the  Csp.
2. AC properties are strongly influenced by synthesis 
conditions. Higher activation temperatures boost 
surface area but can lower heteroatom content and 
yield, while harsh carbonization may damage the 
structure. Impregnation ratio and carbonization 
time also affect performance, requiring careful 
optimization for balanced properties.
3. In the context of doping, activated carbon co-
doped with multiple heteroatoms generally dem-
onstrates higher  Csp compared to materials doped 
with a single heteroatom, as the synergistic effects 
of multiple dopants enhance charge storage and 
electrochemical performance in most cases.
ML techniques like RF, ANN, and XGBoost can accel-
erate the design of AC by predicting key properties from 
synthesis parameters. Limitations such as small datasets 
and inconsistent protocols highlight the need for sys-
tematic data and experimental validation. Integrating 
ML with conventional synthesis enables tailored AC 
properties, while future work should focus on stand -
ardized methods, high-quality data, and combined 
computational–experimental strategies for scalable, 
high-performance supercapacitors.
