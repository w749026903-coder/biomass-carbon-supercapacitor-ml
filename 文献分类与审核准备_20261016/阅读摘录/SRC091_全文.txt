REVIEW
Synergistic Design of Biomass Materials for Moisture‐ 
Induced Electricity Generation
Wangxin Liu | Yanhua Guan | Guanghui Song | Chuanling Si | Lin Dai
State Key Laboratory of Bio‐based Fiber Materials, College of Light Industry and Engineering, Tianjin University of Science and Technology, Tianjin, China
Correspondence: Lin Dai ( dailin@tust.edu.cn )
Received: 1 July 2026 | Revised: 29 August 2026 | Accepted: 9 September 2026
Keywords: biomass | clean energy | hydrovoltaic effect | ion transport | moisture‐induced electricity generation
ABSTRACT
Harvesting the dispersed, low‐grade energy stored in atmospheric moisture has emerged as an attractive route toward 
distributed and sustainable power generation. Moisture‐induced electricity generation (MEG) converts chemical potential 
changes associated with water sorption, ion dissociation, selective ion transport, and interfacial charge transfer into electricity 
without requiring light, wind, or a liquid water supply. Natural biomass is a promising material platform because its polar and 
ionizable groups and hierarchical pore channels support moisture uptake, carrier generation, and directional ion transport. 
However, a single biomass material is unlikely to simultaneously provide high ionic conductivity, a persistent gradient, efficient 
charge collection, and wet‐state stability. This minireview presents a synergistic design framework across molecular, structural, 
and device levels. Biomass chemistry regulates carrier generation and ion selectivity, hierarchical structures maintain water 
activity and ion concentration gradients, and device integration coordinates ionic transport, charge collection, and structural 
stability. We first outline the fundamental mechanism of biomass‐based MEG and then compare cellulose, sodium alginate, 
lignin, proteins, chitosan, and microbial materials in terms of their properties, functions, limitations, and suitable application 
scenarios. Performance optimization strategies are further summarized as ionic regulation, gradient construction, and composite 
integration. Finally, we discuss the main challenges of conversion efficiency, operating lifetime, wet‐state durability, and 
biomass variability, together with future directions for practical and sustainable moisture energy harvesting.

Introduction
Rising global energy demand and the pursuit of carbon 
neutrality have intensified the search for clean and sustainable 
energy solutions. In this context, technologies capable of har -
vesting dispersed, low‐grade energy from ambient environments 
are emerging as a promising complementary pathway [ 1, 2 ]. The 
atmosphere, nearly ubiquitous yet long underexploited, repre -
sents one such reservoir, containing approximately 12,900 km
3 
of water vapor at any given time, with about 495,000 km
3 
of 
water circulating through the atmosphere each year via the hy -
drological cycle [ 3 ]. The adsorption and desorption of water 
molecules at material surfaces are accompanied by pronounced 
changes in chemical potential; if these can be directed into an 
electrical signal, energy can be harvested continuously in the 
absence of light, wind, or a liquid‐water supply. Building on this 
idea, moisture‐induced electricity generation (MEG) has 
advanced rapidly in recent years [ 4, 5 ]. For example, protein 
nanowires derived from Geobacter sustain self‐maintained po -
wer output under ambient humidity [ 6 ], adsorption–evaporation 
synergistic systems extend the continuous output of a single 
cycle to several hundred hours [ 7 ], and emerging platforms such 
as supramolecular hydrogels push the current to the milliampere 
level [ 8, 9 ], moving the technology from a concept toward 
practical use [ 5, 10 ].

With the rapid development of this field, the research focus has 
correspondingly shifted from the early synthetic polymers and 
carbon nanomaterials toward natural biomass [ 8, 11, 12 ]. Bio -
molecules are intrinsically rich in polar and ionizable groups, 
such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, amino, and sulfonic acid moieties, and 
possess native fibrous networks and hierarchical pore channels, 
combining moisture uptake, ion generation, and ion transport 
within a single material (Figure 1 ). They are also abundant, low‐ 
cost, biodegradable, and renewable, aligning well with green 
electronics and sustainable energy [ 13 ]. This rationale is corrob -
orated by analyses of water‐biopolymer interactions, which 
identify hydrophilic groups, interfaces, and hierarchical struc -
tures as key to water sorption and transport [ 14 ], and by reviews of 
bio‐hydrovoltaic systems (both living and non‐living) [ 11 ] and 
cellulose‐enabled generators driven by moisture, evaporation, 
osmosis, or droplets [ 15 ]. In contrast, this minireview focuses on 
ambient‐moisture electricity generation across diverse biomass 
families. We adopt a comparative lens that directly links each 
material's chemical functionality and hierarchical architecture to 
its performance in moisture sorption, ion transport, and gradient 
retention. Given that a single biomass rarely optimizes all these 
attributes simultaneously, we argue that progress lies in the 
rational combination of complementary components rather than 
in the pursuit of a universal candidate.
Here, we aim to construct a comprehensive framework that 
encompasses the full pathway from biomass feedstocks, through 
the mechanisms of water‐to‐electricity conversion, to material 
synthesis and performance optimization strategies (Figure 1 ). 
Biomass functional groups and native structures dictate mois -
ture uptake, ion generation, and transport; multiscale in -
terventions (molecular ionic regulation, structural gradient 
construction, and device‐level composite integration) offset the 
limitations of any single material and maintain electrical output. 
Synergy is realized by aligning biomass properties with the 
mechanism and integrating cross‐scale complementary func -
tions. This logic connects the mechanism discussed in Section 2
with biomass comparison and selection in Section 3 , perfor -
mance optimization in Section 4 , and the biomass‐specific 
challenges and opportunities in Section 5 .

Fundamental Mechanism of Moisture‐ 
Induced Electricity Generation
The broader hydrovoltaic effect refers to the generation of 
electricity from the direct interaction between water and 
nanostructured materials [ 16 ]. As one branch of this effect, 
MEG converts a water‐chemical‐potential difference into elec -
tricity through four coupled processes: water sorption, ion 
dissociation, ion transport, and interfacial charge transfer [ 6, 10 ] 
(Figure 2 ). Hydration increases carrier density and mobility, but 
useful output also requires the driving gradient to be main -
tained. The following subsections introduce only the relations 
needed to connect biomass chemistry and structure with voltage 
and current.

2.4
|
Interfacial Charge Transfer and Closure of 
the External Circuit
Ionic transport in the biomass must be converted into electronic 
current at the electrodes to close the external circuit. Capacitive 
charging generally gives a decaying transient, whereas revers -
ible faradaic reactions can sustain current but may introduce 
parasitic chemistry. Electrode composition and geometry should 
therefore be reported together with the active biomass, and 
capacitive, faradaic, and leakage contributions should be 
distinguished when interpreting continuous output [ 10, 29 ].

Representative Biomass Materials for 
Moisture‐Induced Electricity Generation
Representative biomass materials used in moisture‐induced 
electricity generation can be broadly grouped into cellulose, 
sodium alginate, lignin, proteins, chitosan, and microbially 
derived biomass. Their representative structures and functional 
groups, sorption and ion‐transport roles, pore and network 
features, main limitations, and suitable application scenarios are 
compared in Table 1 . The following subsections compare these 
families under a common set of physicochemical descriptors: 
sorption tendency, hydrated ionic conductivity, pore and 
network features, wet‐state stability, and suitable material roles 
and application scenarios. This framing ties material selection to 
intrinsic properties, not solely to reported performance. Sec -
tion 3.5 treats biomass‐derived polyelectrolytes separately 
because fixed‐charge chemistry pervades these families and 
provides the main lever for ion selectivity, as established in 
Section 2.3 .
3.1
|
Cellulose
Cellulose is the most widely used biomass feedstock in MEG. 
Within the microchannel architecture of natural wood, cellulose 
offers continuous and designable pathways for water diffusion 
and ion migration. It therefore frequently serves simultaneously 
as the moisture‐sorbing, ion‐generating, and conductive sup -
porting matrix. Beyond the moisture uptake and interfacial 
hydration afforded by its hydroxyl groups, its value lies in its 
processability into paper, cotton fabric, nanocellulose networks, 
aerogels [ 42 ]. Paper‐ and fabric‐based systems highlight its low 
cost and ease of integration. An adsorption–evaporation syner -
gistic device composed of LiCl‐loaded cellulose paper and a 
carbon‐black layer delivers long‐term output under ambient 
humidity [ 7 ], while combining carbon‐black cotton fabric with 
an ionic hydrogel yields stretchable, wearable power generation 
[ 43 ]. Nanocellulose offers a higher interfacial area and greater 
chemical tunability; introducing fixed charges by sulfonation, 
carboxylation, or quaternization enhances the generation and 
selective migration of hydrated ions [ 19 ].
Cellulose can be chemically modified or crosslinked to tune 
carrier density, ion selectivity, and wet‐state integrity while 
retaining its primary role as a scalable structural scaffold. 
Sulfated cellulose nanofibrils (CNF) promote ion diffusion and 
can be film‐cast with poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA) [ 44 ], CNF 
compounded with a poly (ionic liquid) forms an ionogel that 
retains ionic conductivity while achieving appreciable me -
chanical strength [ 25 ], and sodium‐ion anchoring or aluminum‐ 
ion crosslinking suppresses swelling at high humidity and 
markedly prolongs stable output [ 30, 45 ]. Wood, as a source of 
cellulose, has native oriented channels that favor directional 
water transport, and delignification, oxidation, or carboxylation 
can expose more fibers and raise the surface charge. Combined 
with LiCl, MXene, ZnO, or a lignin photothermal layer, these 
treatments reinforce the gradient and charge collection [ 28, 
46–48 ]. The limitations of cellulose for MEG are its intrinsically 
low ionic conductivity, typically one to two orders of magnitude 
below that of hydrated sodium alginate at comparable humidity 
because its hydroxyl groups hydrate but do not dissociate, and 
its tendency to swell under high humidity. Cellulose therefore 
performs best not as the carrier source but as the mechanically 
robust, scalable, ion‐conducting scaffold into which fixed 
charges are installed by post‐modification. Cellulose out -
performs alginate and proteins in processability, cost, and wet‐ 
state integrity, not in intrinsic output. This accounts for its 
dominance in large‐area, paper/wood‐based, and structurally 
integrated devices, and implies that a well‐designed cellulose 
system should balance moisture uptake, ion migration, and 
structural stability rather than optimize any single property 
(Table 1 ).

3.3

Lignin
Lignin, an abundant and low‐cost aromatic macromolecule of 
the plant cell wall, features high carbon content and facile 
chemical modification, yet its intrinsically weak hydrophilicity 
and film‐forming capability limit its use as a standalone matrix 
[ 31, 32 ]. It is more suitably applied as a functionalizable ion‐ 
transport medium, a precursor for conductive carbon, or a 
regulator of wood structure. A recent lignin‐derived MEG 
demonstrated that the sulfonic and phenolic‐hydroxyl chemistry 
of lignin can be translated into sustained and scalable moisture‐ 
enabled output, including operation over month‐scale times 
[ 33 ]. The sulfonic groups markedly enhance moisture adsorp -
tion and ion dissociation, while the phenolic hydroxyls promote 
interfacial hydration and charge separation, allowing lignin to 
participate directly in the humidity response rather than acting 
as an inert filler. When compounded with cationic biomass such 
as chitosan, polyelectrolyte interactions strengthen the fixed 
charge within the membrane and the interfacial potential dif -
ference, promoting directional ion migration [ 40 ]. Its high‐ 
carbon aromatic structure also makes it suitable as a carbon 
precursor, and lignin‐based carbon nanofibers can provide an 
electron‐transport network that improves charge collection and 
output stability [ 34 ]. Lignin is a double‐edged sword in wood 
devices. It reinforces mechanical strength yet hinders water 
infiltration owing to its hydrophobic inertness, which necessi -
tates delignification to expose more cellulose [ 47 ]. Separately, 
lignin possesses an intrinsic photothermal capability, and 
structural regulation can promote water desorption while pre -
serving a local gradient [ 35, 46, 52 ]. Unlike strongly hydrophilic 
polysaccharides, lignin and its derivatives afford complementary 
photothermal and moisture‐desorption‐regulating functions in 
composite architectures. Their low hygroscopicity and photo -
thermal response favor daytime regeneration and cyclic opera -
tion, as well as applications in carbonaceous or electrode layers, 
whereas they are generally less suitable as the primary ionic 
layer. The remaining challenges are limited hydrophilicity, 
processability, and structural heterogeneity, which may be 
addressed through functionalization and robust composite 
design [ 53–55 ].
3.4
|
Proteins, Chitosan, and Microbially Derived 
Biomass
Beyond wood‐based components, proteins and microbially 
derived materials offer complementary functionalities that 
further broaden the material design landscape [ 11 ]. Proteins 
contain carboxyl, amino, and amide groups that hydrogen‐bond 
with water and provide proton‐generation and proton‐migration 
sites, and their complex nanostructures are advantageous for 
constructing specialized ion channels. Protein nanowires offer a 
representative example: Geobacter ‐derived films with contin -
uous nanopores sustain a self‐maintained water gradient under 
ambient humidity and deliver stable output via proton migra -
tion, with an open‐circuit voltage near 0.5 V from a film only 
about 7 μm thick [ 6 ]. More broadly, sub‐100‐nm pores can 
sustain a charging gradient through asymmetric adsorption‐ 
desorption at exposed and sealed interfaces, making pore size 
a key design parameter across biomass films [ 56 ].
Structural proteins extend the same principle to processable, 
wearable formats. The polar residues and compositional gradi -
ents of silk fibroin and sericin have been leveraged to enhance 
adsorption and proton conduction and to establish an interfacial 
potential difference [ 38 ]. Self‐stratified devices made from food‐ 
grade proteins such as gelatin, combined with salt and activated 
carbon, achieve appreciable and wearable output at modest cost 
[ 39 ]. Chitosan provides a complementary cationic biomass 
platform: its readily protonated amino groups generate immo -
bilized positive charges for anion‐selective transport, whereas its 
poor film formation and wet‐state stability favor its use as a 
cationic interfacial layer in composites with anionic biomass 
rather than as a standalone MEG matrix [ 40 ]. As an adjacent 
evaporation‐driven bio‐hydrovoltaic example rather than an 
ambient‐moisture MEG, engineered microbial biofilms can 
serve as flexible, free‐standing layers and deliver approximately 
1 μW cm
− 2 
without maintaining cell viability [ 57 ]. Evidence for 
genuinely ambient‐moisture‐driven microbial‐biomass MEGs 
nevertheless remains limited. Waste microbial biomass provides 
an even lower‐cost feedstock; waste activated sludge, for 
instance, features a porous structure and abundant oxygen‐ and 
nitrogen‐containing groups, and when compounded with a 
water‐retaining gel it improves output stability under low or 
fluctuating humidity [ 41 ].
As summarized in Table 1 , these materials offer biocompati -
bility and mechanical compliance, and some protein‐ and 
microbial‐biomass systems operate at low humidity (approxi -
mately 20%–50% RH), favoring bio‐integrated low‐power appli -
cations; however, compositional variability, modest areal 
output, and limited scalability constrain broader use. They are 
therefore most promising as thin, nanoporous functional layers 
on inexpensive biomass supports, which is a direct application 
of the matrix‐component coupling.
3.5
|
Biomass‐Derived Polyelectrolytes: Fixed 
Charges, Ion Selectivity, and Ionic Diodes
Voltage generation depends on unequal cation and anion 
transport rather than on moisture uptake alone. Biomass sup -
plies anionic chemistries, including carboxylated or sulfated 
cellulose, alginate, and lignosulfonate, as well as cationic 
chemistries, including chitosan and quaternized cellulose. 
These fixed charges remain immobilized within the polymer 
matrix, enrich mobile counterions, exclude co‐ions, and shift 
transference numbers, thereby linking biomass polyelectrolyte 
chemistry to the voltage described by Equation ( 4 ).
Pairing oppositely charged phases can create ion‐selective het -
erojunctions with asymmetric transport. Such junctions may act 
as ionic diodes, favoring ion conduction in one direction while 
suppressing it in the reverse direction via interfacial ion deple -
tion. This rectification can reduce back‐diffusion and help pre -
serve the concentration gradient. For example, chitosan/ 
anionic‐biomass complexes and carboxylated/quaternized wood 
bilayers combine cation‐ and anion‐selective regions to suppress

Performance‐Optimization Strategies for 
Biomass MEG Materials
In response to the limitations noted above, optimization should 
coordinate the sequence of moisture sorption, ion dissociation, 
gradient maintenance, and charge collection rather than maxi -
mize moisture uptake alone. Table 2 compares representative 
devices together with their test conditions, electrical metrics, 
and reported output intervals. Based on its core design de -
cisions, assign a primary level to each device: molecular‐level 
ionic regulation (M, Section 4.1 ) modifies functional groups or 
carriers within a phase; structure‐level gradient construction (S, 
Section 4.2 ) spatially organizes pores, orientation, wettability, or 
composition within a scaffold; and device‐level composite inte -
gration (D, Section 4.3 ) assigns complementary functions to 
distinct components or electrodes. Other mechanisms present in 
a device are described as coupled functions rather than addi -
tional category labels. Section 4.4 then discusses how the three 
levels work together.
4.1
|
Molecular Level: Ionic Regulation
The key to ionic regulation is to make adsorbed water efficiently 
generate and drive carriers [ 59 ], rather than simply to adsorb 
more water. Chemical modification of natural polysaccharides, 
such as oxidation, amination, and sulfonation, can increase the 
number of active sites and enhance the interfacial hydration 
capacity [ 22, 44 ]. Partial complexation can stabilize a spatially 
asymmetric fixed‐charge distribution, whereas excessive charge 
compensation may reduce the density of accessible ion‐selective 
sites [ 40, 58 ]. A moderate introduction of ionic species, 
including NaCl, LiBr, and phytic acid, raises the overall con -
ductivity. However, excessive loading may promote ion leaching 
and accelerate gradient decay, thereby offsetting the conduc -
tivity gain [ 23, 27 ]. Increasing ionic strength can also compress 
the electric double layer and weaken ion selectivity; the opti -
mum salt content should therefore be matched to pore size. For 
long‐term operation, covalently tethered charges are preferable 
to freely doped salts when comparable conductivity can be 
maintained.
4.2
|
Structure Level: Gradient Construction
Many devices exhibit high initial output, but as moisture uptake 
approaches equilibrium the gradient weakens and the output 
decays. Therefore, structural design emphasizes maintaining 
internal asymmetric transport. The oriented channels of natural 
wood, modified with anionic/cationic layers, can guide differ -
ential ion migration [ 28 ]. Paper‐based and hydrophilic– 
hydrophobic structures jointly regulate water ingress and 
diffusion through functional‐group and moisture‐uptake gradi -
ents, keeping the device productive across different humidities 
[ 21 ]. In the nanoconfinement regime, stable nanopores can 
effectively inhibit back‐diffusion and enhance directional 
migration, indicating that the coordinated optimization of 
channel orientation, pore size, and humidity gradient outweighs 
a mere increase in specific surface area [ 19 ]. Finely tailored 
micro–meso–macroporous channels further show that cross‐ 
scale pore continuity can coordinate moisture capture, water 
redistribution, and directional ion transport [ 20 ]. Bioinspired 
hollow biomass fibers used for atmospheric water harvesting 
further support this principle: low‐resistance hollow channels 
can accelerate vapor transport and adsorption/desorption over a 
wide humidity range, providing structural inspiration for 
maintaining humidity gradients in biomass MEG devices [ 60 ]. 
Notably, an optimal level of water uptake is critical, as insuffi -
cient hydration limits ion generation and excessive hydration 
compromises the gradient while promoting charge recombina -
tion [ 10, 21, 61 ]. External environmental coupling can be used 
to hold the gradient open indefinitely: radiative‐cooling‐assisted 
water redistribution maintains a day‐night moisture gradient 
and supports self‐sustaining operation [ 61 ], and this strategy has 
recently been implemented directly in delignified wood with 
asymmetric hygroscopic and hydrophobic faces, so that the 
biomass scaffold itself provides both the anisotropic channels 
and the asymmetry [ 62 ].
4.3
|
Device Level: Composite Integration
For wearable and self‐powered sensing, materials should 
simultaneously satisfy flexibility, conductivity, wet‐state stabil -
ity, and processability [ 63 ], so compositing has become the 
principal route to application. At this level, the defining decision 
is to assign competing functions to different components rather 
than forcing one phase to maximize them all. A water‐retaining 
layer can be optimized for sorption and carrier supply, while a 
chemically distinct ion‐regulating layer provides ion selectivity 
and a separate collector extracts electronic current; this func -
tional separation has enabled durable, all‐climate operation 
[ 26 ]. Adjacent bio‐based water‐harvesting systems also highlight 
the value of multicomponent integration, as cellulose/alginate/ 
lignin gels couple hydrophilic networks, hygroscopic salts, and 
lignin photothermal conversion to achieve fast adsorption/ 
desorption and continuous drum‐type operation [ 64 ]. Skin–core 
fibers integrate a moisture‐sorbing outer layer, a conductive 
core, and a built‐in electric field, thereby aligning water 
migration with charge collection [ 50 ]. Bilayer structures 
comprising MXene‐loaded fabric and an ionic hydrogel combine 
capillary transport, electrical conductivity, and water retention, 
which broadens the applicable humidity range and retains sta -
ble output under tensile deformation [ 65 ]. These examples, 
together with printable fabric and gelatin‐based self‐stratified 
systems indicate a future direction where performance optimi -
zation should balance high power output, long‐term durability, 
mechanical flexibility, and sustainable manufacturing via a 
holistic approach to material composition, interfacial engi -
neering, and processing [ 39, 66 ]. Because the electrode is the 
point at which ionic current becomes electronic current, elec -
trode selection belongs to this level as well and should be re -
ported alongside the active material.

Coupling in Synergistic Design
Synergistic design is not the stacking of independent strategies, 
but the resolution of a trade‐off at one scale through a com -
plementary choice at another. Three recurring couplings pro -
vide practical design rules. Fixed charges and salts can increase 
carrier density but also accelerate hydration and gradient loss, 
so ionic strength should be matched to pore size through Debye‐ 
scale nanoconfinement to preserve selectivity without sacri -
ficing conductivity [ 19, 25 ]. Because a gradient in a single 
component decays at equilibrium, this chemical‐structural bal -
ance should be complemented by separating sorption and 
desorption between components or by introducing radiative‐ 
cooling or lignin‐photothermal regeneration, thereby convert -
ing a transient gradient into a renewable cycle [ 61, 62, 64 ]. At 
the device level, conductive fillers improve charge collection but 
may short‐circuit ionic pathways, whereas hygroscopic salts 
raise conductivity but can leach; spatially separating a cova -
lently charged ionic phase from the electronic collector there -
fore makes the two transport modes complementary and allows 
each component to add one function without weakening the 
gradient [ 26, 34, 50 ]. For biomass MEG, operating humidity sets 
the required water‐activity window; pore size limits usable ionic 
strength, and selectivity determines voltage. Table 1 identifies 
suitable application scenarios for each biomass family, while 
complementary functions are supplied by partner components. 
High performance therefore comes from resolving coupled 
trade‐offs rather than maximizing any single property.

Challenges and Outlook
After nearly a decade of development, biomass‐based MEG has 
demonstrated distinctive advantages in material systems, device 
architectures, and output forms, yet several problems remain 
before it can truly reach practical use. The unit output and 
energy‐conversion efficiency of current biomass devices remain 
low, and the trade‐off between power density and operating 
duration is particularly pronounced. Enhancing instantaneous 
output through salt doping or highly hygroscopic components 
often entails ion loss and gradient decay over long‐term opera -
tion, which consequently renders power generation dependent 
on the irreversible consumption of components and precludes a 
reversible cycle [ 67 ]. Photocatalysis‐enhanced hydrovoltaic de -
signs offer one possible solution by consuming accumulated 
ions and restoring the concentration gradient, thereby enabling 
hundreds of hours of more sustainable output [ 68 ]. The 
demonstration that MEG discharge can be cascaded into 
hydrogen evolution suggests a second route in which the 
accumulated carriers are exported chemically rather than 
allowed to build up [ 29 ]. Biomass skeletons generally also tend 
to swell, plasticize, or even degrade slowly in the wet state, and 
constructing a stable network that resists swelling and ion loss 
while maintaining long‐term reversible sorption–desorption is 
the key to extending lifetime [ 33 ]. Beyond these, the diversity of 
biomass sources and the natural variability in composition and 
microstructure impose demands on device consistency and 
scalable processing.
Future development should move from leachable salt doping 
toward covalently tethered charges, with output evaluated at 
controlled pore size and substitution degree. This chemistry can 
be embedded in all‐biomass architectures that use cellulose for 
structural support, alginate and chitosan as complementary 
fixed‐charge phases, lignin‐based photothermal components for 
gradient regeneration, lignin‐derived carbon for electronic 
collection, and selected proteins for low‐RH operation. Coupling 
these material roles with radiative cooling or asymmetric 
wettability could further strengthen the desorption half‐cycle 
and sustain the internal gradient [ 35, 46, 52, 61, 62 ]. Reliable 
translation will also require treating feedstock variability as a 
measurable design parameter by reporting crystallinity, substi -
tution, pore‐size distribution, and source. Performance com -
parisons should therefore consistently specify RH and 
temperature, active area and thickness, electrode material, 
external load, power‐density basis, and an explicit duration 
criterion [ 18 ], as summarized in Table 2 . Meanwhile, biode -
gradability and end‐of‐life behavior should be experimentally 
verified rather than assumed.
With these directions in view, the outlook for biomass MEG is best 
stated in terms of complementarity rather than competition. 
Synergistically exploiting the complementarity of functional 
groups across different biomass materials, together with hierar -
chical structure and gradient design, promises to narrow the gap 
between power and lifetime while preserving renewability. Cross‐ 
fertilization with adjacent fields, including atmospheric water 
harvesting, wearable self‐powered sensing, underwater elec -
tronics, and living biomass, continues to expand the application 
landscape; scalable cellulose‐paper water harvesters and contin -
uous device architectures in particular demonstrate how biomass 
processing, rapid moisture kinetics, and system‐level operation 
can be integrated, offering useful lessons for translating biomass 
MEG from material performance to practical devices [ 69 ]. 
Through such cross‐disciplinary convergence, and with the 
design framework set out in this minireview as an organizing 
principle, biomass‐based MEG is expected to establish a distinc -
tive role in distributed, sustainable clean energy.
