ABSTRACT
Water scarcity and salinity pose critical challenges that necessitate the development of efficient 
desalination processes for brackish water treatment. This study investigates the use of activated carbon 
derived from date palm pollen as a potential electrode material for batch capacitive deionization (CDI). 
Activated carbon exhibits an exceptionally large surface area of 2586.9 m²·g −1, as determined by 
Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area analysis, which provides numerous sites for electrostatic ion 
accumulation during CDI operation. When activated carbon is used as an electrode in batch CDI 
experiments with a 500 ppm NaCl feedstock at an applied cell voltage of 1.2 V, the electrode exhibits a 
salt adsorption capacity (SAC) of 38.0 mg·g−1 after 120 min of adsorption. These results indicate effective 
electrosorptive salt removal from brackish water under practical operating conditions. Additionally, 
electrochemical measurements reveal that the activated carbon electrodes exhibit a high specific 
capacitance of 92 F·g −1, indicating efficient storage of electric charge in electric double layers. The high 
SAC and specific capacitance and the large BET surface area confirm that the date-palm-pollen-derived 
activated carbon is a promising electrode material for batch CDI desalination systems.

1. Introduction
Water scarcity is now recognized as one of the most significant global concerns of this century, threatening 
to affect billions of people around the world because of the increased pressure created by population 
explosion, rapid industrialization, and climate change. Additionally, water quality is declining owing to 
pollution generated by agricultural activities and industrial emissions [1],[2]. Thus, new methods have 
garnered tremendous interest in producing clean water through desalination technologies such as reverse 
osmosis (RO) and thermal distillation. Existing methods used to purify seawater through either RO or 
thermal distillation have proven highly effective but require significantly high energy, which makes these 
processes economically unfeasible for producing fresh water from brackish or low-salinity feedstocks 
[3],[4]. The urgent need for sustainable, energy-efficient, and economical methods for producing purified 
water has led researchers to develop capacitive deionization (CDI) technology. CDI is an attractive 
alternative technology that provides results similar to those of RO and thermal distillation; it requires 
significantly lower energy consumption, has a reduced environmental impact due to the chemicals used, 
and possesses modular design capabilities [5],[6]. CDI is an electrochemically operated, water desalination 
technology that removes ionic impurities by reversibly storing them into the electric double layers (EDLs) 
formed at the interface between two oppositely charged porous electrodes [7],[8]. Unlike pressure-driven 
or thermal methods, CDI utilizes low applied voltages (typically between 0.8 and 1.6 V) to electrosorb ions 
onto the surfaces of two oppositely charged electrodes. Ions are then released back into the aqueous 
solution when the voltage is reversed or reduced, allowing electrode regeneration and continuous CDI 
operation through repeated cycles of adsorption and desorption [9],[10]. The distinct advantages of CDI

over traditional desalination processes include significantly reduced energy consumption for brackish 
water applications. Reportedly, energy requirements depend on feed salinity and system configuration, 
and various studies indicate a range of 0.27–2.0 kWh·mol⁻¹ for CDI systems, whereas RO systems treating 
similar feed solutions require 3–5 kWh·mol⁻¹ [11]. Additionally, CDI does not need high-pressure pumping 
or thermal energy input, reducing system complexity and capital costs and requiring minimum 
maintenance [12],[13]. CDI is a modular and scalable solution for decentralized water treatment systems 
in remote and resource-limited areas [14]. Additionally, CDI generates only a concentrated brine waste 
stream and does not produce secondary waste streams; the concentrated brine can be treated or reused 
as a valuable resource [15]. 
Recent developments in CDI architecture have enabled greater operational capability and expandability 
of the technology. The incorporation of ion-exchange membranes into membrane CDI (mCDI) systems to 
impede co-ion expulsion allows a practical increase in the charge efficiency of CDI systems from low 
percent values to ranges of 80–95% [15],[16],[17]. By utilizing continuously flowing carbon slurries as 
electrodes, flow-electrode CDI systems have eliminated periodic electrode regeneration, thus allowing 
continuous operation [18],[19]. Furthermore, hybrid CDI systems that utilize both capacitive and Faradaic 
(charge storage) mechanisms have demonstrated enhanced salt hay-stacking characteristics and 
improved selectivity for several targeted ions [20],[21],[20]. These technological advancements have 
introduced several challenges in CDI operation, including limited salt adsorption capacity (SAC) in relation 
to that achieved through the RO process, co-ion expulsion in symmetric CDI systems, parasitic Faradaic 
reactions that reduce CDI charge efficiency, and long-term cycling instability [22],[23]. Electrode materials 
are critical to the operation of CDI systems and contribute significantly to how effectively a CDI system 
can desalinate water [24],[1]. The performance of CDI electrodes is influenced by several interrelated 
material parameters, including (i) specific surface area (SSA), which indicates the number of available sites 
for ion adsorption; (ii) pore size distribution, where micropores provide sites for high capacitance (less 
than 2 nm) and mesopores promote rapid ion transport (2–50 nm); (iii) electrical conductivity of the 
electrode material, which enables low resistance by minimizing ohmic losses and allowing a more uniform 
charge distribution; (iv) surface wettability and hydrophilicity of the electrode material, which promote 
improved ion access to the internal porous structure of the electrode; and (v) electrochemical stability of 
the electrode material over several charge–discharge cycles [25],[26],[27],[26]. Activated carbon has 
become a dominant electrode material used in CDI applications owing to a distinct combination of its 
properties, namely, large SSA (usually between 1,000 and 3,000 m²·g⁻¹), hierarchical porosity, excellent 
chemical stability, good electrical conductivity, and low cost [28],[29]. Activated carbon can be tuned to 
improve CDI performance using various activation methods, including physical activation with CO₂ or 
steam and chemical activation using KOH, NaOH, H₃PO₄, or ZnCl₂, alongside post-processing modifications 
[30],[31]. 
Chemical activation using KOH produces activated carbon with an ultra-large SSA (greater than 3,000 
m²·g⁻¹) and excellent microporosity; however, excessive chemical activation negatively affects the 
structural integrity and electrical conductivity of activated carbon [32],[33]. In addition to the 
aforementioned conventional activation methods, several advanced electrode engineering approaches 
have been developed to address the inherent limitations of untreated activated carbon. These include 
introducing heteroatoms (particularly nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur) as dopants into the

activated carbon matrix; creating pseudocapacitive sites for charge storage; improving the surface 
wettability of activated carbon; enhancing the electrical conductivity of activated carbon; and producing 
a favorable surface charge distribution that prevents co-ion expulsion [38],[34],[40]. Additional surface 
treatments, such as surface functionalization via plasma treatment, metal ion modification, or oxidative 
treatments, introduce a greater number of oxygen-containing functional groups onto the surface of

improved charge efficiency, rate performance, and cycling performance have been demonstrated in 
composite electrode structures that utilize carbon materials such as MXenes, carbon nanotubes, 
graphene, activated carbon fibers, and conducting polymers [37],[38],[39],[40].
Biomass precursors provide a model for a sustainable circular economy by enabling the fabrication of 
activated carbon electrodes from plant-driven carbon structures using renewable resources [39]-[41]. 
Biomass-derived activated carbon possesses numerous beneficial characteristics in relation to traditional 
carbon sources manufactured from fossil fuels or petroleum: (1) an abundant and renewable supply 
source; (2) a low-cost to negative-cost source derived from agricultural and industrial waste streams; (3) 
low environmental impact due to the waste valorization process; (4) the presence of heteroatoms (such 
as nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, and phosphorus) within the carbon structure that enhance electrochemical 
properties; and (5) diverse microstructures resulting from differences in feedstock sources, which allow 
electrode properties to be tailored to specific application [42]-[43].
Various agricultural byproducts, food-processing wastes, and lignocellulosic materials have been 
transformed to high-performance CDI electrodes. Activated carbon derived from rice husks demonstrates 
ultrafast desalination kinetics with an SAC of ~30 mg·g−1 owing to its highly porous structure and high SSA 
[44]. Activated carbon produced from coconut shells and activated with KOH exhibits SSAs as high as 2,500 
m2·g−1 and excellent electrochemical stability during multiple charge–discharge cycles [30]. Hollow fibers 
consisting of wood-derived activated carbon exhibit a specific capacitance of 295 F·g −1 and retain 99.5% 
of their capacitance after 10,000 cycles [45]. Nitrogen-doped porous carbon derived from Platanus fruit 
exhibits an SSA of 3846 m 2·g−1, a specific capacitance of 138.81 F·g −1, and an SAC of 18.05 mg·g −1 [46]. 
Nitrogen-doped polyhedral macrotubes derived from lotus stems achieve specific capacitance and SAC of 
221.34 F·g−1 and 26.07 mg·g −1, respectively, with an 85% capacity retention after 50 cycles [47]. Metal-
ion-modified super activated carbon demonstrates a significant increase in SAC after treatment with Cu2+ 
ions (from 35.4 to 49.3 mg·g−1) and a charge efficiency of 93.4% [37].
More recently, several types of biomass materials have been converted to CDI electrodes. For example, 
sugarcane-bagasse-derived activated carbon possesses a cross-linked 3D carbon–nitrogen structure and 
the corresponding electrode exhibits a stable capacitance of ~47 F·g −1 [48]. Biomass sources include 
residual glycerol derived from biodiesel production [49], coffee grounds [50], rattan, and other 
biomorphic woods that possess naturally formed microchannels for improved ionic transport [51] as well 
as various agriculture and forestry byproducts [52],[53]. Existing research has shown that biomass-derived 
activated carbon not only exhibits electrochemical performance comparable to or exceeding that of 
commercial activated carbon but also addresses waste management challenges and reduces production 
costs [54],[55].

The date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.; family Arecaceae) is among the most economically valuable plants 
in arid (such as deserts) and semi-arid (such as deserts with some annual rainfall) regions, particularly in 
the Middle East and North Africa, where it is a dominant tree and an important source of food and 
economic value [56]. As byproducts of date palm fruit and fronds, large quantities of agricultural wastes 
are generated and disposed of, burned, or left to decompose. The majority of agricultural wastes 
generated during date palm cultivation have little or no economic value when discarded [57]. Date palm 
pollen is produced in large quantities during the pollination season and is a waste product that may have 
significant value. Less than 1% of the pollen is used for pollination, leaving a large quantity of unused 
pollen [58]. From a materials perspective, pollen grains serve as a precursor for the production of 
activated carbon. Pollen grains possess complex hierarchically arranged microstructures and naturally 
occurring nanostructures that remain intact and preserve structural integrity during thermal treatment or 
carbonization. Therefore, pollen-grain-derived activated carbon exhibits electrochemical activity and pore 
characteristics unique to pollen [59]. During the thermal processing of pollen, its biochemical composition, 
including proteins, fatty acids, carbohydrates, and various heteroatom-containing compounds, contribute 
to heteroatom incorporation during carbonization, which enhances the electrical conductivity of the 
resulting carbon [60],[61]. The utilization of date palm pollen as a precursor for activated carbon 
production advances the sustainable use of this biomass and creates value-added products for water 
treatment. Although existing research suggests that date palm pollen has potential as a precursor for the 
fabrication of activated carbon, the synthesis, characterization, and CDI performance of activated carbon 
derived from date palm pollen have not yet been systematically evaluated. Research on this product is 
noteworthy for two main reasons: 1) a substantial body of literature supports the use of various types of 
biomasses as precursors for the production of activated carbon; and 2) significant quantities of date palm 
pollen are readily available in regions where date palm cultivation is widespread. Given the unique 
microstructural characteristics of date palm pollen and the potential presence of intrinsic heteroatoms, 
further research should investigate the synthesis and CDI performance of activated carbon derived from 
date palm pollen to determine whether it can exhibit high efficiency in CDI applications while supporting 
sustainable feedstock utilization.
This study aims to address the existing research gap regarding the use of date palm pollen as a source of 
large-surface-area activated carbon for CDI electrodes. Particularly, this study focuses on (1) synthesizing 
activated carbon from date palm pollen through optimized chemical activation processes that maximize 
the surface area and generate hierarchical pore structures; (2) characterizing the physicochemical 
properties of date-palm-pollen-derived activated carbon, including surface area, pore size distribution, 
morphology, elemental composition, and surface chemistry; (3) fabricating CDI electrodes using the 
produced activated carbon and evaluating their electrochemical performance, including specific 
capacitance, charge–discharge behavior, and electrochemical stability; (4) evaluating the performance of 
date-palm-pollen-derived activated carbon during desalination concerning salt adsorption capacity, 
charge efficiency, desalination rate, and cycling stability; and (5) comparing the performance of the 
developed CDI electrodes with that of previously reported biomass-derived and commercial activated 
carbon materials in the literature. This study systematically investigates the feasibility of using date palm 
pollen as an innovative precursor for producing high-performance CDI electrodes, contributes to the 
development of sustainable biomass-based electrode materials, and creates opportunities for recycling

2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Date-Palm-Pollen-Derived Activated Carbon Preparation
Al-Hassa, located in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, is one of the largest producers of dates in the Gulf 
Cooperation Council (GCC) region. For this study, pollen from date palm plantations in Al-Hassa was 
collected and prepared during the pollination period. After collection, the pollen was used to produce 
activated carbon. The method used to prepare this activated carbon involved several sequential steps 
designed to create a large surface area and impart appropriate porosity for charge–discharge cycling. 
Accordingly, the preparation process involved a series of pre-treatment and chemical conversion steps 
required to produce activated carbon.
The pre-treatment process began with washing freshly collected pollen with deionized water to remove 
surface contaminants such as dust, debris, and other soluble impurities. The washed pollen was then dried 
in an oven at 80 °C for 24 h to remove moisture and obtain a uniform starting material. The dried pollen 
was further subjected to carbonization in a tubular furnace in a nitrogen atmosphere to prevent oxidation 
during the process. Carbonization was performed at 900 °C in an inert atmosphere. When the carbonized 
material was subsequently mixed with activating agents (such as KOH) or other chemical compounds that 
impart porosity and create a large surface area, a significantly greater number of micropores formed 
compared to those in materials produced through conventional carbonization processes.
The activation of carbonized material involves the addition of an activating agent, typically KOH, to impart 
the desired porosity or form significantly small pores; therefore, the carbonized material was mixed with 
the activating agent prior to and during heat treatment. During heat treatment, a uniform heating rate of 
5–10 °C·min−1 was maintained, and the maximum temperature was held for 1–2 h. The resulting material 
possessed a large internal surface area and exhibited a well-defined pore size distribution, which yielded 
a significantly greater amount of activated carbon than would otherwise be obtained.
After chemical activation, the carbonized material was cooled to room temperature while maintained in 
a nitrogen atmosphere. Further, the material was washed with dilute HCl solution to remove residual 
activating agents and mineral impurities and was subsequently rinsed with deionized water until a neutral 
pH was achieved. The final product was a uniformly dried material that was ground into a fine powder, 
which was subsequently used to fabricate electrodes for CDI applications.

The structural, chemical, thermal, and morphological characteristics of date-palm-pollen-derived 
activated carbon were characterized using various analytical techniques. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns 
of activated carbon were recorded using a Malvern PANalytical X’Pert3 X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα 
radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) operating at 40 kV and 40 mA over a scanning range of 10°–80° with a step size of 
0.02° and a scanning rate of 2° min −1. The crystalline structure and the extent of graphitization were 
inferred from the XRD patterns. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to determine the 
thermal stability of activated carbon by using a thermogravimetric analyzer. Approximately 5–10 mg of 
the sample was heated to 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C·min −1 in a nitrogen atmosphere at a flow rate 
of 50 mL·min−1. Weight loss observed in the thermogravimetry curve indicated the thermal decomposition 
behavior, moisture content, volatile matter, and fixed carbon content of activated carbon. Brunauer–
Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was performed using a Quantachrome® ASiQwin™ instrument to evaluate 
the surface area and porosity from N₂ adsorption data. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of 
activated carbon was conducted using a Nicolet Is5 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo 
Scientific) over the spectral range of 4,000–400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹. Scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) was performed to visualize the surface morphology and microstructure of activated 
carbon. Samples were mounted on an aluminum stub using conductive carbon tape and were coated with 
a thin layer of gold or platinum to improve electrical conductivity and imaging quality. SEM images were 
obtained at various magnifications to illustrate surface texture, porosity, and particle morphology. 
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was carried out to observe the internal structure and pore 
morphology of activated carbon using the JEOL JEM2100F transmission electron microscope operating at 
200 kV. For TEM sample preparation, a small amount of activated carbon was dispersed in ethanol using 
ultrasonication. A drop of the dispersion was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid. TEM images 
were obtained to analyze the pore structure, arrangement of carbon layers, and extent of graphitization 
at the nanoscale.
2.3. Electrochemical Testing
The capacitive behavior, charge storage properties, and electrochemical performance of date-palm-
pollen-derived activated carbon were evaluated by electrical testing of the electrode in an aqueous 
electrolyte using a three-electrode system. The three-electrode system was operated using a 
multichannel potentiostat (Corrtest CS310) with the CS Studio6 software for data acquisition. A platinum 
mesh electrode served as the counter electrode, an Ag/AgCl electrode served as the reference electrode, 
and date-palm-pollen-derived activated carbon (DPAC@CP) served as the working electrode. Cyclic 
voltammetry (CV) was performed using the DPAC@CP electrode at scan rates of 5–100 mV·s −1 within a 
potential window of 0.2–0.8 V in an aqueous 1 M NaCl solution (vs. Ag/AgCl). Na ion extraction data were 
obtained using a 60 mL electrochemical cell, and the distance between the electrodes was maintained at 
15 mm. CV curves were used to evaluate the electromechanical characteristics, capacitance, and rate of 
charge delivery of DPAC@CP electrodes. The specific capacitance (C, F·g) was calculated from the CV

2.4. CDI Performance Evaluation
CDI testing was performed to determine the desalination capacity of the DPAC@CP electrodes using a 
custom-designed CDI cell under varying operating conditions. 
Cell configuration for CDI testing: The CDI cell consisted of two identically sized activated carbon 
electrodes separated by an insulating spacer (thickness: 1–2 mm) and arranged in a symmetric 
configuration. All electrodes were fabricated using the same method employed for electrochemical 
testing but were produced at a larger size to allow flow-through operation. Titanium plates served as 
current collectors. The CDI cell was assembled in a flow-through configuration with inlet and outlet ports 
to facilitate continuous solution flow through the cell.
CDI testing method: A saline solution with a specified initial conductivity was prepared by dissolving NaCl 
in deionized water. The saline solution was pumped through the CDI cell using a peristaltic pump at 
predefined flow rates. During the adsorption process, a DC power supply connected between the 
electrodes applied a constant voltage across the cell. The conductivity of the treated saline solution was 
continuously monitored at the outlet of the CDI cell using a conductivity meter. The adsorption process 
was considered complete when the conductivity stabilized at a low and constant value, indicating the 
saturation of both electrodes. The desorption or regeneration process, which involved removing the 
applied voltage and flushing the electrodes with the same solution, continued until the solution returned 
to its original conductivity level (baseline).
Operating conditions for CDI testing: CDI performance was systematically evaluated under multiple 
operating conditions.

surface is chemically heterogeneous but thermally stable, which is desirable for processes involving

indicates that nitrogen uptake increases sharply at low relative pressure (p/p₀), demonstrating significant

According to the IUPAC classification of gas adsorption behavior, the isotherm represents a hybrid of Type 
I and Type IV characteristics typical of activated carbon, indicating that micropores dominate the structure 
while transport pores are also present [65]. The BET surface area is ~2586.9 m²·g⁻¹, total pore volume is 
~1.6428 cm³·g−1, and average pore size is ~1.276 nm, suggesting that micropores contribute significantly 
to the total pore volume and mass transfer properties of the carbon sample; therefore, in combination 
with the large amount of micropore volume, a considerable number of wider porosities contribute 
significantly to the bulk pore volume and its mass transfer. Figure 1d shows the FTIR spectrum, confirming 
the presence of oxygen-containing polar functional groups on the carbon surface. The broad absorption 
band at ~3251 cm⁻¹ corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations associated with phenolic hydroxyl groups or 
adsorbed H 2O. This peak corresponds to the C=C skeletal vibrations of aromatic compounds that 
contribute to the carbon framework; however, in oxidized or activated carbon, this region can also include 
contributions from oxygenated aromatic and conjugated carbon structures [66]. The pronounced 
absorption bands at 986 and 599 cm⁻¹ fall within the region often referred to as the fingerprint area, 
where C–O stretching vibrations are commonly observed. However, based on the XRD evidence indicating 
the presence of quartz or silica, these bands can also originate from Si–O stretching vibrations associated 
with residual silica or mineral matter. The presence of similar strong absorption bands in the same spectral 
region of reference IR spectra further supports the assignment to quartz or silica species. 
3.2. Morphological Characterization
The SEM image shown in Figure 2a reveals the formation of a hierarchical porous structure in activated 
carbon, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies reporting that biomass-derived activated 
carbon typically exhibits an irregular, porous morphology with numerous voids, crevices, and 
interconnected pathways formed during the activation and etching of the original biomass structure [67]. 
This morphology has been widely reported for chemically activated bio-derived carbon materials and 
facilitates rapid mass and ion transport through mesoporous and macroporous transport channels that 
act as diffusion pathways and buffering reservoirs while providing microporous adsorption sites. The TEM 
analysis shown in Figure 2b indicates that the material possesses a largely disordered (or turbostratic) 
carbon structure characterized by significant contrast variations and short, locally aligned lattice fringes 
corresponding to nanographitic microdomains embedded within an amorphous carbon matrix. 
These morphological characteristics of turbostratic carbon structures have been widely reported to 
enhance mass transport, electrochemical accessibility, and adsorption performance in porous carbon 
materials [68].

3.3.1. Galvanostatic Charge–Discharge Analysis
Electrochemical tests were conducted through GCD tests to investigate the charging–discharging 
characteristics, rate capabilities, resistive losses, and cycling stability of activated carbon electrodes 
derived from date palm pollen for electrochemical desalination and energy storage applications. The GCD 
profiles of the DPAC@CP electrodes exhibit a symmetric triangular shape, as shown in Figure 4a, which is 
characteristic of the ideal capacitive behavior and is consistent with the CV results. This symmetry 
indicates a high coulombic efficiency and highly reversible charge storage. The quasi-linear voltage–time 
relationship further confirms that the charge storage mechanism is primarily governed by EDLC rather 
than diffusion-controlled Faradaic reactions [72]. The longest discharge time, corresponding to the highest 
specific capacitance, occurs at the lowest current density 1.0 A·g⁻¹. However, as the current density 
increases to 3.0 A·g⁻¹, the discharge time decreases, indicating a reduction in capacitance due to kinetic 
limitations such as restricted ion transport within the fine pore structure and increased polarization. 
However, the preservation of the triangular GCD profile across the tested current densities confirms the 
high-rate capability of the electrodes. 
The internal resistance of the DPAC@CP electrodes was determined from the instantaneous IR drop 
observed at the beginning of the discharge curve, as illustrated in Figure 4b. This IR drop reflects the 
combined contribution of ESR, charge-transfer resistance, and the resistance associated with the current 
collector–electrode interface. The date-palm-pollen-derived electrodes exhibit a relatively low internal 
resistance across the tested current densities. The magnitude of the IR drop increases with current 
density, consistent with Ohm's law (ΔV = IR), indicating efficient electronic and ionic transport and low 
resistive losses, which is also supported by EIS results [72]. Minimizing IR drop is critical in CDI systems 
because large IR drop leads to higher energy consumption during repetitive charging and discharging 
cycles associated with adsorption and desorption processes, thereby reducing the overall energy 
efficiency and operating life of the CDI system.

of 3.0 A·g⁻¹. As shown in Figure 4c, the electrodes demonstrate excellent stability, retaining ~96% of their

4.1. Effect of Applied Voltage
The effect of cell voltage on CDI desalination performance was evaluated at a constant flow rate and feed 
concentration by continuously monitoring the conductivity of the saline solution during the adsorption 
(charging) step; the results are shown in Figure 5a. The conductivity of the solution was measured 
immediately after the application of voltage. Conductivity decreases rapidly, confirming the removal of 
ions from the aqueous solution through electrosorptive ion adsorption via EDL formation at the DPAC@CP 
electrodes. This behavior is consistent with previously reported CDI operation within an applied voltage 
range of 1–1.2 V [75]. At an applied voltage of 0.8 V, the conductivity of the saline solution decreases from 
1050 to 840 μS·cm⁻¹ after 120 min. A rapid decrease to ~690 μS·cm⁻¹ is observed at 1.0 V after 20 min, 
indicating improved ion adsorption kinetics. However, a relatively slower decrease in conductivity from 
940 to 110 μS·cm⁻¹ is observed at 1.2 volts, suggesting less efficient net sodium ion removal despite the 
higher driving potential. 
The nonlinear relationship between conductivity reduction and increasing applied voltage has been 
reported in previous studies on CDI and is often attributed to reduced charge efficiency at higher 
polarization levels. This reduction can result from increased co-ion expulsion and the occurrence of 
parasitic Faradaic reactions, such as electrode oxidation or water electrolysis, particularly when the 
applied voltage approaches or exceeds the practical stability limits for CDI operation [76]. To compare 
both desalination capacity and desalination kinetics, CDI Ragone plot (SAR vs SAC) analysis was performed 
according to established CDI benchmarking methods; the plots are shown in Figure 5b. The CDI Ragone 
plots obtained at three different applied voltages demonstrate an expansion of the SAR–SAC performance 
envelope with increasing voltage. At 0.8 V, the CDI system exhibits an SAC of ~15 mg·g −1 with an SAR of 
0.55 mg·g−1min−1. At 1 V, the SAC increases to ~25 mg·g−1 with an SAR of ~0.65 mg·g−1min−1. Moreover, the 
CDI performance further improves at an applied voltage of 1.2 V, reaching an SAC of ~38 mg·g −1 and an 
SAR of 1.1 mg·g−1min−1. This enhancement of CDI performance with increasing voltage reflects the strong 
electrostatic driving forces for ion adsorption and the resulting increase in ion accumulation within the 
EDL. However, the transient conductivity profiles indicate that the overall desalination performance is 
strongly influenced by charge efficiency and the extent of side reactions. Therefore, the "optimal" 
operating voltage for CDI systems must balance the improved SAC and SAR achieved at high voltages with 
the efficiency losses associated with accelerated parasitic reactions and reduced charge utilization [77] 
[75].
4.2. Effect of Flow Rate
The effect of hydrodynamic conditions on the desalination performance of the CDI cell was investigated 
by maintaining a constant feed salinity while systematically varying the feed flow rate at a fixed applied 
voltage of 1.2 V; the results are shown in Figure 5c. The dynamics of the CDI systems are governed by the 
relationship between ion transport and electrode charge storage [78]. Ion transport occurs through the 
combined effects of diffusion and advection within the spacer and flow channels, along with diffusion in 
the porous electrodes. Under different hydrodynamic conditions, the flow rate directly influences the rate 
at which ions contact the electrode surface and the residence time available for ions to diffuse into the 
internal pore structure of the electrodes.

The experimental results show that after 120 min of operation, changes in the flow rate significantly affect 
the effluent conductivity. As the flow rate increases from 5 to 20 mL·min −1, the effluent conductivity 
decreases from 1,050 μS·cm −1 to 870 and 640 μS·cm −1 at 5 and 10 mL·min −1, respectively, and then 
increases to 820 at 20 mL·min −1. These results indicate that the optimal flow rate for the operating 
conditions of the CDI system is ~10 mL·min −1. At high flow rates (20 mL·min −1), the reduced desalination 
performance is attributed to insufficient residence time for ions to diffuse into the internal pore structure 
of the electrodes, particularly the smaller micropores, before the solution exits the CDI cell. Conversely, 
at low flow rates (5 mL·min −1), the longer contact time between the solution and electrode is partially 
offset by the development of thicker concentration boundary layers and increased concentration 
polarization, both of which reduce the effective driving force for ion adsorption at the electrode interface 
[73]. The trade-off between desalination capacity and adsorption rate under different hydrodynamic 
conditions was further evaluated by plotting SAR versus SAC at an applied voltage of 1.2 V. The resulting 
Ragone plot in Figure 5d allows a quantitative assessment of CDI performance across the tested flow rates 
[79]. The data indicate that increasing the flow rate enhances the kinetic performance of the CDI system. 
For example, at a flow rate of 5 mL·min−1, the CDI system exhibits an SAR of ~0.4 mg·g −1min−1 and an SAC 
of ~25 mg·g −1. At 10 mL·min −1, the SAR increases to ~0.6 mg·g −1min−1 with SAC ranging between 
approximately 15 and 28 mg·g−1. At 20 mL·min−1, the system achieves a peak SAR of ~1.3 mg·g −1min−1 (at 
higher-than-average capacities). This behavior is consistent with CDI transport mechanisms: high flow 
rates accelerate convective mass transfer and reduce the external concentration gradient (resulting in 
higher SAR), whereas short contact times limit the approach to equilibrium adsorption (reduced SAC). 
Overall, the results demonstrate that the electrodes maintain effective desalination performance 
(reducing SAC and increasing SAR) across a range of flow rates, indicating efficient ion transport 
(hierarchical porosity) and operational flexibility for CDI systems [73].
The DPAC@CP electrodes exhibit effective results at various flow rates, which allows for flexibility and 
adaptability of operation when used in different scenarios. Good desalination at high flow rates can be 
achieved owing to the efficient movement of ions through the hierarchical pore structure of the carbon 
electrode material and the high electrochemical activity associated with carbon.
4.3. Effect of Solution Concentration
Feed salinity (100–500 ppm) significantly influences conductivity reduction and ion removal efficiency in 
CDI systems; the corresponding results are shown in Figure 5e. At 100 ppm, conductivity decreases from 
240 to 160 μS·cm⁻¹, corresponding to an approximate reduction of 80 μS·cm⁻¹ between 100 and 0 ppm. 
When feed salinity increases to 250 ppm, the conductivity decreases from 600 to 420 μS·cm⁻¹, 
representing a conductivity reduction of ~180 μS·cm⁻¹. At a higher feed concentration of 500 ppm, the 
conductivity decreases from approximately 1,050 to 630 μS·cm⁻¹, resulting in a reduction of ~420 μS cm⁻¹ 
for 0–50 ppm. Therefore, the larger decrease in conductivity observed at higher salinity levels can be 
attributed to the greater availability of ions for adsorption and the lower resistance of the solution. 
However, the efficiency of ion removal is influenced by not only ion concentration but also factors such 
as ion strength and applied voltage. Therefore, evaluating CDI performance solely based on conductivity 
reduction is insufficient for determining the optimal operating concentration [80].

To further evaluate desalination performance, CDI Ragone plots (SAR versus SAC) were constructed for

variations in applied voltage, flow rate, and feed concentration) demonstrates that activated carbon 
derived from date palm pollen is a highly effective electrode material for CDI. The DPAC@CP electrodes 
exhibit high desalination performance, good operational flexibility, and favorable capacity–rate 
characteristics, indicating their application scope in practical water desalination processes.

surface areas as large as ~2680 m2·g−1 that still exhibit relatively low SAC values [88]. Therefore, additional

This study demonstrated the successful valorization of date palm pollen into ultra-large-surface-area 
activated carbon, which exhibited superior electrochemical and desalination performance when used as 
a sustainable CDI electrode. Structural characterization revealed that the material consists primarily of an 
amorphous or turbostratic carbon framework with a well-developed hierarchical porous structure, 
providing a large BET surface area of 2586.9 m²·g⁻¹. This hierarchical porosity facilitated both efficient ion 
storage within micropores and rapid ion transport. Electrochemical evaluation confirmed that the 
electrode exhibits typical EDLC behavior, as indicated by quasi-rectangular CV curves at scan rates of 5–
100 mV·s⁻¹, low polarization with minimal IR drop, and stable charge–discharge profiles. The electrode 
also demonstrated excellent cycling stability, retaining 96%–98% of its capacitance after 100 charge–
discharge cycles at a current density of 3 A·g⁻¹, indicating strong durability and resistance to repeated 
electrochemical cycling. 
Regarding CDI desalination performance, the electrode effectively removed ions under various feed 
operating conditions. For a feed conductivity of ~1,050 µS·cm⁻¹, the effluent conductivity decreased to 
~840 µS·cm⁻¹ at an applied voltage of 0.8 V and to ~630 µS·cm⁻¹ at 1.0 V after 120 min of operation. 
Additionally, the electrode exhibited strong electrosorption capability, achieving an SAC of ~38 mg·g⁻¹ at 
a salinity of 500 ppm, along with favorable adsorption kinetics characterized by an SAR of 1.3–1.4 
mg·g⁻¹min⁻¹ at the SAC of 35–38 mg·g⁻¹. Further, flow-rate experiments conducted at 1.2 V demonstrated 
the operational flexibility of the CDI system, with an optimal flow rate near 10 mL·min−¹, corresponding to 
effluent conductivity values of 630–640 µS·cm⁻¹. This behavior reflected the balance between sufficient 
residence time and enhanced mass transfer within the electrode structure. Overall, the results indicated 
that date-palm-pollen-derived activated carbon possesses an exceptionally large surface area as well as 
exhibits stable capacitive behavior and high CDI electrosorption performance. Consequently, date-palm-
pollen-derived activated carbon represents a promising, low-cost electrode material for the desalination 
of brackish water, while supporting circular economy principles through the utilization of agricultural 
byproducts.
