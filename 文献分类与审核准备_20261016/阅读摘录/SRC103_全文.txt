ABSTRACT
Catalytic graphitization of biomass-derived carbon offers a promising route to produce biographite as a sustainable alternative to
petroleum-based synthetic graphite for lithium-ion battery (LIB) anodes. This study investigates the physicochemical properties of
biocokes produced from pyrolysis oil at carbonization temperatures ranging from 150°C to 500°C. Using an iron (Fe) catalyst,
graphitization was performed at 1500°C, significantly lower than the /C243000°C required for conventional synthetic graphite. The
effects of introducing an intermediate-temperature hold (400°C –600°C) prior to graphitization were evaluated, simulating a
“delayed coking ” process to enable the coproduction of sustainable aviation fuels (SAFs). Chemical structure evolution during
biocoke formation was analyzed, and proposed mechanisms are presented. Biographites produced via the delayed coking pathway
exhibited high crystallinity and excellent electrochemical performance in both half-cell and full-cell LIB configurations. The full
cells exhibited an initial discharge capacity close to the theoretical capacity of the NMC622 cathode (175 mAh/g at 4.2 V), and high
capacity retention (/C2488%) after 150 cycles. Notably, the graphitic and electrochemical properties remained stable across the range
of intermediate hold temperatures. These findings provide a foundation for optimizing temperature parameters in delayed coking
systems to enable scalable, integrated production of biographite and SAFs from pyrolysis oil.
1 | Introduction
Rechargeable lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) were developed and
commercialized as high-performing and cost-effective energy stor-
age systems. Since commercialization in 1991, LIBs have revolu-
tionized the electrochemical energy storage technology owing to
their high energy density, light-weight nature, small size, high
Coulombic efficiency (CE), long cycle life, good rate performance,
high working voltage, minimal self-discharge, negligible memory
effect, etc [ 1–5]. The LIBs are extensively used in portable
electronic devices, grid energy storage, and electric vehicles
(EVs). The EVs, powered by LIBs, can achieve longer driving
ranges and are transforming the automotive sector by offering a
sustainable, environmentally friendly mode of transportation with
lower carbon emissions [ 6]. In 2023, almost 14 million EVs were
sold worldwide, with a 35% year-on-year increase, indicating the
rapid adoption and growing confidence in EVs as a sustainable
transportation solution [ 7]. Due to the increased popularity of
EVs, the demand for LIBs is increasing exponentially.

Among the various essential components in LIBs, the active mate-
rials are most widely studied, as they are responsible for the revers-
ible intercalation and deintercalation of lithium ions (i.e., Li+)[ 8].
Commercial LIBs use graphite as the anode active material,
together with a binder and conductive carbon. Graphite is pre-
ferred because of its high gravimetric capacity (theoretical maxi-
mum /C24372 mAh/g), low delithiation potential (/C250.2 V vs. Li/Li+),
high CE, extended plateau capacity, long cyclability, good rate per-
formance, low voltage hysteresis, cost effectiveness, environmental
friendliness, etc [ 9–15]. Both natural and synthetic graphites are
used in the commercial LIB cells. Natural graphite is extracted
from geographically constrained natural reserves. Mining, refin-
ing, and beneficiation of natural graphite require substantial
energy and lead to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. These pro-
cesses are also associated with the generation of mining dust and
the utilization of toxic hydrofluoric acid [16]. Compared to natural
graphite, synthetic graphite offers greater consistency and tunabil-
ity. Thermal treatments of soft carbon precursors (e.g., petroleum
coke) at very high temperatures (/C243000°C) make synthetic graph-
ite. It is worth noting that graphite is considered a critical material,
and synthetic graphite has the potential to provide a secure and
reliable supply chain for graphite in LIBs. However, the produc-
tion of synthetic graphite involves nonrenewable feedstocks and
emits GHG. Additionally, the process is energy-intensive (i.e.,
costly) and takes longer times [ 17].
Catalytic graphitization of renewable biocarbon is an emerging
alternative approach to sustainably produce anode-grade biogra-
phite for LIBs at low temperature and shorter time, overcoming
the key limitations of synthetic graphite [ 18–21]. Group VIII
transition metals, e.g., iron [ 22], cobalt [ 23], or nickel [ 24],
can catalyze the graphitization process via a dissolution-
precipitation mechanism [22] and/or a formation-decomposition
mechanism [25]. Among these transition metals, iron-based cat-
alysts are mostly used because of relatively higher carbon solu-
bility in iron, d-orbital electron vacancies, availability, low cost,
etc [26, 27]. Although iron can be used in many different forms to
catalyze the graphitization process, reduced metallic iron powder
is preferred over other available forms such as salts, oxides, orga-
nometallics, etc [ 13].
Feedstock selection is another key variable to consider for scal-
able production of biographite [ 18, 28]. To date, various biocar-
bon feedstocks have been catalytically graphitized such as
softwood [20], hardwood [ 29], glucose [ 30], lignin [ 23], lignocel-
lulose [ 31], cellulose [ 32], hemicellulose [ 33], biochar [ 34],
bagasse [ 35], coconut coir [ 36], corn straw [ 37], waste coffee
grounds [ 38], passion fruit shells [ 39], olive pomace [ 40], beech
[41], fiberboard [ 12], paper towel [ 19], etc. An emerging feed-
stock for catalytic graphitization is pyrolysis oil (also known
as bio-oil), which is derived from the fast pyrolysis of biomass.
Given the scalable production of biographite, pyrolysis oil offers
several key advantages, including low inorganic content, high
carbon content, and easier handling and transportation [ 42–44].
Our recent study explored the mechanism of iron-catalyzed
graphitization of pyrolysis oil. Catalyst loading was optimized,
and the effects of temperature and pyrolysis oil aging were also
studied. The optimal biographite demonstrated promising perfor-
mance as anode active material in LIB half-cells [ 22]. However,
the process ’s scalability was challenged by several factors. Our
follow-up work addressed the key challenges in processing
pyrolysis oil into LIB-grade graphite via iron catalysis at scale,
including viscosity, iron-induced foaming, swelling, and aging.
To address these challenges, five pathways were explored, includ-
ing (i) defoamer use, (ii) use of iron oxide (Fe 2O3) as graphitiza-
tion catalyst, (iii) pH adjustment of pyrolysis oil, (iv) pyrolysis oil
coking at 300°C–500°C, and (v) thermal pretreatment of pyrolysis
oil at 150°C –200°C. The low-temperature pretreatment pathway
successfully addressed the aforementioned challenges and pro-
duced highly crystalline biographites showing impressive electro-
chemical performance in LIB half-cells [ 45].
The objective of this study was to thoroughly characterize the
physicochemical properties of products obtained via pretreat-
ment and coking of pyrolysis oil at 150, 200, 300, 400, and
500°C under inert nitrogen flow and without iron catalyst.
For simplification, the products obtained at five temperatures
are called biocokes, hereafter, and named BC-150, BC-200,
BC-300, BC-400, and BC-500, respectively. Biographites were
made from BC-150 at 1500°C using iron catalyst following an
intermediate hold step (i.e., delayed coking) at three different
temperatures (400, 500, and 600°C) for 1 h. The biographites
were purified via acid refluxing and named BG-400, BG-500,
and BG-600, hereafter. The electrochemical performance of
these biographites was tested as anode active materials in
LIB half-cells and full-cells.
2 | Experimental Section
2.1 | Materials
This study utilized pyrolysis oil, collected from Ensyn Technologies
Inc. (Ontario, Canada), as feedstock for the preparation of biocokes
and biographites. The oil was produced through fast pyrolysis of
woody biomass and had a dark brown opaque appearance. The spe-
cific gravity of the supplied pyrolysis oil sample was 1.15 –1.25 at
20°C, and the pH was 3.2. Reduced iron in powder form (particle
size < 10 μm; 99.9% trace metals basis; Sigma–Aldrich) was used as
a catalyst for the graphitization of biocoke. Hydrochloric acid
(HCl, 37% w/w; Fisher Scientific) was applied to chemically separate
residual iron from the product after graphitization. Conductive
carbon black (C45, Timcal), styr ene-butadiene rubber (SBR, MSE
Supplies LLC), carboxymethyl c ellulose (CMC, MSE Supplies
LLC), and Milli-Q water were used to prepare the electrode. The
electrolyte for half-cell testing was purchased from Tomiyama
Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd, and comprised 1.2 M lithium hexa-
fluorophosphate (LiPF6) in a 3:7 weight ratio of ethylene carbonate
(EC)/ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC).
2.2 | Preparation of Biocokes
Biocokes were prepared as described in our previous work [ 45].
Briefly, the raw pyrolysis oil was heated at five different temper-
atures, i.e., 150°C (5 h), 200°C (1 h), 300°C (1 h), 400°C (1 h), and
500°C (1 h). The experiments were conducted under nitrogen flow
(100 mL/min) to ensure an inert atmosphere. Figure 1a–e repre-
sents the experimental conditions (temperature, hold time, heat-
ing rate, and cooling rate) used for biocoke preparation. The raw
pyrolysis oil did not solidify into biocoke when pretreated at 100°C
for an extended period (12 h), and therefore was not considered for
further study.

2.3 | Catalytic Graphitization of Biocoke
As reported in our recent work, a low-temperature pretreatment
(150°C–200°C) of pyrolysis oil was very effective for scalable pro-
duction of biographite using reduced iron catalyst [ 45]. The pres-
ent study considered BC-150 only for catalytic graphitization.
Before mixing with iron, BC-150 was ground into fine powder
in a mortar. The iron loading was kept at 2.5 times the fixed car-
bon content of the pyrolysis oil. The readers are referred to our
previous report that strategically explored the effect of iron load-
ing on the catalytic graphitization of pyrolysis oil [ 22]. The BC-
150 and iron were dry-mixed and loaded on an alumina boat
before graphitization in a tube furnace (GSL-1500X, MTI
Corporation, Richmond, California, USA) under nitrogen atmo-
sphere (flow rate: 100 mL/min). The graphitization was per-
formed at 1500°C for /C2424 h, with a heating and cooling rate
of 4°C/min below 600°C and 2°C/min above 600°C. The mixture
of BC-150 and iron powder was held at three different interme-
diate temperatures (400°C, 500°C, and 600°C) for 1 h. Figure 1f–h
schematically demonstrates the temperature profile for biogra-
phite preparation using iron catalyst.
2.4 | Removal of Iron via Acid Refluxing
The catalytic graphitization transformed the biocoke into biogra-
phite with the help of iron; however, the removal of iron from
biographite was essential, considering the anode application of
biographite in LIBs. The solid mixture of biographite and iron
was powdered before refluxing with HCl for 3 h. The size reduc-
tion step (i.e., milling) helped efficiently isolate the iron from
biographite as acid-soluble iron chloride. To prepare high-purity
biographite, acid refluxing was continued 5 times, using fresh
HCl in each step. After acid refluxing, biographite was recovered
from the acid via vacuum filtration. The biographite powder
(BG-400, BG-500, and BG-600) was dried at 105°C for 4 h before
further study.

3.1.2 | Biocoke Color
The coking temperature determined the color of the biocokes, as
depicted in Figure 2b. The physical appearance of BC-150 and BC-
200 was similar when cooled to room temperature. Both of these
biocokes showed a moderate degree of swelling (formation of an
expanded and perforated solid coke structure) [45]. These biocokes
(BC-150 and BC-200) were easily crushed into brown colored pow-
der using a mortar. It is noted that the brown color of the biocoke
slightly faded in the case of BC-200. On the other hand, BC-300,
BC-400, and BC-500 showed a comparatively higher degree of
swelling after cooling to room temperature. The rapid release of
volatiles at higher coking temperatures could be correlated with
the highly swelled structure of these biocokes. These three biocokes
had a black appearance as opposed to the brown color of BC-150
and BC-200. The change of color from brown (BC-150 and BC-200)
to black (BC-300, BC-400, and BC-500) indicates the formation of
biocokes with successively higher proportions of carbon.
3.1.3 | SEM-EDS Analysis
The surface morphological features of the biocokes were evalu-
ated by recording the SEM microphotographs, as shown in the
top two rows in Figure 3. The BC-150 and BC-200 biocokes dem-
onstrated similar morphology with a heterogeneous distribution
of the micron-sized particles. The edges of the particles seemed
smooth. The morphology of the other three biocokes differed
from that of BC-150 and BC-200. Interestingly, BC-300, BC-
400, and BC-500 biocokes showed nearly identical morphology
and a homogeneous distribution of particle sizes in the micron
range. The edges of the particles looked sharper. It is worth not-
ing that very few pores were visually detected on the SEM image
of all five biocokes.
To explore the elemental distribution on the surfaces of the bio-
cokes, EDS maps were recorded. As demonstrated in the third
and fourth rows of Figure 3, the biocokes were mainly composed
of carbon and oxygen, with the dominant presence of carbon,
especially for samples prepared at higher coking temperatures.

3.1.5 | Particle Size Analysis
The particle size distribution of biocokes is critical, as it controls
the physical mixing with powder catalyst. Figure 4 shows the par-
ticle size distribution of the five biocokes, and the corresponding
Dv10, Dv50, and Dv90 values are summarized in Table S2. The
BC-150 and BC-200 samples showed nearly identical particle size
distributions, with relatively smaller particles. Biocoke, having a
smaller particle size, possesses relatively more available surface
for interaction with the catalyst powder. However, the Dv10,
Dv50, and Dv90 increased progressively as the coking tempera-
ture increased. For example, the Dv50 of BC-150 was 49.46 μm,
whereas for BC-500, it was 283.3 μm. A poor coke-catalyst mixing
was observed for BC-300, BC-400, and BC-500 as they had con-
siderably larger particle sizes and lower available surface to inter-
act with the catalyst. In addition to the larger particle size, it was
also very difficult to mill these coke samples (BC-300, BC-400,
and BC-500), which ultimately resulted in the formation of
poor-quality biographite [ 45].
3.1.6 | Elemental Analysis
Chemical analysis quantified the distribution of key elements
(C, H, N, and O) in the prepared biocokes. The results of the ele-
mental analysis are displayed in Table S3. The biocoke samples
showed significant changes in elemental distribution with
increasing coking temperature. Carbon was found to be predom-
inantly present in all the biocoke samples, and its content
increased with coking temperature due to the removal of hydro-
gen and oxygen via dehydration, decarboxylation, and dehydro-
genation reactions. Both hydrogen and oxygen content decreased
with temperature, leading to a decline in H/C and O/C ratios.
Hydrogen and oxygen are released during coking as H 2O,
CO2, CO, etc., resulting in a condensed aromatic structure of bio-
coke. The nitrogen content in biocoke increased progressively
with temperature. This is because nitrogen is often lost during
the carbonization of biomass at relatively higher temperatures
as volatile compounds. Moreover, nitrogen could be incorporated
into the stable heterocyclic aromatic structure of biocoke during
the coking process. While the oxygen-rich light components are
evaporated, the remaining heavier components polymerize into
solid biocoke [ 46].
3.1.7 | XRD Analysis
The processing temperature has a profound impact on the crys-
talline structure, i.e., hierarchical ordering of carbon materials.
The nanocrystalline graphitic domains are strongly influenced
by the carbonization temperature. The crystallinity of biocokes
was assessed by applying the XRD technique at room tempera-
ture. The recorded XRD patterns of the biocokes are shown in
Figure 5a. All the biocokes exhibited broad diffraction patterns
over a range of 2 θ values. The broad nature of the peaks suggests
predominantly amorphous carbon with very limited crystallinity.
In general, the characteristic (002) and (100) peaks of graphitic
carbon appear clearly when the coking is performed at a rela-
tively higher temperature. For example, calcined coke made from
the residue of atmospheric and vacuum distillation of pyrolysis
oil at 1200°C showed both the (002) and (100) peaks [ 47]. The
coking experiment in this study was performed at 500°C or lower.
As a result, the long-range ordering was not formed in the bio-
cokes, which is evident from the absence of the (100) peak and
the presence of a very broad (002) peak. The coking
temperature (150°C –500°C) led to the volatilization of low-
molecular-weight compounds from pyrolysis oil, resulting in
increased carbon content in the biocokes. However, the position
of the (002) peak shifted to higher 2 θ diffraction angles with an
increase in coking temperature, as indicated by the arrow. This
observation supports the formation of biocokes containing high-
molecular-weight polyaromatic hydrocarbons. Considering the
broader diffraction patterns of the biocokes, the peak fitting tech-
nique was not applied to estimate the characteristic parameters
such as crystallite size ( Lc) and interlayer distance (d 002) follow-
ing the Debye Scherrer equation and Bragg ’s law.

3.1.8 | FTIR Analysis
The FTIR spectroscopy technique was applied to study the surface
chemical functionality and carbon skeleton structure of biocokes
made at different temperatures. The heat treatment considerably
influenced the quantity and distribution of surface functional groups
in biocokes, shown in Figure 5b. The FTIR spectrum of biocoke
made at 150°C, i.e., BC-150, identified numerous chemical moieties
including –OH (3361 cm − 1), aliphatic C –H (2934 cm − 1), C = O
(1696 cm− 1), C = C (1603 cm − 1, 1514 cm − 1), C –H (1452 cm − 1),
and C-O (1201 cm− 1, 1045 cm− 1). The coking temperature (150°C)
was slightly above the atmospheri c drying temperature of pyrol-
ysis oil; therefore, the BC-150 bi ocoke closely resembled the raw
pyrolysis oil feedstock in terms of chemical composition except
for the water and some other volatile compounds that were released
during coking. As understood from Figure 5b, intensities of these
characteristic absorption bands decreased gradually with an
increase in coking temperature, indicating a series of processes
including further removal of the volatile organic compounds, cleav-
age of oxygen-containing groups, cleavage of alkyl side chains, ring
opening of oxygen-containing groups, etc. These chemical reactions
further accelerated the condensation and polymerization of the car-
bon backbone [48]. At the higher coking temperatures (BC-400, BC-
500), most of the FTIR signals disappeared. The BC-400 and BC-500
biocokes exhibited a low-intensity, broad absorption peak at
1560 cm− 1, characteristic of the unsaturated C= Cb o n do fa na r o -
matic ring. It is to be noted that the aromatic ring carbon skeleton is
not highly IR active. The presence of only unsaturated C= Cb o n d s
in BC-400 and BC-500 indicates homogenization of chemical com-
position and structure, coupled with enhanced restriction of bond
vibrations due to structural order via aromatization [47]. This obser-
vation is strongly supported by the high carbon content of the bio-
cokes at higher coking temperature (Table S3). Based on the FTIR
analysis, it is strongly emphasized that heat treatment not only
helped eliminate volatiles but also accelerated molecular transfor-
mation, leading to the formation of solidified biocokes.
3.1.9 | XPS Analysis
The XPS technique examines the elemental composition and
chemical bonding state of a sample’s surface by irradiating it with
X-rays and measuring the kinetic energy of the emitted photoelec-
trons. The XPS spectra of the biocokes were recorded to investigate
their surface chemistry. The low-resolution survey spectra, shown
in Figure S2, indicate the presence of C 1s and O 1s on the surface
of biocokes; however, their relative proportion varied as listed in
the inserted table in Figure 6. The carbon content increased with
coking temperature while the oxygen content decreased. The BC-
150 biocoke had 72.4% C 1s and 27.6% O 1s, while BC-500 showed
90.3% C 1s and only 9.7% O 1s, indicating a greater degree of car-
bonization in BC-500 than in BC-150.
To further explore the nature of chemical bonding, i.e., the chem-
ical states of carbon in biocokes, C 1s peaks were fitted, i.e.,
deconvoluted. The results of the XPS analysis after peak fitting
are summarized in Figure 6. The peak fitting identified different
types of chemical bonding in biocokes. The relative proportion of
these chemical species is listed in Table S4. The C –C and C –H
bonds were predominantly present in all the samples, and their
relative proportion increased with coking temperature. The pro-
portion of C –OH (alcohol) and C –O–C (ether) chemical bonds
decreased significantly with coking, indicating the removal of
oxygen with temperature. The carbonyl (C = O) and ester
(O–C=O) functional groups were present in relatively lower per-
centages compared to the aforementioned species; however, their
proportion also varied with an increase in coking temperature.

3.2 | Mechanism of Biocoke Formation
Biocoke is a solid carbonaceous material that inevitably forms
during the chemical and/or thermal upgradation of pyrolysis
oil [ 49]. For the preparation of carbon materials from pyrolysis
oil, coking was performed in this study. For other thermal upgra-
dation processes of pyrolysis oil, the formation of coke presents
safety and economic challenges, including catalyst fouling/deac-
tivation, thermal reactor clogging, and diminished energy effi-
ciency. Yet, little is known about the mechanism of biocoke
formation during the heating of pyrolysis oil. Biocoke can be
formed by thermally treating pyrolysis oil over a broad tempera-
ture range [ 50]. Cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin-derived
components actively participate in the formation of biocoke.
Interactions among the components present in pyrolysis oil play
a key role in defining the properties of biocoke, including yield.
Both the water-soluble and water-insoluble fractions in pyrolysis
oil can form coke at low temperatures. The water-soluble fraction
includes alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, anhydro-
sugars, lignin monomers, etc. The water-insoluble fraction con-
tains lignin-derived oligomers having larger aromatic ring
systems [ 51–54]. Pyrolysis oil is extremely complex in terms of
chemical composition, and some of the functional groups are
exceedingly reactive at room temperature, e.g., sugars, aldehydes,
carboxylic acids, etc [ 50]. These chemical compounds become
more activated when pyrolysis oil is heated and undergoes
decomposition, chemical interaction with each other, and poly-
merization, leading to the formation of biocoke. Wang et al.
found that levoglucosan, furans, and cyclic ketones in the
water-soluble fraction formed biocoke at a temperature range
of 250°C –450°C [ 50]. It is to be noted that levoglucosan is the
single most abundant chemical compound in pyrolysis oil (6 –
7 wt%). The authors defined coke as the solid residue insoluble
in CHCl3:CH3OH (80:20 v/v) mixture. In addition to the inherent
chemical properties of pyrolysis oil including, ash, other factors
potentially influence the biocoke formation, such as temperature,
heating rate, reaction atmosphere, atmosphere pressure, etc [ 49].
With increasing temperature, the degree of aromatization
increases, leading to the formation of biocoke with a more con-
densed structure and lower O/C and H/C ratios. Several studies
have reported that solid biocoke forms from liquid pyrolysis oil
due to the polymerization of chemical compounds via free radical
reactions [ 55–57]. The free radicals are generated from the rup-
ture of covalent bonds upon the action of heat, and the concen-
tration of free radicals increases with temperature. Two types of
free radicals are involved during the coking process: (i) long-lived
stable free radicals and (ii) short-lived transient/active free rad-
icals. The stable free radicals can be monitored in-situ using con-
tinuous wave electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR)
spectroscopy. However, due to the short lifetimes of transient free
radicals (microseconds), it is difficult to detect them by EPR, as
the time resolution of continuous-wave EPR is milliseconds. A
large number of active free radicals are annihilated during the
process. One possible strategy to capture the active free radicals
is the use of 9,10-dihydrophenanthrene (DHP) as a hydrogen
donor and then measuring the hydrogen donation reaction of
DHP [56]. During the early stage of coking, there is an induction
period during which lighter components progressively polymer-
ize into heavier components, encompassing the critical coke pre-
cursors, i.e., polycyclic aromatics, which dissolve and accumulate
in pyrolysis oil [ 55]. When the coke precursors in pyrolysis oil
reach their solubility threshold, they precipitate and polymerize
to form coke. Certain transient free radicals are incorporated into
macromolecular coke products and transformed into stable free
radicals. These stable free radicals are then confined/trapped by
the steric hindrance of nascent coke macromolecules with small
aromatic structures, thereby prolonging their lifespans and
reducing their reactivity. During the later stage, the coke precur-
sors are exhausted, and the coke structure undergoes thermal
condensation, resulting in the formation of larger aromatic

3.3 | Catalytic Graphitization of Biocokes via
Delayed Coking
Considering the economic aspects of the overall process, it is
essential to capture volatiles before graphitization, as they
can be further processed into sustainable aviation fuels
(SAFs). To capture volatiles, an intermediate hold step is
required, which needs to be performed in a separate unit.
The idea is similar to delayed coking as applied to petroleum
oil upgrading. Delayed coking is a thermal cracking process that
converts low-value residues from the atmospheric and vacuum
distillation of crude petroleum oil into high-value products such
as petroleum coke, lighter gases, naphtha, gasoline, diesel,
heavy gas oils, etc [ 58–61]. The process is typically performed
at a temperature of /C24500°C under a low-pressure, low-oxygen
environment with hydrocarbon vapors acting as the primary
atmosphere inside the coke drum. Depending on the feedstock
and process conditions, three different types of cokes can be
formed, namely shot coke, sponge coke, and needle coke.
The green needle coke (GNC) has a needle-like long structure
and is produced from low-sulfur and highly aromatic feed-
stocks. The GNC can be calcined at 1000°C –1500°C to remove
remaining volatile materials and enhance carbon purity [ 62]. The
calcined needle coke is processed at /C242800°C into anode-grade
synthetic graphite for LIBs [ 63]. This study aimed to explore the
effects of delayed coking at three intermediate temperatures on
the physicochemical and electrochemical performance of BG-400,
BG-500, and BG-600 biographites. The capture and processing of
volatiles into SAFs are outside the scope of this study and will
be available in future reports.

3.3.2 | Raman Analysis
Raman spectroscopy is highly sensitive to structural disorder in
graphitic materials. The typical Raman spectrum of graphite
exhibits three peaks at /C241350, 1580, and 2700 cm − 1, correspond-
ing to the D-band, G-band, and 2D-band, respectively. The
D-band emerges due to the out-of-plane defects (sp 3 carbon
vibrations), while G-band is indicative of in-plane order (sp 2 car-
bon vibrations) [23]. The 2D-band is sensitive to stacking and the
number of graphene layers. The Raman spectra of BG-400, BG-
500, and BG-600 are displayed in Figure 10 (bottom row). The
Raman spectra were averaged over 10 scans. It is worth empha-
sizing that Raman spectroscopy analyzes a small sample and can
provide spatially resolved information, whereas XRD is a bulk
characterization technique that analyzes a relatively larger sam-
ple. Table 1 lists various parameters obtained from the Raman
spectra analysis. In particular, the intensity ratio of the D-band
to G-band, i.e., ID/IG, was used to assess the degree of disorder in
graphitic materials. The ID/IG values of BG-400, BG-500, and BG-
600 were calculated to be 0.08, 0.12, and 0.09, indicating a very
low degree of disorder. The degree of graphitization factor ( α) for
BG-400, BG-500, and BG-600 was estimated as 92, 89, and 91%,
respectively. The lower ID/IG and higher α values support the
higher degree of graphitization in the three biographites. The
intensity ratio of 2D-band to G-band, i.e., I2D/IG, was calculated
to investigate the multilayer graphene sheets in the biographites
(monolayer: I2D/IG /C242–3, bilayer: 1 <I2D/IG < 2, multilayer:
I2D/IG < 1) [ 23]. The I2D/IG values for BG-400, BG-500, and
BG-600 were estimated at 0.41, 0.43, and 0.41, respectively, indi-
cating that the biographites had multilayer structures.

3.3.4 | HR-TEM Analysis
The HR-TEM images were recorded to visualize the evolution of
graphitic crystalline structures. Figure 11 (bottom row) shows
the HR-TEM photographs of biographites. The photos illustrate thin
overlapping sheets and layers typical of graphitic structures featur-
ing well-aligned planes. As visible in the images, the experimental
conditions maintained during the c atalytic graphitization process
ensured the formation of close ly spaced graphene layers with
low interlayer spacing (d 002). This observation is supported by
the low d 002 values calculated from the XRD data shown in
Table 1. The HR-TEM images also reveal significant growth of
the graphene sheets along the c-direction, giving rise to high stack-
ing height ( Lc) and a-direction offering high lateral size ( La). The

long-range ordering, suggests high crystallinity of the biographites.
The SAED patterns reveal bright concentric diffraction rings, indi-

kled domains, suggestive of partially ordered structures, was not
prominent in the HR-TEM images. Based on the investigation of
the well-defined lattice fringes demonstrated in HR-TEM images,
it can be stated that all three biographites (BG-400, BG-500, and
BG-600) were formed with high crystallinity. Therefore, the varia-
tion in delayed coking temperature had a negligible effect on the
crystallinity of the biographites.
3.3.5 | Particle Size Analysis
The electrochemical performance of a battery is strongly influ-
enced by the particle size and particle size distribution of the
electrode active materials [ 64]. All the important performance
matrices, including capacity, discharge/charge behavior,
cycling performance, C rates, CE, etc., are influenced by the par-
ticle size distribution of the active material. A narrow particle
distribution yields superior cell performance compared to
broader distributions. Nonetheless, particle size reduction has
limitations, as excessively small particles can adversely affect
performance. Moreover, regardless of particle size distribution,
the presence of coarse particles is observed to facilitate lithium
plating, thereby diminishing cell efficiency and compromising
battery safety. In this study, the particle size distribution of biog-
raphites was determined with LD method. As listed in Table S2,
the average Dv10, Dv50, and Dv90 were 12.94 μm, 49.09 μm, and
98.74 μm for BG-400; 12.93 μm, 43.57 μm, and 68.86 μmf o r
BG-500; and 14.44 μm, 52.12 μm, and 99.55 μm for BG-600.
The histograms shown in Figure 12 indicate that the particle
size distribution of BG-400 and BG-600 is nearly similar and
broad, while the BG-500 sample shows a relatively narrower
particle size distribution.

4 | Conclusion
This study systematically investigated the physicochemical
characteristics of pyrolysis oil-derived biocokes and their con-
version to high-performance biographites via catalytic graphiti-
zation. Biocokes produced at varying coking temperatures
(150°C–500°C) exhibited significant changes in elemental com-
position, morphology, thermal stability, and carbon structure,
which were comprehensively characterized using numerous
analytical techniques. The biocoke formed at 150°C was identi-
fied as the most suitable precursor for graphitization due to its
favorable particle size, surface morphology, and ease of catalyst
integration. Catalytic graphitization of BC-150 at 1500°C, incor-
porating an intermediate ‘delayed coking ’ step at 400°C –600°C,
produced highly crystalline biographites with low surface area
and excellent purity —properties that are essential for anode
materials in LIBs. Importantly, t he intermediate hold tempera-
ture did not cause wide variation in the crystallinity, structure,
and electrochemical performance of the resulting biographites.
All samples demonstrated strong performance in both half-cell
and full-cell LIB configurations. These findings offer a promis-
ing pathway for the scalable, low-temperature production of
biographite from pyrolysis oil. The incorporation of a delayed
coking step enables simultaneous recovery of condensable
vapors—potential feedstocks for SAF production —positioning
this approach as a compelling biorefinery strategy. Future work
should focus on integrating SAF synthesis into the biographite
production system and on conducting detailed techno-
economic and life-cycle assessments to evaluate commercial
feasibility and environm ental benefits at scale.
