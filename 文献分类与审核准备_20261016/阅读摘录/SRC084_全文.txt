Introduction
With the rapid advancement of biomedical technology, the 
demand for simulating and intervening in biological sig -
nals has been growing exponentially [ 1–3]. As a crucial 
component of biological signaling, neural signal trans -
mission presents higher requirements for bionic electro -
chemical devices due to its complexity and rapid response 
characteristics. SCs [ 4], renowned for their fast charging-
discharging capabilities and high power density, share 
similarities with neurons' rapid-response mechanisms 
[5], making them a focal point in bionic electrochemical 
device research. Applying SCs to biological signal simu -
lation—particularly neural signal transmission—not only 
drives progress in neural interface technology [ 6] but also 
provides innovative solutions for biomedical equipment 
[7].

potential of SCs in biosignal simulation [ 8], with a par -

Abstract
This review examines the potential of supercapacitors (SCs) in simulating biological signals, with a particular focus 
on neural signaling applications. Owing to their rapid charge–discharge kinetics and high power density, SCs exhibit 
functional parallels with neuronal response mechanisms, positioning them as promising candidates for bioinspired elec -
trochemical devices. The paper begins by outlining the fundamental principles, material architectures, and performance 
characteristics of SCs. Subsequently, it establishes the theoretical framework for biological signal simulation, encom -
passing neural signal propagation mechanisms and the properties of bioelectrical signals. The core discussion centers 
on supercapacitor-based neural signal simulation, addressing the similarities in dynamic behavior with neuronal activity, 
supported by experimental findings and computational modeling. Current technological limitations, including material 
optimization, structural design, and biocompatibility challenges, are critically analyzed alongside potential solutions. The 
review concludes by highlighting emerging applications in neural interfaces and biomedical devices, while identifying key 
research directions for future investigation. This synthesis highlights the distinct advantages of SCs in bioelectronic signal 
emulation and underscores the need for continued exploration in this interdisciplinary field.

Overview of SCs
Basic Principle of SCs
SCs, also known as electrochemical capacitors, realize 
energy storage through the electric double layer effect and 
the Faraday pseudocapacitance effect (Fig. 1a–c) [13]. The 
basic principles include the following two aspects:
Electric Double Layer
The electric double layer (EDL), a well-established fun -
damental in electrochemistry, arises from physical charge 
adsorption at the electrode–electrolyte interface, enabling 
rapid, non-Faradaic energy storage [ 14]. The formation 
of EDL primarily depends on the electrode materials' 
high specific surface area and good electrical conductiv -
ity. Electrode materials with high specific surface area 
provide more active sites, thereby enhancing the charge 
storage capacity of EDLs [ 15]. Simultaneously, excellent 
electrical conductivity ensures efficient charge transport 
within the electrode, which is essential for achieving rapid 
energy cycling. The physical adsorption characteristics of 
EDL imply that its charge storage mechanism differs from 
chemical reactions in batteries [ 16]. In EDLCs, charges 
are stored through physical adsorption on ions on the elec -
trode surface—a highly reversible process enabling nearly 
infinite charge–discharge cycles. Additionally, the thick -
ness and ion distribution of EDLs influence their interac -
tion with external electric fields, affecting phenomena like 
Joule heating (Fig. 2a–c) [ 17]. The Poisson-Boltzmann 
equation forms the theoretical basis for describing EDLs, 
with advanced models like the fractional-space general -
ization accounting for spatial complexity (Fig. S1e and f) 
[18]. The electric double layer effect not only holds signifi-
cant applications in energy storage but also shows promis -
ing potential in thermal management and nanoscale heat 
transfer [16]. For instance, in studies investigating extreme 
near-field heat transfer, the influence of the electric double 
layer on heat flux at metal surfaces was examined (Fig. 
S2a and d) [ 20], revealing that the contribution of the 
electric double layer to heat flow far exceeds predictions 
from traditional heat transfer theories [ 21]. Therefore, 
the electric double layer effect is a crucial phenomenon 
in electrochemical and energy science, which relies on 
electrode materials' high specific surface area and excel -
lent conductivity. The physical mechanisms of the electric 
double layer are being thoroughly understood so that the 
performance of energy storage and conversion devices can 
be further optimized [ 22].
Faraday Pseudocapacitance Effect
The Faraday pseudocapacitance effect is another key 
charge storage mechanism in supercapacitors (SCs), 
involving fast, reversible surface redox reactions that pro -
vide higher energy density than pure EDL mechanisms 
(Fig. 3a–e) [ 24]. Compared to traditional double-layer 
capacitors, pseudocapacitors exhibit higher energy density 
and power density, making them particularly effective for 
applications that require fast charging/discharging, such 
as electric vehicle start-stop energy recovery and rapid 
response requirements in biosignal simulation [ 25]. The 
energy storage performance of SCs relies on both double-
layer capacitance and/or pseudocapacitance [ 26], with the 
latter stemming from rapid reversible surface redox reac -
tions. MOFs, emerging electrode materials, have garnered 
attention due to their superior capacitance performance 
compared to conventional materials. However, MOF-
based SCs still face practical challenges, including low 
potential windows, limited cycle life, and poor rate per -
formance [27].
Nevertheless, MOFs' unique and tunable chemical 
structures offer multiple pathways to enhance supercapaci -
tor performance [ 28]. This fast surface-redox process is 
crucial for high-power applications. For instance, Nb 2O5 
electrodes demonstrate the ability to retain pseudocapaci -
tive properties of nanoscale materials. They exhibit no 
half-infinite diffusion limitation even during extremely 
short charging times (within one minute), achieving a high 
specific capacitance of 380 F/g and an area capacitance of 
0.46 F/cm2. This indicates that optimizing electrode mate -
rials and structures can significantly enhance the energy 
density and power density of SCs [ 30]. Furthermore, 
mathematical simulations and numerical modeling analyz -
ing interactions between electrode materials and charge

Structure and Materials
The performance of SCs is primarily determined by their 
structural design and material composition, with key com -
ponents including electrode materials and electrolytes [ 37, 
38]. The selection and optimization of electrode materials 
are crucial for enhancing the electrochemical performance 
of SCs [ 39]. In recent years, researchers have developed 
various novel electrode materials such as carbon-based 
materials, metal oxides, and conductive polymers. These 
materials are widely used in SCs due to their high specific 
surface area, excellent electrical conductivity, and inher -
ent stability [ 40]. Notably, nanostructured materials such 
as carbon nanotubes and graphene have garnered attention 
for their superior electrochemical properties [ 41]. Electro-
lytes are another critical factor affecting supercapacitor 
performance [ 42], as they not only provide ionic con -
ductivity but also influence capacity, power density, rate 
capability, and cycle life [ 43] The physical and chemical 
Pseudocapacitance (Faradaic Reaction) : Pseudoca -
pacitance involves electron transfer across the electrode–
electrolyte interface, coupled with the electroadsorption of 
ions or reversible redox reactions [ 24]. While these reac -
tions are often exceptionally fast and appear capacitive, 
they are intrinsically limited by the kinetics of the elec -
trochemical reaction  and ion diffusion within the bulk of 
the material [ 25]. As a result, their useful frequency range 
may be limited to tens or hundreds of Hertz. This could 
potentially distort the very high-frequency components of 
neural signals or fail to replicate rapid, successive spikes 
accurately [26].
(2) V oltage Profile and Signal Fidelity:
EDLC (Linear Response) : The charge/discharge profile of 
an ideal EDLC is highly linear and triangular in a con -
stant-current cycle. This linear voltage–time relationship  
is a significant advantage for neural signal simulation. It 
enables a more direct and predictable translation of an 
applied current pulse into a voltage waveform, allowing 
for precise control over the amplitude and shape of the 
simulated neural signal [ 27]. This linearity simplifies the 
design of driving circuits for generating complex, biologi -
cally plausible waveforms.
Pseudocapacitance (Non-Linear Response) : The volt -
age profile of pseudocapacitive materials is often non-lin -
ear due to the potential-dependent nature of the faradaic 
reactions [ 28]. This results in a non-triangular, slightly 
curved charge/discharge profile . While this provides 
higher capacitance, the non-linearity can introduce dis -
tortion when trying to replicate a specific neural voltage 
waveform. The generated signal may not be a faithful 
reproduction of the input command, requiring more 
sophisticated pre-distortion algorithms or compensation 
circuits to achieve high signal fidelity [ 29].
(3) Power Delivery and Pulse Shape.
EDLC (Exponential Decay) : Upon discharge, an EDLC 
typically provides a burst of power that follows an expo -
nential decay, governed by its RC time constant [ 30]. 
While this can mimic the initial phase of an action poten -
tial, it may not perfectly capture the entire shape without 
additional shaping circuits [ 31].
Pseudocapacitance (Redox Hysteresis) : The discharge 
curve of a pseudocapacitor can be influenced by the 
specific redox reactions, potentially offering a different 
voltage profile than an EDLC [ 32]. However, hysteresis 
between charge and discharge cycles can be a source of 
signal inaccuracy and energy loss, which is undesirable for 
precise simulation [ 33].

Mechanism of Neural Signal Transmission
Neural signal transmission serves as the fundamental 
mechanism for information exchange in living organisms, 
primarily mediated through electrical and chemical signals 
between neurons [131]. Neurons transmit signals via action 
potentials, which are rapid electrical impulses triggered by 
changes in the potential gradient across a cell membrane 
[131]. This process involves three distinct phases: depo -
larization, repolarization, and hyperpolarization, involving 
transmembrane movement of sodium and potassium ions.
During depolarization, sodium ion channels open, 
allowing rapid influx of sodium ions into the cell and 
increasing the membrane potential to a more positive state. 
Subsequently, potassium ion channels open, allowing 
potassium ions to exit the cell and leading to repolariza -
tion [ 132]. Hyperpolarization occurs when potassium ion 
channels close slowly, temporarily lowering the membrane 
potential below the resting potential (Fig. 7c) [133].
Synapses serve as connection points between neurons, 
transmitting signals through the release of neurotransmit -
ters. When an action potential reaches the synaptic ter -
minal, it triggers the opening of calcium ion channels, 
allowing calcium ions to flow into the cell and prompt -
ing the release of neurotransmitters from synaptic vesicles 
into the synaptic cleft. The neurotransmitter then binds to 
receptors on the next neuron, continuing the signal trans -
mission process (Fig. 8a) [134].
Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for design -
ing bionic electrochemical devices, as they require rep -
licating the characteristics of neurons, including rapid 
response and signal transmission. SCs, with their fast 
charging/discharging capabilities and high power density, 
can effectively simulate these dynamic processes in bio -
mimetic applications, opening new possibilities for the 
development of neural interfaces and biomedical devices 
[135, 136].

and complex spatiotemporal characteristics during trans -
mission within organisms [ 137]. Neural signals typically 
operate within the frequency range of several to hundreds

SCs effectively mimics the rapid response characteristics 
of neurons, providing crucial support for the development

High Precision
Ensuring the accuracy of signal transmission and prevent -
ing information loss or distortion is a critical challenge in 
communication system design [ 168, 169]. Multiple tech -
nologies and methods can be employed to achieve this 
goal. Error Detection and Correction Codes (EDCCs) are 
commonly used approaches to ensure data transmission 
accuracy [170]. These codes add redundant information to 
the original data, enabling receivers to detect and correct 
errors introduced during transmission [ 171]. For example, 
Hamming codes and Extended Hamming codes are linear 
error correction codes widely used in computer networks, 
storage devices, and digital communication channels to 
detect and correct errors during transmission [ 171, 172]. 
Additionally, Turbo codes represent a forward error cor -
rection method that approaches the Shannon limit, making 
them suitable for encoding and decoding text and images 
[172]. Network coding technology is also employed to 
enhance data transmission reliability and security [ 173].
In network coding, original data packets can be encoded 
into an infinite number of codewords, where a subset of 
these codewords suffices to decode and restore the origi -
nal data. This approach not only improves data transmis -
sion reliability but also serves as a lightweight security 
mechanism to prevent eavesdroppers from decoding and 
recovering the original data [ 174–176]. Thirdly, in real-
time communication systems, retransmission mechanisms 
serve as a crucial means to ensure reliable data transmis -
sion [177]. By integrating retransmission with worst-case 
scheduling analysis, this approach enables handling one or 
more erroneous packet retransmissions without compro -
mising the guaranteed delay margins for other data pack -
ets [ 178, 179]. This method proves particularly effective 
for industrial applications requiring strict reliability and 
time-sensitive requirements [ 180]. Modern communica -
tion systems employ advanced machine learning technolo -
gies, especially artificial neural networks, to enhance the 
effectiveness and adaptability of error correction codes 
in diverse and dynamically changing communication 
environments [ 181, 182]. This methodology significantly 
surpasses traditional error correction methods by better 
addressing noise data challenges [ 183]. To ensure informa-
tion integrity, optimized algorithms based on binary matrix 
coordinate parity check values can be implemented [ 184]. 
These algorithms are easy to implement, fast, and efficient, 
making them suitable for small-scale binary matrices (e.g., 
4 × 8 or 7 × 8 bits) where error quantities are relatively 
low and easily detectable [ 185, 186]. Through the inte -
grated application of error detection/correction codes, net -
work coding, retransmission mechanisms, and advanced 
machine learning technologies, signal transmission 
only aids in deepening our understanding of brain mech -
anisms but also paves the way for building autonomous 
cognitive systems and high-performance artificial intelli -
gence [152, 153].

Biocompatibility
When designing devices for long-term implantation or 
direct contact with biological tissues, ensuring material 
and structural compatibility with these tissues is crucial 
to avoid immune responses or toxicity [ 212, 213]. This 
requirement stems from the interactions between implants 
and the host, which may trigger adverse reactions such 
as inflammation, fibrosis, infection, and thrombosis. To 
enhance implant safety and functionality, it is crucial to 
develop materials that elicit the desired tissue responses. 
Surface chemistry is a key factor affecting implant bio -
compatibility. By engineering surface properties (physical 
and chemical characteristics) to reduce protein adsorption 
and cellular interaction, implant biocompatibility can be 
improved [ 214]. For example, selective surface modifica -
tion can enhance implant efficacy by mimicking natural 
solutions to create surfaces that mimic cell membrane 
properties, thereby endowing synthetic materials with 
pseudo-biological characteristics.
Additionally, the use of immunomodulatory biomate -
rials serves as another strategy to improve implant bio -
compatibility [ 215]. Through knowledge-based selective 
application of these materials, neutrophil activation can be 
reduced while optimizing macrophage polarization from 
M1 to M2, Th1 to Th2 lymphocyte switching, and Treg 
induction. This demonstrates that modulating host immune 
system responses can promote implant-tissue integration 
and healing [ 216]. However, despite these advancements, 
the interactions between implants and the host remain com-
plex and require further research to fully understand their 
mechanisms. For instance, the spontaneous adsorption 
and denaturation of fibrinogen are key events that trigger 
inflammatory responses on implant surfaces. Therefore, 
understanding these molecular and cellular mechanisms 
is crucial for realizing the full potential of implant bio -
materials [ 217]. Therefore, to design long-term implants 
that are compatible with biological tissues or devices that 
directly contact them, it is essential to comprehensively 
consider the material's surface chemistry, immunomodula -
tory properties, and interactions with host tissues [ 218]. 
Selective surface modification is employed, immunomod -
ulatory biomaterials are utilized, and implant–host interac -

Dynamic Characteristics of SCs and Similarity with 
Neural Signals
The dynamic characteristics of SCs closely resemble 
the spatiotemporal properties of neural signals, provid -
ing unique advantages for their application in biological 
signal simulation [ 253]. Neural signals are renowned for 
their complex frequency and amplitude variations, which 
enable rapid responses to external stimuli and efficient 
information transmission. This fast-response capability 
aligns perfectly with the characteristics of SCs.
However, the degree of this similarity is not uniform 
and is critically governed by the underlying materials and 
device architecture. The key to emulating neural signals lies 
in replicating two primary dynamic features: the rapid, all-
or-nothing spike (action potential) and the complex, vary -
ing-frequency burst patterns [ 254]. The ability of an SC to 
faithfully reproduce these features is a direct function of 
its time constant (τ = RC). Electric double-layer capacitor 
(EDLC)-dominant materials, such as certain activated car -
bons and graphene, exhibit nearly ideal rectangular cyclic 
voltammograms and linear voltage–time charge/discharge 
profiles [255]. This linearity is crucial for high-fidelity sig -
nal generation, as it allows an input current pulse to be 
directly and predictably translated into a voltage wave -
form, accurately mimicking the shape and duration of a 
neural spike. In contrast [ 256], pseudocapacitive materials 
(e.g., MnO₂, RuO₂) often show non-linear, curved charge/
discharge profiles due to their Faradaic reactions. This 
intrinsic non-linearity can distort the output signal, making 
it less faithful to the intended neural waveform. However, 
it might provide a higher charge per volume for simulat -
ing longer-duration events [ 257]. The device structure 
dictates ion transport efficiency, which directly impacts 
the high-frequency response. A simple two-dimensional 
planar stack geometry forces ions to travel long, tortuous 
paths through the electrode bulk, leading to a high effec -
tive resistance (R) and capacitance (C), and thus a large 
time constant that limits high-frequency performance 
[258]. This can filter out the high-frequency components 
of a neural signal burst. Advanced three-dimensional (3D) 
architectures, such as interdigitated micropatterns or verti -
cally aligned nanoelectrode arrays, drastically shorten and 
straighten ion diffusion pathways [ 259]. This minimizes 
both R and C, resulting in a very small time constant (τ) 
that can extend the device's operational bandwidth into the 
kilohertz range, essential for accurately simulating rapid 
neural firing sequences without distortion [ 260].

efficient neural signal transfer. First, the rapid charge–dis -
charge capability of SCs enables immediate response to 
neural signal changes, meeting the high dynamic perfor -
mance demands of neural interfaces. This characteristic 
is crucial for achieving precise neural signal regulation 
and transmission(Fig. 13a–d) [ 363]. Second, the flexible 
structural design of SCs allows adaptation to complex bio -
logical environments, enhancing device compatibility and 
comfort. This flexibility not only reduces stimulation to 
biological tissues but also improves durability and stabil -
ity [364]. Additionally, the miniaturization and customiza -
tion of SCs enable integration into various neural interface 
devices, offering greater design flexibility and application 
potential [ 365]. This opens up vast prospects for develop -
ing next-generation, efficient, and reliable neural interface 
devices.
Potential in Biomedical Devices
In biomedical devices, SCs demonstrate significant appli -
cation potential, particularly in energy storage and man -
agement [ 366]. Their rapid charge–discharge capabilities 
enhance device responsiveness and efficiency, making 
them ideal for high-efficiency energy management sys -
tems, such as cardiac pacemakers and neurostimulators 
[367]. First, SCs provide stable power support to ensure 
prolonged operational reliability, which is critical for 
life-support equipment and other vital medical devices 
[368]. Second, their customizable design and miniatur -
ization potential enable integration into various wearable 
and implantable devices [ 369]. This flexibility not only 
expands design freedom but also improves adaptability in 
complex biological environments. Additionally, the high 
power density and fast response characteristics of SCs 
enhance overall performance in biomedical applications, 
enabling faster response to physiological signal changes 
and ultimately improving treatment efficacy and patient 
experience.

Conclusion
SCs demonstrate remarkable advantages in bio-signal sim -
ulation, particularly in neural signal transmission applica -
tions. Their rapid charge–discharge capabilities and high 
power density mirror the fast-response mechanisms of 
neurons, making them ideal candidates for bionic electro -
chemical device research. Furthermore, the flexible archi -

to complex biological environments, thereby enhancing 
device compatibility and stability. These characteristics 
position SCs as a promising solution for neural interfaces 
and biomedical devices, with broad application prospects.

electronic components are being explored to enhance over -

grated technology, more efficient energy management and 
signal processing can be achieved, improving the overall 
efficiency of devices. SCs demonstrate unique advantages 
in various applications due to their high power density, 
long cycle life, and excellent thermal performance. Com -
pared with batteries, SCs provide rapid energy release and 
absorption, which is particularly crucial for applications 
requiring fast response. In grid-connected applications, 
integrating SCs with electrochemical batteries has proven 
to be an attractive solution, combining the large-capacity 
storage of batteries with the high dynamic performance 
of SCs. This combination not only provides energy and 
power services such as inertia simulation, black start, or 
power oscillation damping, but also avoids over-engineer -
ing Energy Storage Systems (ESS) while extending bat -
tery life. Additionally, by directly connecting batteries and 
supercapacitor banks through DC-DC converter interfaces,

mize performance and provide grid frequency support. In 
microgrid systems, supercapacitor-based ESS has become 
one of the optimal choices due to its high storage capacity, 
wide operating temperature range, cost-effectiveness, and 
environmental advantages. The design and development of 
these systems, along with hardware and software designs 
tailored for specific microgrid applications, showcase the 
potential of SCs in enhancing energy utilization efficiency
