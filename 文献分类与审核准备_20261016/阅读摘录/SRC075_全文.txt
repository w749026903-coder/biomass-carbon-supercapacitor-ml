ABSTRACT
Recent years have seen remarkable progress in the integration of Density Func-
tional Theory (DFT) calculations and Machine Learning (ML) techniques, par-
ticularly within the realm of materials science. This review delves into this bur-
geoning field, focusing on their application to MXenes, a significant family of 
two-dimensional (2D) materials comprising transition metal carbides, nitrides, 
or carbonitrides. MXenes have garnered attention for their remarkable proper-
ties and versatile applications, including catalysis, gas sensing, and energy stor-
age. We further investigate the integration of ML methodologies, utilizing vari-
ous algorithms to predict MXene properties with high accuracy and efficiency. 
Through a comprehensive literature analysis, we underscore the synergistic rela-
tionship between DFT calculations and ML approaches, showcasing how their 
combined use accelerates MXene discovery and optimization for diverse techno-
logical applications. Additionally, we discuss current challenges and future direc-
tions in leveraging these computational techniques to advance our understanding 
and utilization of MXene materials. This review serves as a valuable resource for 
researchers at the intersection of computational methods and materials science, 
offering insights into state-of-the-art techniques for predicting MXene proper-
ties, performance, and guiding future research endeavors in this dynamic field.

MXenes: structures, properties, 
and applications
Introduction to MXenes
MXenes constitute an intriguing class of materials 
that have garnered immense interest in the scientific 
community due to their remarkable properties and 
versatile applications. These materials, denoted by 
the general formula  Mn+1XnTx, where M represents a 
transition metal, X denotes carbon or nitrogen, and 
 Tx signifies surface functional groups (e.g., -OH, -F, 
H, S or -O), are derived from their bulk counterparts 
known as MAX phases [41–43]. MAX phases, with the 
general formula M n+1AXn, are layered ternary com-
pounds composed of transition metals (M), group A 
elements (typically early transition metals or post-
transition metals), and carbon or nitrogen (X) [44–46]. 
The distinguishing feature of MAX phases is their 
unique crystal structure, which consists of alternat-
ing layers of transition metal carbide or nitride and 
A-group elements. The ‘ A’ layers are often composed 
of elements such as aluminum, silicon, or gallium. 
The process of selectively etching the ‘ A’ layers from 
MAX phases under appropriate conditions leads to the 
formation of MXenes, which exhibit distinct layered 
structures with intriguing properties. MXenes typi-
cally feature transition metal carbide or nitride lay-
ers interspersed with intercalated functional groups, 
such as hydroxyl or fluorine terminations. This lay-
ered arrangement imparts MXenes with exceptional 
mechanical flexibility and high surface area, making 
them attractive candidates for various applications 
[47–50]. Figure 3 showcases six distinct MXene for-
mulas, each representing a unique stoichiometry and 
layer arrangement. The  M2X configuration embodies 
a fundamental MXene structure with a single transi-
tion metal layer flanked by two X layers, presenting a 
straightforward yet inherently functional composition 
(e.g.,  Mo2C) [51]. In contrast, the  M2XO2 configuration 
(e.g.,  Mo2CO2) introduces an additional oxygen layer 
between each transition metal layer, enhancing func-
tionality and facilitating applications in energy storage 
and catalysis [52, 53]. The M’2M”X2 configuration (e.g., 
 C r2NbC2) involves the juxtaposition of two different 
transition metals with one X layer between them, offer-
ing customizable properties suited for specific appli-
cations [54]. In M’2M”2X3 (e.g.,  Cr2Nb2C3), two transi-
tion metal layers are interspersed with three X layers, 
yielding a more intricate structure conducive to ion 
storage and sensing [55]. M’2M”X2O2 features identical 
transition metal layers separated by an oxygen layer 
(e.g.,  Cr2NbN2O2), providing enhanced stability and 
electrochemical properties suitable for batteries and 
capacitors [56]. Finally, M’2M”2X3O2 (e.g.,  V2Cr2C3O2) 
combines two different transition metal layers with 
three X layers and an additional oxygen layer, amal-
gamating diverse elemental properties for multifunc-
tional applications.

composed of transition metal carbide or nitride layers

tional groups, encompassing hydroxyl or fluorine 
terminations, intricately modulate MXene surface 
chemistry, enabling tailored interactions crucial for 
diverse applications [58]. This structural diversity 
underpins MXenes’ exceptional mechanical flexibility,

transport. In electronic devices, such as transistors

Applications of MXenes
Energy storage
MXene have emerged as promising electrode materials 
for energy storage devices such as batteries and super-
capacitors [66, 67]. Their high electrical conductivity 
facilitates rapid charge transfer, while their large sur-
face area enhances ion storage capacity. Additionally, 
MXene excellent ion diffusion kinetics ensures efficient 
ion transport within the electrode material, leading to 
improved energy storage and retrieval performance 
[68]. These attributes make it valuable contenders in 
the quest for high-performance energy storage solu-
tions, with potential applications in portable electron-
ics, electric vehicles, and grid-scale energy storage sys-
tems [69].
Catalysis
Catalysis stands as one of the most intriguing and 
impactful domains where MXenes have showcased 
their prowess. MXenes, with their remarkable cata-
lytic properties, have garnered significant attention 
across a spectrum of catalytic applications, ranging 
from pivotal reactions like the hydrogen evolution 
reaction (HER) [70, 71] and oxygen evolution reac-
tion (OER) to other crucial catalytic processes [72, 73]. 
Their catalytic prowess stems from a combination of 
factors, notably their high electrocatalytic activity and 
tunable surface chemistry. The high electrocatalytic 
activity exhibited by MXenes is a cornerstone of their 
catalytic performance. This attribute enables MXenes 
to accelerate chemical reactions with remarkable effi-
ciency and selectivity, thus making them highly effec-
tive catalysts for a diverse range of transformations. By 
serving as catalysts, they play a pivotal role in facili-
tating key reactions essential for sustainable develop-
ment, including those involved in renewable energy 
production, water electrolysis, and environmental 
remediation. For instance, in the context of the water 
gas shift reaction (WGS) [74, 75], they have demon-
strated exceptional catalytic activity in promoting the 
conversion of carbon monoxide (CO) and water  (H2O) 
into carbon dioxide  (CO2) and hydrogen  (H2), a criti-
cal step in hydrogen production and carbon emissions 
reduction processes [76]. Moreover, its tunable sur-
face chemistry further enhances their catalytic perfor-
mance, allowing for customization of catalytic activity 
and selectivity to suit specific reaction requirements.
Sensing
MXenes exhibit significant promises in sensing appli-
cations, spanning gas sensing, and environmental 
monitoring. Their remarkable sensitivity to alterations 
in surface interactions and electrical conductivity ena-
bles the detection of diverse analytes with exceptional

Computational studies on MXenes
Introduction to computational methods
In the realm of computational chemistry, density 
functional theory stands as a cornerstone, enabling 
the numerical solution of the Schrödinger equation to 
unravel the intricacies of quantum systems [81, 82]. 
This process involves breaking down the equation 
into discrete components, allowing for computational 
analysis via algorithms. From this solution emerges a 
wealth of information, including the electronic wave 
function and density, which delineates the behav-
ior and spatial distribution of electrons within the 
system. Leveraging these insights, various physical 
quantities pertinent to the system’s ground state can 
be extracted, offering crucial understanding of stabil-
ity, reactivity, and molecular geometry. Furthermore, 
DFT computations unveil the distribution of atomic 
charges within molecules, crucial for predicting inter-
molecular interactions in chemical reactions. Beyond 
molecular systems, DFT also extends its reach to mate-
rials science, elucidating the band structure and elec-
tronic properties of materials, thus shedding light on 
conductivity and optical behavior. Additionally, DFT 
facilitates the analysis of energy landscapes, providing 
derivatives of energy with respect to atomic positions, 
which in turn reveal critical points such as minimal 
and saddle points on potential energy surfaces. This 
understanding is fundamental for predicting reaction 
pathways and rates, thus bridging the gap between 
theoretical insight and practical applications in chemi-
cal and materials science.
Density functional theory
Density Functional Theory is a widely adopted quan-
tum mechanical framework for investigating the elec-
tronic structure of materials at the atomic scale. Within 
this formalism, the total energy of a many-electron 
system is expressed as a functional of the electron 
density, enabling an efficient yet accurate description 
of complex materials, including MXenes. In practice, 
DFT calculations are commonly performed using 
well-established computational packages such as the 
Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [83– 85], 
which provides a robust platform for implementing 
various exchange–correlation approximations and 
treating periodic systems. A central aspect of DFT 
methodology is the classification of exchange–corre-
lation (XC) functionals, which largely determine the 
accuracy of calculated properties. These functions are 
typically organized in a hierarchical framework based 
on their level of approximation. The Local Spin Den-
sity Approximation (LSDA) represents the simplest 
level, where the XC energy depends solely on the local 
electron density and spin polarization [86]. Although 
computationally efficient, its applicability is limited in 
systems with strong density inhomogeneities.
The Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) 
improves upon LSDA by incorporating the gradient 
of the electron density, allowing for a more accurate 
description of structural, energetic, and electronic 
properties in a wide range of materials [87]. Further 
refinement is achieved with Meta-GGA functionals, 
which include higher-order terms such as kinetic 
energy density, enhancing the treatment of both 
localized and delocalized electrons [88]. At a more 
sophisticated level, Hybrid Functionals combine a 
portion of exact exchange from Hartree–Fock theory 
with conventional DFT approximations, significantly 
improving the prediction of band gaps, electronic 
structure, and charge-transfer behavior. These func-
tionals are further classified into Global Hybrids, 
Range-Separated Hybrids, Screened Hybrids, and 
Double-Hybrid approaches, depending on how 
exchange and correlation contributions are treated 
[89–91]. Such methods are particularly important for 
accurately describing transition metal-based systems 
and low-dimensional materials like MXenes. Within

Machine learning techniques
The use of machine learning in materials science 
primarily focuses on the screening and design of 
new applied materials through the development of 
systematic and high-throughput algorithmic frame-
works [113, 114]. This process relies on data acquired 
from experiments or density functional theory calcu-

dictions. Python is the predominant programming 
language used for ML, although MATLAB is also 
popular choice in the materials field, and a growing 
number of low- and no-code solutions are becoming 
available [115]. ML has been crucial in predicting the 
potential of MXenes and suggesting new materials 
for specific applications. Integrating ML in materials 
science not only forecasts the suitability of certain 
materials for use but also provides statistical insights 
into the key attributes of these materials, explain-
ing why they are suitable for specific purposes [116]. 
The workflow for creating a machine learning model 
involves several steps [117, 118]. Machine learning 
techniques have become increasingly important in 
MXene research due to their ability to rapidly predict 
material properties from large datasets.
Traditional algorithms such as linear regression, 
support vector machines, and random forests have 
been widely used for property prediction. However, 
more recent approaches, including deep learning 
and graph-based models, provide improved capa-
bility for capturing complex, non-linear relation-
ships. In addition, machine learning enables high-
throughput screening of MXenes by significantly 
reducing computational cost compared to DFT 
calculations. This makes ML particularly useful for 
exploring large compositional spaces and identify-
ing promising candidates for experimental valida-
tion. The performance of machine learning models 
strongly depends on the choice of descriptors used 
to represent MXene structures. These descriptors 
can be broadly classified into structural, electronic, 
and compositional features. Structural descriptors 
include lattice parameters, layer thickness, and sur-
face terminations, which capture geometric charac-
teristics of MXenes.
Electronic descriptors, such as band gap, density 
of states, and charge distribution, provide insight 
into electronic behavior and are often derived from 
DFT calculations. Compositional descriptors, includ-
ing elemental properties such as atomic number,

Data collection
To develop a machine learning model, having well-
prepared data for performance evaluation is crucial, 
typically obtained through experimental or theoretical 
methods [119]. The data must go through collection, 
examination, processing, cleaning, and refining. This 
process includes dealing with missing values, fixing 
inaccuracies, standardizing units, and analyzing corre-
lations between data points. Once refined, the dataset 
should be split into three separate sets: training, vali-
dation, and testing. The training set is used to adjust 
the internal parameters of the algorithm by fitting 
the model to this data portion. The validation set is 
used to determine the best combination of algorithms, 
hyperparameters, and features. Hyperparameters are 
external values to the model that relate to the training 
process and can be selected and adjusted. To minimize 
bias towards the validation set, k-fold cross-validation 
should be used, with k typically recommended to be 
5 or 10 [120].
The test set evaluates the model’s performance on 
new, unseen data. Data preparation consumes a sig-
nificant amount of time in machine learning projects, 
underscoring its importance to the model’s success. 
The integration of DFT calculations with machine 
learning models typically follows a structured work-
flow. First, high-quality datasets are generated using 
DFT simulations, providing fundamental properties 
such as total energy, band structure, adsorption ener-
gies, and charge distribution. These outputs are then 
transformed into meaningful descriptors or features 
that can be used as inputs for machine learning mod-
els. Subsequently, the dataset undergoes preprocess-
ing, including normalization, feature selection, and 
splitting into training, validation, and testing sets. 
Machine learning algorithms are then trained to learn 
the underlying relationships between input descrip-
tors and target properties. Finally, the trained models 
are used for rapid prediction and screening of new 
MXene materials, significantly reducing computa-
tional cost compared to conventional DFT calculations.
Feature engineering
Features are input variables used to predict out-
put properties, also termed descriptors in materials 
design, covering a broad spectrum of material proper-
ties from experimental data to computed values like 
those from DFT. Prioritizing predictive features is 
vital, akin to selecting the appropriate algorithm, with 
expert knowledge being crucial [121]. While numer-
ous features ranging from hundreds to thousands may 
be initially considered, only a handful typically prove 
significant for the final model’s effectiveness, mak-
ing feature selection indispensable [122]. This process 
employs various methods such as filter methods using 
specific statistical metrics, wrapper methods like back-
ward feature elimination, or specialized algorithms 
like LASSO regression, all aiding in refining the model 
for optimal performance.
Model optimization
The goal of model optimization is to determine the 
best parameters and hyperparameters for an algo-
rithm. Machine learning can be categorized into super-
vised and unsupervised learning. Supervised learning 
is typically used for data classification or prediction, 
while unsupervised learning is used to uncover rela-
tionships within datasets. Numerous algorithms are 
available for these tasks, including linear regression 
[123], random forest [124], decision trees [125], k-near-
est neighbors [126], gradient boosting [127], support 
vector machines [128], artificial neural networks [129], 
and XGBoost [130]. In ML models, underfitting and 
overfitting are common issues [131]. Underfitting 
occurs when a model fails to capture the underlying 
patterns in the training data, resulting in poor gen-
eralization to validation and test datasets. This issue 
is generally straightforward to detect. Overfitting, on 
the other hand, happens when a model captures the

training data too closely, making it overly specific and 
unable to generalize well with new data. Although 
cross-validation can help reduce overfitting, it cannot 
eliminate it, and this problem is particularly prevalent 
in materials science.
Feature importance
After achieving satisfactory results through cross-
validation during model optimization, it’s essential 
to investigate feature importance to interpret the 
model and derive meaningful insights from the data. 
This step can help identify whether additional related 
features should be included or if irrelevant features 
should be excluded to minimize noise. There are sev-
eral techniques available for evaluating feature impor-
tance [132]. Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI) is used 
specifically for tree-based algorithms such as decision 
trees, random forests, and XGBoost, and it is relatively 
cost-effective to calculate [133]. Feature Permutation 
Importance (FPI) involves randomly shuffling each 
feature individually and assessing the model’s perfor-
mance each time; significant features will cause a more 
substantial decline in performance metrics when shuf-
fled. While FPI can be applied to any machine learn -
ing algorithm, it is more computationally demanding 
than MDI. SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) 
values, derived from cooperative game theory, con-
sider all possible combinations of features and meas-
ure the change in model output with the inclusion of 
each feature [134]. This approach, requiring exten-
sive model retraining, is even more computationally 
intensive than FPI but is applicable to any algorithm. 
The choice of descriptors directly influences model 
performance, as relevant and physically meaningful 
features improve prediction accuracy and generaliza-
tion, whereas poorly selected descriptors can lead to 
reduced reliability and overfitting.
Model interpretation
When interpreting a model, the final assessment set aims 
to gauge the model’s genuine performance using fresh 
data that wasn’t part of earlier stages like feature engi-
neering and optimization. Typically, this set constitutes 
10% to 30% of the entire dataset. If further refining of the 
model is necessary, it should be conducted using new 
data. The minimal requirements for a useful machine 
learning model can vary widely depending on factors 
such as the field of application, data characteristics, and 
the specific problem being addressed. In general, for 
regression tasks, it’s recommended to achieve an  R2 of 
at least 0.5, whereas for classification tasks, an accuracy 
of at least 70% is desired [135]. However, these bench-
marks may vary depending on the problem, and alter-
native statistical measures might offer different insights. 
For regression tasks, it is essential to report metrics 
such as  R2, mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean 
squared error (RMSE). For classification tasks, metrics 
such as accuracy, precision, recall, balanced accuracy or 
F1 score, and either the kappa value or Matthews’s cor-
relation coefficient should be considered. Additionally, 
factors like resistance to overfitting (indicated by a drop 
in metric performance from cross-validation to the final 
test), training time, computational efficiency, scalability 
concerning data volume, and interpretability are crucial 
aspects to consider.

Critical analysis
Despite the significant progress achieved through the 
integration of DFT and ML in MXene research, several 
critical challenges remain that limit their full poten-
tial. A key issue lies in the limited availability of high-
quality, standardized datasets, as the computational 
cost of DFT restricts large-scale data generation and 
introduces inconsistencies across studies. In parallel, 
while ML models provide rapid predictions, their reli-
ability is often constrained by overfitting, insufficient 
data diversity, and limited validation on complex 
MXene systems. Furthermore, the transferability of 
these models across different compositions and sur-
face terminations remains inadequate, reducing their 
applicability in broader materials discovery.
Another important limitation is the lack of inter-
pretability in advanced models, which hinders physi-
cal understanding and practical implementation. 
Addressing these challenges will require the develop-
ment of robust and standardized data infrastructures, 
improved validation strategies, and the adoption of 
hybrid approaches that integrate physical principles 
with data-driven methods. In this context, emerging 
techniques such as graph-based learning and explain-
able artificial intelligence offer promising directions 
for enhancing both predictive accuracy and scientific 
insight, ultimately enabling more reliable and scalable 
exploration of MXene materials.
Challenges and limitations
Data quality and quantity remain a major challenge 
in MXene research, as machine learning models 
require large and diverse datasets to learn effec-
tively, while obtaining high-quality experimental 
data for MXene properties is labor-intensive and 
time-consuming, and inconsistencies in experimental 
procedures and data reporting can introduce biases 
and uncertainties that impact model performance. 
Another significant issue is the interpretability of 
models; despite their predictive power, machine 
learning models often lack interpretability, making 
it difficult to extract actionable insights and under -
stand the underlying physics governing MXene 
properties, which highlights the need for interpret-
able techniques that provide transparent explana -
tions. Transferability and generalization also pre-
sent major limitations, as models trained on specific 
MXene datasets may struggle to generalize to new 
compositions or experimental conditions do not pre-
sent in the training data, requiring careful consid -
eration of data representation, feature selection, and 
validation strategies.
Computational cost and scalability further con-
strain progress, since training complex models on 
large datasets can be prohibitive and demands the 
development of scalable algorithms, efficient fea-
ture engineering, and parallel computing strategies. 
The integration of domain knowledge from materi-
als science, chemistry, physics, and computational 
modeling adds complexity due to the heterogene-
ity of MXene properties, requiring interdisciplinary 
collaboration and hybrid modeling approaches that 
combine data-driven methods with fundamental 
principles. Experimental validation and the estab-
lishment of a robust feedback loop between compu-
tational predictions and experiments remain chal-
lenging due to resource constraints, experimental 
variability, and reproducibility issues, emphasizing 
the need for close collaboration between computa-
tional researchers and experimentalists.
Ethical and societal implications, including con-
cerns related to data privacy, bias, and algorithmic 
transparency, also need to be addressed through 
proper data governance and responsible practices. 
Despite significant progress, limitations persist in 
integrating DFT and machine learning for MXene 
research, particularly due to the scarcity of high-
quality and standardized datasets, issues with model 
reliability such as overfitting and lack of robustness 
on unseen data, and limited transferability across 
different compositions and structures. Further -
more, the interpretability of complex models, espe-
cially deep learning approaches, remains limited, 
hindering physical understanding, and addressing 
these challenges requires the development of larger 
datasets, improved validation strategies, and hybrid 
physics-informed machine learning models.

Conclusions
In conclusion, the interdisciplinary fusion of materi-
als science, computational modeling, and machine 
learning has propelled MXene research to new 
heights, offering unprecedented insights into the 
structure–property relationships and potential appli-
cations of MXene materials. The extensive body of 
research reviewed here highlights the remarkable 
progress made in predicting, synthesizing, and char-
acterizing MXenes, paving the way for their diverse 
applications in energy storage, catalysis, sensing, and 
beyond. Looking ahead, the prospects for MXene 
research are promising, with exciting opportunities on 
the horizon. By embracing advanced machine learning 
techniques, integrating high-throughput experimental 
screening, and adopting sustainable materials design 
principles, researchers can accelerate the discovery of 
novel MXene compositions with tailored properties 
and enhanced performance. Moreover, the develop-
ment of materials informatics frameworks, digital 
twins, and uncertainty quantification methods will 
further enhance our ability to design, optimize, and 
deploy MXene-based materials and devices in real-
world applications. As we embark on this journey of 
exploration and innovation, it is essential to foster col-
laboration, diversity, and inclusivity within the MXene 
research community.
By fostering open exchange of data, ideas, and 
expertise, we can collectively tackle the grand chal-
lenges facing materials science and engineering and 
unlock the full potential of MXene materials for soci-
etal benefit. In closing, the future of MXene research 
is bright, with boundless opportunities for discovery, 
innovation, and impact. By harnessing the collective 
expertise and creativity of researchers from diverse 
backgrounds, we can continue to push the boundaries 
of MXene science and technology, shaping a more sus-
tainable, resilient, and interconnected world for gen-
erations to come. While DFT and machine learning 
approaches provide powerful predictive capabilities, 
experimental validation remains essential to confirm 
their reliability. Several studies have demonstrated 
good agreement between computational predictions 
and experimental measurements for MXene proper-
ties such as electronic conductivity, adsorption behav-
ior, and structural stability. However, discrepancies 
can arise due to idealized conditions in simulations 
compared to real experimental environments. There-
fore, integrating computational predictions with 
experimental validation is crucial for ensuring prac-
tical applicability and guiding the design of MXene-
based materials.
