Engineering the future of heat transfer: artificial neural 
network and genetic algorithm synergy to model next-
generation hybrid nanofluid performance
Md. Munirul Hasan · Md Mustafizur Rahman  · Suraya Abu Bakar · Devarajan Ramasany
Received: 19 June 2025 / Accepted: 10 July 2026 / Published online: 31 August 2026
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag London Ltd., part of Springer Nature 2026
Abstract
The use of conventional heat transfer fluids has many limitations, and industrial applications require efficient 
heat transfer systems. Existing cooling systems suffer from low thermal conductivity and poor heat transfer, 
which result in reduced efficiency and high power consumption. This study proposes an optimised artificial 
neural network (ANN) and genetic algorithm (GA)-based artificial intelligence model to predict the thermal 
conductivity and viscosity of graphene nanoplatelets (GNP)–Cellulose nanocrystals (CNC)/Ethylene Glycol 
(EG)-water hybrid nanofluids, thereby improving heat transfer performance. Single GNP and hybrid GNP/CNC 
nanofluids are studied, with field-emission scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy 
used to characterise nanoparticles. A two-step preparation method was used for thermal conductivity and vis -
cosity measurements across temperatures (30–80 °C) and volume concentrations (0.02–0.2 vol%). The research 
employs a combined experimental analysis and modern machine learning methods, including ANN and GA, to 
develop an optimised predictive model. The results indicate a considerable improvement in thermal conductiv -
ity, with GNP/CNC hybrid nanofluids at 0.2 vol% and 80 °C showing a 52.77% increase over the base fluid. 
The rheological behaviour analysis reveals that viscosity increases with volume concentration and decreases 
with increasing temperature. At a low concentration of 0.2 vol%, the viscosities at 30 °C are approximately 
27.5% higher (hybrid nanofluid). The developed GA-ANN model demonstrated a high predictive ability, with 
a coefficient of determination (R²) = 0.998, RMSE = 0.3231, and MAE = 0.683, which was significantly more 
accurate than conventional ANN models ( P < 0.01). The results demonstrate the feasibility of hybrid nanoflu -
ids for enhanced heat-transfer applications and provide a consistent predictive framework for estimating their 
properties. This study represents significant progress in thermal management systems, paving the way for new 
heat transfer solutions for next-generation technologies.
Keywords Thermal conductivity · Viscosity · Artificial neural network · Genetic algorithms · Optimisation

1 Introduction
The limitations of classical heat transfer fluids have underscored the need for effective cooling approaches, par -
ticularly as modern thermal management systems become increasingly sophisticated. Nanofluids are one pos -
sible solution, consisting of microstructures dispersed in a base fluid (such as water, ethylene glycol, oil, or 
glycerol), which offer improved thermophysical properties. The tunability of these fluids, achieved by modifying 
nanoparticle characteristics, enables unprecedented versatility across various applications [ 1]. Recent contribu-
tions include hybrid nanofluids, whose composition comprises multiple nanomaterials that interact synergisti -
cally, resulting in enhanced performance. These hybrid systems improve thermal performance by optimising the 
thermal networks and aspect ratios of different nanomaterials [ 2]. Their use encounters real-world difficulties, 
namely increased viscosity, which requires higher pressure drops and pumping power [ 3]. Thus, a critical area 
that needs further investigation and optimisation is the trade-off between improved thermal performance and 
system power efficiency. The growing need for thermal management systems has spurred in-depth investigations 
into advanced heat-transfer materials. This followed Choi’s [ 4] groundbreaking work introducing nanofluids, 
which demonstrated improved thermal properties arising from suspending nanoparticles in base fluids. This work 
has since attracted tremendous research activity in various fields, including chemistry, energy, and industry [ 5]. 
Carbon nanostructure-based fluids have been widely studied, with a focus on carbon nanotubes [ 6], graphene 
derivatives, and their hybrids [ 7]. Graphene nanoplatelets (GNP) are among the most promising materials, bal -
ancing the challenging thermal performance of monolayer graphene with the proven stability and cost-effective 
properties of graphitic carbon. These innovations help overcome the fundamental limitation of classical cooling 
systems: the low thermal conductivity of the heat-transfer fluids.
On the other hand, there are considerable research gaps in hybrid nanofluids. Although considerable work has 
been done on single-carbon nanostructures, hybrid arrangements that offer synergistic advantages have not been 
extensively studied. The interaction mechanism between nanofluid composition and thermophysical properties 
in multi-nanofluid systems is not entirely understood. While graphene nanoplatelets possess various promising 
properties, challenges in dispersion stability and long-term performance warrant further exploration. A detailed 
study of the optimisation of combinations of carbon nanostructures and their synergy to date for thermal conduc-
tivity enhancement is still lacking. Such a gap is critical to the cost-effectiveness and practicality of realistic ther-
mal management systems. In addition, although machine learning methods can predict nanofluid characteristics, 
there is an opportunity to combine genetic algorithms with neural networks to optimise hybrid nanofluid charac-
teristics. This combination has been barely explored. Graphene-based nanofluids have recently gained consider-
able interest due to significant advancements in thermal management systems and the availability of GNP in the 
market. Despite this, the specific surface area of GNP continues to increase, leading to a strong tendency toward 
aggregate formation via van der Waals forces, which remains a major hurdle for thermal applications requiring 
stable dispersion [7]. To overcome this limitation, researchers investigated different innovative methods. Kulan-
daivel et al. [8] explored the use of environmentally friendly GNP-palm oil and CNC hybrid alternatives. GNP 
nanofluids based on water were examined by Bansal et al. [ 9], who investigated possible behaviours as a func -
tion of concentration over a volume concentration range of 0.0125% to 0.0375%. Carbon-based materials have 
evolved for several generations in thermal applications, from simple carbon black to distributed nanostructures 
[10], such as functionalised carbon nanotubes [11]. Over the last decade, carbon spheres of various sizes, ranging 
from the nanostructure scale to the mesoscale, have emerged individually. Among these, graphene nanoplatelets 
have shown great promise, providing a practical compromise between the remarkable characteristics of graphene 
and low-cost manufacture [12]. These multi-layered graphene flakes retain many of graphene’s attractive proper-
ties while enhancing cost-effectiveness for industrial applications.
Recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) have revolutionised nanofluid research through complex 
data-driven technologies such as artificial neural networks (ANNs), adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference systems 
(ANFIS), and support vector machines (SVMs). These AI approaches have accurately predicted the thermophysical

intelligent systems (CMIS) resulted in excellent accuracy, with an average error in predicting thermal conduc -

synergistic benefits from multiple AI techniques, persist in current AI modelling approaches, indicating the need 
for a next-level methodological approach. However, recently introduced data-driven methods have shown great 
potential in predicting hybrid nanofluids. Said et al. [ 17] demonstrated that ML-based methods, including Mul -
tivariate Linear Regression (MLR) and its interaction variant (MLRI), outperformed traditional empirical cor -
relations for nanodiamond-Fe3O4 hybrid nanofluids. Using optimised ANN structures (1–4−1 and 1–5−1) with 
tangent sigmoid activation functions integrated with the ANFIS and RSM methodologies, Borode and Olubambi 
[18] demonstrated considerable success in predicting viscosity. Some additional comprehensive work on predic-
tive modelling of MWCNT-MgO hybrid nanofluids was outlined using the 3–104-1 MLP architecture, achieving 
an MSE as low as 4.73 × 10−5 with the well-known Levenberg-Marquardt paradigm [19]. These drives, confirmed 
by previous studies [ 19, 20], emphasise the rising potential of machine learning to characterise complex fluid 
classes, such as nanofluids.
Computational approaches for predicting the thermophysical properties of advanced materials are becoming 
more complex. The ability of the genetic algorithm (GA) to optimise ANN models’ weights and biases to improve 
predictive performance makes it a practical tool for global optimisation [21]. Previous research has demonstrated 
higher predictive accuracy for GA-optimized ANN models than for conventional approaches [22]. GA and ANN 
Models have been applied to various fields in recent years. Some notable successes include Li et al. [ 23], who 
achieved 92.3% accuracy in predicting nitrogen diffusion behaviour, and Chi et al. [ 24], who successfully pre -
dicted erosion rates in shale gas systems. Several studies have shown that GA optimisation improves the configu-
ration and prediction stability of ANN networks [25]. Nonetheless, significant research gaps remain in applying 
GA-based ANN models to predict thermophysical properties, particularly for complex systems such as nanoflu-
ids. The exploration of hyperparameter tuning for maximum prediction accuracy with optimised model complex-
ity and size remains unexplored, as anticipated, which is a key challenge in thermal engineering applications.
In this regard, the current study aims to advance hybrid nanofluid technology through experimental studies and 
AI-based modelling. The main novelties of the article are outlined below:
1. This study investigates the synergistic effects of two distinct elongating nanoparticles, graphene nanoplatelets 
and cellulose nanocrystals, in aqueous and aqueous-ethylene glycol nanofluids, highlighting the research gap 
regarding CNC as green cooling promoters. This study involves the experimental characterisation of the sta -
bility and properties of the GNP-CNC hybrid nanofluid.
2. The study developed a genetic algorithm-optimised artificial neural network (GA-ANN) for property predic-
tion. The study also reveals a relationship between thermal conductivity and viscosity as a function of tem -
perature and volume fraction.

2 Methods and materials
Two distinct nanoparticles were selected for their complementary properties in thermal applications. High-purity 
(99.9%) graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs) were procured from Nanografi Nanotechnology (USA), exhibiting 
exceptional characteristics, including a specific surface area of 800 m²·g⁻¹, a uniform thickness of 3 nm, and 
a consistent diameter of 1.5 μm. These properties make GNP particularly suitable for applications requiring 
enhanced thermal conductivity. Cellulose nanocrystal (CNC), sourced from MY Biomass Sdn. Bhd. (Malaysia) 
were selected as a sustainable complement to GNP. All nanoparticle measurements were conducted using an 
Internal Sartorius Analytical Balance (Model: BSA24S-CW), ensuring accuracy within ± 0.1 mg for reliable 
sample preparation and characterisation. The preparation process employed specialised equipment to achieve a 
uniform dispersion of nanoparticles. This included a magnetic stirrer with a rotating magnetic probe (Thermo 
Fisher, USA) and an advanced ultrasonicator system (CE ISO Ultrasonic Homogeniser Sonicator Processor Cell 
Disruptor Mixer). The ultrasonication process was conducted at 20 kHz with a power output of 950 W, capable 
of effectively processing sample volumes ranging from 20 to 1000 mL.
A challenge in CNC preparation arose from its inherent hydrophilicity, necessitating the development of a 
specialised spray-drying technique for powder conversion. This process utilised a small fan system in which the 
CNC suspension was exposed to heated air through the spray dryer nozzle, facilitating rapid moisture evapora -
tion and the formation of stable CNC flakes. These flakes were collected and pulverised into a fine powder. The 
resulting CNC nanoparticles exhibited excellent characteristics, including a crystallinity index of 80%, crystal 
lengths ranging from 100 to 150 nm, crystal diameters of 9–14 nm, and a hydrodynamic diameter of 150 nm. 
The physical properties of both GNP and CNC nanoparticles are detailed in Table 2. In contrast, Table 3 presents 
the thermophysical properties of the base fluids (water and ethylene glycol) at a reference temperature of 30 °C. 
These properties are fundamental parameters for the subsequent analysis and characterisation of the hybrid nano-
fluid system.

2.1 Nanofluid preparation
The preparation of nanofluids followed a two-phase protocol encompassing both single-component and hybrid 
nanofluid synthesis. In the first phase, GNP was dispersed in a 60:40 base fluid at precise volume concentrations 
of 0.02%, 0.06%, 0.1%, 0.15%, and 0.2%. While carbon-based nanoparticles tend to be hydrophobic, GNPs can 
disperse without surfactants due to their novel electrical and thermal conductivities. Hence, the EG-to-water ratio 
of 60:40 was chosen because it offers a favourable balance among thermal conductivity, viscosity, freezing-point 
suppression, and corrosion protection. Water provides excellent heat-transfer characteristics, whereas ethylene 
glycol improves thermal stability and operational reliability across a wide range of conditions. The 60:40 ratio 
has been widely adopted in thermal management systems and nanofluid investigations, making it a practical and 
representative base fluid for evaluating the thermophysical performance of the prepared hybrid nanofluids [ 17]. 
For the first 2 h, a magnetic stirrer was used to disperse the solution before transferring it to the probe ultrasoni-
cator with a 13-mm-diameter probe. The dispersion was ultrasonicated for 5 h at 50% power to ensure optimal 
dispersion without compromising stability. In the second phase, hybrid nanofluids of GNP-CNC were synthe -
sised by mixing both nanoparticles in equal quantities at a volume concentration of 0.02% to 0.2%. Initially, the 
mixture was stirred magnetically at 400–500 rpm, then mixed for 120–180 min to achieve optimal powder blend-
ing, with systematic 15-minute stirring intervals. The first mixing phase played a crucial role in ensuring uniform 
dispersion of nanoparticles in the base fluid.
After the mixing step, the solutions were stabilised. This included treatment in an ultrasonic bath, followed by 
probe ultrasonication for 5 h at 50% power output. One noteworthy aspect of this method was the introduction 
of 5-minute cooling breaks every 15 min during sonication. Such a controlled interval approach was crucial for 
the dispersion process, ensuring that the fluid temperature remained fixed at a low value and the geometry of the 
nanoparticles remained intact. The weight and density of the hybrid nanoparticles were calculated using estab -
lished equations (Eqs. 1 and 2) to characterise the final nanofluid compositions [34] accurately.

2.2 Stability analysis
The intrinsic tendency of nanoparticles to agglomerate due to their high surface area poses a challenge to the 
stability of hybrid nanofluids and their practical applicability. To address this crucial problem, the stability and 
dispersibility of CNC-containing nanofluids were analysed using various characterisation techniques. The frame-
work of this investigation was based on three approaches: temporal sedimentation imaging, UV-Vis spectroscopy, 
and Zeta potential analysis. UV-Vis spectroscopy was conducted using a PerkinElmer LAMBDA UV/Vis spec-
trophotometer, with measurements taken from 200 to 800 nm. Samples were placed in specialised quartz cuvettes 
and carefully diluted to ensure optimal clarity for accurate transmission measurements of incident light. The 
physical stability of the nanofluid dispersions was quantitatively assessed using zeta potential measurements on 
an Anton Paar Litesizer 500, which quantified electrostatic repulsion between similarly charged particles. On the 
other hand, Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) (PerkinElmer TGA 4000) was used to characterise the thermal 
stability of the synthesised nanofluids. Thermogravimetric (TG) analyses (TGA) were performed at atmospheric 
pressure, in a nitrogen atmosphere, from 30 °C to 500 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C min − 1, giving insights into 
the thermal properties of the nanofluid systems.
2.3 Characterization
Microstructural and compositional analyses were conducted on the fabricated nanofluids using various sophis -
ticated characterisation tools. The dispersion properties and particle size distribution of GNP and GNP-CNC 
hybrid nanofluids in EG-water base fluid were assessed using a digital Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) 
system (Tecnai G220S-TWIN, USA). TEM images were obtained from nanofluid samples sonicated for 15 min 
to ensure homogeneous dispersion prior to TEM analysis. The examination was performed at an accelerating 
voltage of 200 kV to achieve optimal resolution and contrast. The surface morphology was studied using a 
Scanning Electron Microscope (Hitachi TM-3030 Plus, Czech Republic). After applying appropriate coating 
procedures, a sputter coating was applied to increase surface conductivity, thereby permitting in situ visualisa -
tion of the nanofluid microstructure. Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed to analyse 
the chemical composition and molecular interactions of the nanofluids. The FTIR spectra (4000–500 cm⁻¹) were 
recorded using KBr pellets to obtain the best signal. This information provides useful insights into the functional 
groups and chains, as well as their interactions, in nanofluid systems.
2.4 Thermal conductivity measurement
The thermal conductivity of Water/EG-based GNP/CNC hybrid nanofluids was measured using a KD2-Pro ther-
mal analyser (Decagon Devices Inc., USA) based on the transient hot-wire (THW) principle. However, the THW 
method is the most frequently used method for measuring the thermal conductivity of nanofluids [ 35]. The pre-
cisely controllable range for volume concentrations and temperatures was established with the experimental 
setup, as depicted in Fig. 2. All temperatures were calibrated using a WNB7-MEMMERT temperature bath 
(Germany). In particular, probe placement and vibration were controlled to minimise the experimental error. The 
temperature bath has been integrated with a custom-designed horizontal frame that ensures the KS-1 probe is ver-
tically positioned at the centre of each sample vial. Twenty consecutive readings were taken at five intervals for 
each combined concentration at a given volume and temperature to ensure measurement reliability and statistical 
significance. The technical parameters of the KD2-Pro thermal analyser are shown in Table 4.

2.5 Viscosity
Rheological characterisation was conducted using two complementary systems: a Brookfield DV-I prime vis -
cometer and an RST coaxial cylinder rheometer. Viscosity measurements were performed at various volume 
concentrations across a 20–80 °C temperature range. The RST rheometer, equipped with a precision-controlled 
circulating water jacket, offered an extensive measurement capability ranging from − 200 °C to + 180 °C, with 
viscosity detection limits from 0.0001 Pa·s to 5.4 × 10⁶ Pa·s. All experiments were conducted under steady-state 
conditions using rotational measurements with controlled shear rates. Base fluid viscosities were compared with 
ASHRAE standard data to verify the accuracy of the measurements. However, all tests used a normalised sample 
volume of 15.7 mL, and data were acquired via direct computer interfacing with the RST rheometer. Addition -
ally, five independent readings were taken for each condition to ensure measurement reliability and statistical 
significance, and the results were averaged to mitigate experimental uncertainty. This approach follows the gen-
eral principles of rheological characterisation demonstrated in earlier studies, in which analogous Brookfield 
rheometer methods were used [36].

2.6.2 Genetic algorithm optimisation
The Genetic Algorithm (GA) is a computational, adaptive, probabilistic optimisation methodology that integrates 
Darwinian evolution with Mendel’s theory of mixed heredity, making it more effective in optimising complex 
systems [ 43]. GA was initially conceived by Holland [ 44] and later further developed by many researchers, 
including Whitley [ 45], Houck et al. [ 46], and Deb et al. [ 47]. Due to its flexibility and proven optimisation 
performance, it is utilised in various applications, including function optimisation, image recognition, neural 
network optimisation, and scheduling problems. Combining GA with neural networks is a promising approach to 
optimise network parameters. In this hybrid framework, each individual in the GA population represents a poten-
tial solution, encoding weights and biases for both the hidden and output layers. The population consists of such 
individuals, forming a complete search space. The neural network prediction error is used as each individual’s 
fitness; the Mean Squared Error (MSE) is the primary fitness measure in this study.
Four basic mechanisms of the GA optimisation process work together to evolve the optimal solutions. The 
algorithm starts by randomly creating a community of weights and biases. In the selection phase, Roulette Wheel 
Selection is applied to obtain the most promising individuals (those with higher fitness values have a greater 
probability of selection). Next, select the genetic information followed by crossover, for which a single-point 
crossover is used with a probability of 0.97. Finally, with a probability of 0.01, mutations change random bits 
to maintain genetic diversity within the population. This evolutionary process repeats across generations, with 
each typically producing better solutions through the selective retention of superior individuals and the elimina-
tion of suboptimal ones. Against the backdrop of evolution, the purposeful blending of GA with neural networks 
illustrates a complementary optimisation strategy that juxtaposes and harnesses the global search qualities of evo-
lutionary algorithms with the modelling characteristics of neural networks. It combines the advantages of both 
approaches to deliver better performance on complex prediction problems by identifying optimal neural network 
weights and biases that yield high accuracy.
2.6.3 GA-ANN model development
Neural networks primarily rely on the proper choice of weights and biases, as these parameters significantly 
impact prediction accuracy. Misconfiguration of these essential parameters can cause the model to become stuck 
in local optima, leading to poor performance [ 48]. As with conventional neural networks, data passes through 
the network first, and weights and biases are adjusted in reverse [ 49]. This initialisation can cause significant 
inconsistencies in their behaviour, affecting prediction accuracy, since these parameters control how features are 
extracted from raw input data. To overcome these limitations, the use of the GA as a component of neural net -
works has been proposed as an effective approach to enhance the model’s generalisation ability [24]. The inclu-
sion of GA helps the optimisation process to find the optimal weights and biases for the network as a whole, thus 
improving the network’s performance. The overall system flow of GA-ANN optimisation is illustrated in Fig. 4. 
Next, the number of iterations (i.e., generations) in the ANN and GA components is independently initialised 
for the optimisation framework. The GA takes the initial weights and biases as its starting population. Then, the 
process iteratively follows systematic evolutionary steps: evaluating each population member’s fitness using a 
specified fitness function and performing genetic operations such as selection, crossover, and mutation. This evo-
lutionary cycle continues iteratively, with continuous reassessment of fitness, until specific termination criteria 
are met. Once these conditions are met, the optimised weights and biases are returned to the ANN as optimal 
values. This trained network, with the optimal parameters, can be used to make performance predictions.

2.8 Data preparation
The effectiveness of neural networks depends on their ability to learn mappings between samples and their cor -
responding targets. The performance and predictive ability of the network model depend heavily on the training 
and testing datasets, and these should be key considerations when tuning the model. The diverse range of sample 
data leads to significant fluctuations during the network learning and training phases, which can affect the model’s 
accuracy and reliability.
To address these obstacles, data preprocessing is becoming increasingly necessary. Several preprocessing 
methods can be applied to the experimental data, including min-max normalisation and z-score standardisa -
tion. In this study, the min-max normalisation technique is applied, which is widely recommended for machine 
learning, particularly neural networks, due to its compatibility with activation functions and its effectiveness in 
improving training stability and convergence. Conversely, Z-score standardisation is more suitable when the 
dataset is normally distributed, as it centres and scales the data based on statistical variance ([53]). The min-max 
technique normalises the data into a predefined range of −1 to 1, as defined by Eqs. (9) and (10). The network 
can learn the underlying architecture more effectively and stably by scaling all input parameters to a standard 
range. Therefore, the likely interval for normalisation is between − 1 and 1, as it preserves relationships among 
values while preventing smaller-scale variables from overflowing larger-scale variables during model training. 
Such standardisation mechanisms have been successfully used to accelerate convergence and improve the overall 
accuracy of neural network models.

3 Results and discussion
3.1 Preparation of hybrid nanofluid
Hybrid nanoparticle dispersions were prepared and characterised using a systematic two-step method at the 
Advanced Automotive Liquid Laboratory (A2LL), Faculty of Mechanical and Automotive Engineering Technol-
ogy at the Universiti Malaysia Pahang Al-Sultan Abdullah. In the first step, the ultrasonication method is used, as 
it is considered the most important method for preparing stable dispersions of hybrid nanoparticles [54]. For this 
study, the mixing period was 5 h (under ultrasonication) to achieve adequate particle fragmentation and suspen-
sion in the base fluid (without surfactant addition), and the ingredient concentration ranged from 0.01% to 0.2%. 
This period is consistent with the optimal sonication periods reported for hybrid nanofluid stability [ 55]. Addi-
tionally, the stability of the prepared hybrid nanofluids was assessed using a range of characterisation techniques 
to evaluate their long-term viability, including visual observation, sedimentation analysis, and zeta potential 
measurements [56]. A stable analysis of the study design enabled an operational focus on study preparation and 
informed sample selection based on long-term stability characteristics. A pre-preparation step was performed to 
identify the important parameters (including determining the concentration of GNP and CNC in the base fluid). 
However, there were standard (weights being calculated through Eq. (1) of NPs) methods for calculating volumes 
of both GNP and CNC through Equations ( 20) and (2), respectively. This approach is consistent with literature 
optimisation methods for hybrid nanofluid preparation.
Morphological characterisation was performed, particularly with scanning electron microscopy (SEM), to 
obtain a detailed surface inspection of the dispersion of graphene and CNC in the base fluids. The particle disper-
sion data under various magnifications are shown in Fig. 6, indicating good particle dispersion. Since the morpho-
logical understanding of hybrid nanofluids is crucial for improving their thermal and electrophysical properties 
[57], optimising their structural density and organisation is necessary. These numbers provide an adequate rep -
resentation of the distribution of the hybrid nanofluid and its respective structural composition. This enables 
surfactant-free hybrid nanofluids to be applied to practical applications. This suggests that prolonged sonication 
time is vital for obtaining stable dispersions, a key challenge in manufacturing the nanofluid [ 58]. Unlike many 
nanofluid formulations that rely on surfactants to enhance dispersion stability, the present hybrid nanofluid was 
prepared without the use of any chemical dispersion agent. Surfactants can improve nanoparticle suspension; 
however, they may also affect thermophysical properties, increase viscosity, promote foaming, and degrade at 
elevated temperatures. Therefore, achieving high colloidal stability in a surfactant-free system is advantageous 
for long-term thermal applications [59].

3.2 Thermogravimetric analysis
Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) is used to assess a material’s thermal stability by measuring mass changes 
as a function of temperature. The analysis examines material properties under various environmental conditions, 
providing valuable information for evaluating thermal stability. The TGA measurements of nanofluids of GNP-
EG/W and GNP/CNC-EG/W were performed at various volume concentrations in temperature ranges of 30 °C 
up to 500 °C (Fig. 7). GNP showed a different degradation pattern than GNP + CNC mixtures. There was a con-
siderable weight loss between 100 °C and 200 °C. For GNP (0.02–0.2%) and GNP + CNC (0.02–0.06%), a loss 
of weight around 130 °C and CNC concentrations (0.1–0.2%), a loss of weight near 150 °C was noticed, which 
is primarily an initial loss due to moisture evaporation from the samples [60].
A positive correlation between higher nanoparticle concentration and degradation temperature was demon -
strated. An increase in the volume concentration of GNP and GNP: CNC nanoparticles resulted in a noticeable 
shift toward higher degradation temperatures. These shifts in the TGA curves indicate higher thermal stability of 
the nanofluids compared to the base fluid (EG/W) and demonstrate improved temperature resistance with increas-
ing nanoparticle concentration. The third weight-loss event, occurring near 200 °C, is likely due to the removal 
of oxygenated functional groups from the nanoparticle surfaces [61]. The hydrophobic nature of both GNPs and 
CNCs can further explain the observed thermal behaviour. Furthermore, under air, TGA involves two competing 
and simultaneous processes that contribute oppositely to the weight: a weight loss associated with the oxidation 
of carbon, which produces gaseous CO 2, and a weight gain associated with the oxidation of residual metallic 
catalyst particles [62].

3.4.3 Zeta potential analysis
Zeta potential measurements of single and hybrid nanofluids were performed using an Anton Paar Litesizer 500 
(Germany) to assess the stability characteristics of the fluids. In this method, changes in the conductivity of a

the mechanisms of colloidal stability. The values of zeta potential measure the effective surface charge that par-
ticles have at the shear plane and can predict interparticle interactions in the suspension. The electrical potential

nanofluids exhibited considerable zeta potential, with most cases falling between − 73 mV and 82 mV . These

interparticle repulsion forces, inhibiting agglomeration phenomena [77].

3.8 GA configuration
A GA method creates an optimal ANN, primarily by optimising the population size parameter. This optimisation 
strategy is important because an earlier study showed that population size significantly affects GA performance 
[89]. Choosing an appropriate population size is an inherent compromise: smaller populations may converge on 
local optima. In comparison, larger populations may increase computational complexity and simulator runtime 
without proportional improvements in performance [ 43]. The empirical findings on model performance metrics 
are shown in Fig. 19(a), where evaluation was performed incrementally across population ranges of 50. A popula-
tion size of 250 achieved optimal performance, with an R² of 0.972, an RMSE of 0.968, and an MAE of 2.253. 
These values decrease significantly with reduced and expanded population sizes, indicating the importance of this 
parameter in the GA optimisation [90]. Hence, the population range was selected based on preliminary sensitivity 
analysis and convergence studies. Population sizes ranging from 50 to 250 were evaluated in increments of 50. 
It was observed that smaller populations were susceptible to premature convergence and local optima, whereas 
increases beyond a population size of 250 yielded only marginal improvements in model performance. Therefore, 
a population size of 250 was adopted as a compromise between optimisation accuracy and convergence stability.
Another critical factor, the number of generations, was also explored. As shown in Fig. 19(b), the model per-
formance presentation with a constant population size of 50 illustrates the positive effect of the generation count 
on model performance. The best results were obtained from testing in the range of 50 to 400 generations (incre -
ment: 50), yielding an R² of 0.986, an RMSE of 0.843, and an MAE of 2.67 for 250 generations (the optimal 
number of generations). This finding is consistent with research by Deb et al. [47], which suggests that reproduc-
ing the evolutionary process beyond this step yields rapidly diminishing returns in model accuracy, accompanied 
by an increasing computational burden. Additional GA parameters, including selection method (roulette wheel), 
crossover probability (0.7), mutation probability (0.1), and tournament range (3), were established based on pre-
liminary testing and are detailed comprehensively in Table 5. These parameters were selected in accordance with 
the best practices described by Houck et al. [91]. The combination of these optimised parameters yielded a GA-
ANN model with enhanced predictive capabilities for estimating the thermophysical properties of GNP-CNC/
EG-water hybrid nanofluids compared to conventional ANN approaches, consistent with the findings of Zhang 
et al. [92] and Hashmi et al. [21].

relation models provide explicit mathematical expressions that are simple, transparent, and easy to implement 
in engineering calculations. Second, correlations are computationally efficient and more accessible for real-time 
applications, preliminary design analyses, and situations with limited computational resources. Third, empiri -
cal correlations enhance interpretability by revealing direct relationships between input variables and output 
responses. This is particularly valuable for understanding the underlying physical trends. Finally, providing both 
approaches ensures broader usability of the study. The GA-ANN model is suitable for high-precision predictions, 
while the correlation models serve as practical tools for engineers and researchers who require rapid estimations 
with reasonable accuracy. Therefore, the correlation models are not intended to replace the GA-ANN model but 
to complement it by offering simplicity, interpretability, and ease of application, thereby increasing the overall 
utility and impact of the work.
3.11 Research significance
The application of hybrid nanofluids has attracted considerable attention for enhancing thermal management sys-
tems due to their superior heat transfer characteristics compared with conventional fluids. Among emerging nano-
fluid formulations, GNP-based hybrid nanofluids offer significant potential due to their high thermal conductivity 
and favourable stability. However, several knowledge gaps remain in the current literature. First, few studies 
have investigated the combined thermophysical behaviour of GNP-CNC hybrid nanofluids across a wide range 
of temperatures and nanoparticle concentrations. Second, most existing studies rely on conventional empirical 
correlations or standard ANNs, which often exhibit limited accuracy in capturing the complex nonlinear relation-
ships between operating parameters and thermophysical properties. Third, comprehensive optimisation of ANN 
architectures for predicting multiple thermophysical properties of hybrid nanofluids remains underexplored.
To address these gaps, the present study experimentally investigates the thermal conductivity and viscosity 
of highly stable GNP-CNC/EG-water hybrid nanofluids prepared using a surfactant-free two-step method. Fur -
thermore, a hybrid GA-ANN framework is developed to optimise network architecture, learning parameters, and 
prediction performance. By integrating experimental characterisation with intelligent computational modelling, 
this study provides a reliable and accurate methodology for predicting thermal conductivity and viscosity while 
reducing the need for extensive experimental measurements. The findings advance data-driven thermal-fluid 
research and support the development of efficient thermal management systems for engineering applications.
4 Conclusions
This study explores GNP-CNC/EG-water hybrid nanofluids and uses AI to predict their thermophysical proper -
ties. Stable GNP and GNP-CNC hybrid nanofluids were synthesised using a two-step method with prolonged 
ultrasonication. Findings are summarised below.
i) These nanofluids showed excellent stability over 60 days without surfactant, with zeta potentials from − 73 
to 82 mV confirming this. FESEM and TEM analyses validated nanoparticle dispersion and the formation 
of network structures.
ii) Thermophysical property measurements showed notable improvements in thermal transport. Thermal con -
ductivity increased with temperature and concentration, reaching a maximum of 29% at 0.2% volume and 
80 °C, due to percolation and increased Brownian motion.
iii) Viscosity studies showed moderate increases with nanoparticle volume fraction (from 23 to 25.5% at 0.2%) 
and decreases with rising temperature due to weaker intermolecular forces. GNP-CNC hybrid nanofluids had 
a slightly higher viscosity than pure GNP nanofluids but remained practical, balancing heat-transfer benefits 
and pumping power requirements.

iv) A hybrid GA-ANN model was developed to predict thermophysical properties accurately. The optimal setup 
involved two hidden layers (56–32 neurons) and was optimised for the architecture, parameters, and training 
functions. The best models achieved R² of 0.998 for thermal conductivity and viscosity across the experi -
mental domain.
v) The GA-driven model outperformed standard ANN methods, reducing mean relative error by 28.4% for 
thermal conductivity and 51.4% for viscosity. Empirical correlations from nonlinear regression offer a sim-
pler engineering alternative, with R2 values of 0.968 and 0.975.
vi) This work presents a stable, surfactant-free hybrid nanofluid as an eco-friendly, cost-effective thermal man-
agement solution. The GA-ANN framework advances predictive modelling of complex nanofluids, reducing 
experimental effort and aiding the design of nanofluids for specific applications.
vii) Despite promising results, the study was limited to specific concentration (0.01–0.2%) and temperature 
(30–80 °C) ranges and did not consider particle size or surface chemistry. Future work should expand the 
experimental domain, include additional physicochemical parameters, assess real-time heat transfer, explore 
ternary systems such as ZnO-TiO2-SiC, and conduct techno-economic and life-cycle analyses to support 
industrial adoption.
This research enhances thermal management through the use of nanomaterials and AI. The new surfactant-free 
hybrid nanofluids and the GA-ANN model show promise for automotive, electronics, renewable energy, and 
industrial cooling applications, supporting sustainable energy efforts. It highlights the potential of hybrid nano -
materials and computational methods for complex thermal energy issues.
