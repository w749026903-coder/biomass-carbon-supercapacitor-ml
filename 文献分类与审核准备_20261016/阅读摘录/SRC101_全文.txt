ABSTRACT: The rapid demand for sustainable, high-performance energy
storage has accelerated the development of biomass-derived electrode materials
for next-generation supercapacitors. However, the intrinsically low electrical
conductivity and limited electrochemical activity of many metal oxide electrodes
remain major challenges that restrict their practical application. In this work, a
novel Fe-doped ZnO/activated carbon composite derived from oil palm empty
fruit bunches (Fe-ZnO/AC) was successfully synthesized via a hydrothermal
method and employed as an electrode material for asymmetric supercapacitors.
ZnO was selected for its low-cost, abundance, nontoxicity, and facile synthesis,
while Fe doping was introduced to enhance electrical conductivity, charge-
transfer kinetics, and redox activity. Simultaneously, activated carbon derived
from empty fruit bunch (EFB) waste provides a conductive, porous carbon
matrix that facilitates ion diffusion and electron transport. This study highlights
the synergistic integration of Fe-doped ZnO with activated carbon, enabling simultaneous enhancement of conductivity,
electrochemical performance, structural stability, and sustainability within a single-electrode system, a strategy that remains rarely
explored for asymmetric supercapacitor applications. The Fe-ZnO/AC electrode exhibited a high specific capacitance of 458.2 F g
−1
at 0.1 A g
−1
in a three-electrode configuration. In an asymmetric two-electrode configuration, the device delivered specific
capacitances of 179.7 and 102.3 F g
−1
for the coin cell and flexible cell, respectively. The corresponding energy densities reached 35.9
and 20.5 W h kg
−1
with power densities of 239.7 and 240.4 W kg
−1
, respectively. These findings demonstrate the excellent
electrochemical performance, stability, and energy storage capability of the Fe-ZnO/AC composite, highlighting its strong potential
as a low-cost, sustainable, and high-performance electrode material for next-generation asymmetric supercapacitors.
1. INTRODUCTION
The global demand for high-performance, environmentally
friendly, and efficient energy storage systems continues to rise
in line with the rapid advancement of modern technologies.
Among various options, supercapacitors have attracted
significant attention due to their density, rapid charge−
discharge capability, and excellent cycling stability compared
to conventional batteries.
1−5
However, their relatively low
energy density remains a limitation.
6
Consequently, the
development of advanced electrode materials capable of
simultaneously delivering high capacitance and long-term
stability has become a research focus. In this context, transition
metal oxides and porous carbon materials are emerging as
promising candidates, as they offer complementary charge-
storage pseudocapacitance and electric double-layer capaci-
tance (EDLC) mechanisms, respectively.
7−9
Supercapacitors generally store energy through two primary
mechanisms, EDLC and pseudocapacitance. In EDLC systems,
charge storage occurs through the electrostatic accumulation of
electrolyte ions at the electrode/electrolyte interface without
involving faradaic reactions, enabling excellent power capability
and long-term cycling stability.
10,11
Porous carbon materials,
including activated carbon, graphene, and biomass-derived
carbon, are widely utilized as EDLC electrodes due to their
high electrical conductivity, large accessible surface area, and
hierarchical pore structures. In contrast, pseudocapacitive
materials store charge through rapid and reversible faradaic
reactions involving surface redox processes, providing higher
specific capacitance than conventional carbon-based electro-
des. Transition metal oxides have attracted considerable
attention as pseudocapacitive materials because of their
multiple oxidation states and rich redox activity. However,
their practical applications are often limited by poor electrical
conductivity and structural instability during repeated charge−
discharge cycles.
12
Therefore, integrating transition metal
oxides with porous carbon materials represents an effective
strategy to combine the high capacitance contribution of

Zinc oxide (ZnO) is considered one of the most promising
metal oxides due to its natural abundance, low cost, non-
toxicity, and notable redox activity. However, its poor electrical
conductivity and limited electrochemical stability due to
structural degradation and residual impurities significantly
restrict its electrochemical performance.
14−24
To overcome
these limitations, two widely adopted approaches include metal
doping and composite formation with a conductive carbon
matrix. Among various dopants, iron (Fe) has been shown to
effectively enhance the electrical conductivity and charge
carrier concentration of ZnO. Kumar et al. (2023) reported
that Fe-doped ZnO exhibited a specific capacitance of 286 F
g
−1
in a three-electrode configuration, demonstrating the
positive effect of Fe incorporation on charge transfer kinetics.
25
In another study, integration of ZnO with activated carbon
(AC) was widely investigated to enhance surface area and
facilitate ion transport.
26

27
reported a
ZnO/AC nanocomposite with a specific capacitance of 160 F
g
−1
, while Yadav et al. (2022) obtained 77.7 F g
−1
from a ZnO-
AC composite derived from walnut shell waste.
28
These
highlight the synergistic contribution of the EDLC behavior of
carbon and the pseudocapacitive properties of ZnO, although
the overall performance remains moderate.
In recent work, Saka and Levent (2024) demonstrated that
nitrogen- and ZnO-doped carbon particles derived from waste
biomass can improve electrochemical performance, yielding
high-capacitance electrodes suitable for energy storage
applications.
29
Recent studies have demonstrated that oil
palm empty fruit bunch (EFB)-derived activated carbon is a
highly promising electrode material for supercapacitors owing
to its naturally abundant carbon content and well-developed
porous structure. Yulianti et al. reported hierarchically porous
EFB-derived activated carbon with a specific surface area of
1215 m
2
g
−1
and an outstanding specific capacitance of 459.28
F g
−1
, where the presence of naturally occurring SiO
2
enhanced
electrode wettability.
30
More recently, biomass-derived acti-
vated carbon prepared from EFB achieved a specific surface
area of up to 1375 m
2
g
−1
, delivering a specific capacitance of
346 F g
−1
together with 82.9% capacitance retention after 5000
charge−discharge cycles, further confirming the excellent
potential of EFB-derived carbon for sustainable electro-
chemical energy storage.
31
Despite these encouraging advances, most reported studies
have focused on optimizing the porous carbon framework itself
or introducing heteroatom functionalities to improve the
capacitive performance. The combination of EFB-derived
activated carbon with Fe-doped ZnO to simultaneously exploit
the high electrical conductivity and large surface area of
biomass-derived carbon together with the enhanced pseudo-
capacitive activity of Fe-modified ZnO has rarely been
investigated. This represents an important opportunity for
the development of low-cost, sustainable, and high-perform-
ance electrode materials.
In this study, Fe-ZnO/AC composites were synthesized via a
hydrothermal method and comprehensively evaluated in both
three-electrode and asymmetric two-electrode configurations.
The results demonstrate that the synergistic effects of Fe
doping and ZnO-carbon hybridization with a locally abundant,
waste-derived carbon matrix significantly enhance the redox
activity, electrical conductivity, and cycling durability. Distinct
from previous studies, this work integrates Fe doping and
ZnO-carbon hybridization within an EFB-based activated
carbon matrix, achieving improved electrochemical perform-
ance through a fully biomass-supported material architecture.
This integrated approach offers an effective and sustainable
material design approach for the development of high-
performance materials with enhanced electrochemical proper-
ties for advanced energy-storage applications.

2.4. Synthesis of Fe-ZnO/AC Composite
The composite of Fe-ZnO/AC powders was prepared by dispersing
Fe-doped ZnO and AC in a solution containing CTAB, isopropanol,
and urea, maintaining the same ratio of CTAB and isopropanol as
used in the Fe-doped ZnO synthesis. The resulting suspension was
then transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and
subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 150 °C for 2 h. After
completion of the process, the obtained composite was thoroughly
rinsed several times with DI water and subsequently dried at 105 °C
for 24 h to yield the final Fe-ZnO/AC composite (Figure 1). To
investigate the effect of Fe-doped ZnO loading, composites containing
4%, 8%, 15%, and 25% relative to the mass of EFB were synthesized.
The resulting samples were designated as 4%-Fe-ZnO/AC, 8%-Fe-
ZnO/AC, 15%-Fe-ZnO/AC, and 25%-Fe-ZnO/AC, respectively.

4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
4.1. Structural Analysis
XRD analysis was conducted to investigate the crystalline
structure and phase composition of the synthesized samples.
The XRD pattern of the Fe-doped ZnO sample exhibits a
series of lattice diffraction peaks corresponding to the
hexagonal wurtzite structure of ZnO, including the (100),
(002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), (201), (004),
and (203) planes, which are consistent with the COD (2014)
reference database,
40
as shown in Figure 2a. No additional
diffraction peaks associated with secondary phases are
detected, as shown in Figure 2a. No additional diffraction
peaks associated with secondary phases are detected, indicating
that the primary crystal structure remains wurtzite ZnO after
Fe incorporation. A direct XRD curve comparison between
pristine ZnO and Fe-doped ZnO under identical conditions
(Figure 2b) reveals a slight shift in several diffraction peaks,
with a 2θ displacement of approximately 0.02°−0.04°. Such
peak shifts are commonly attributed to lattice distortion
induced by dopant incorporation. The partial substitution of
Zn
2+
ions by Fe ions within the ZnO lattice modifies the lattice
parameters due to differences in ionic radius and local
electronic interactions, leading to measurable changes in
diffraction angle.
41
To further verify the structural effect of Fe incorporation,
the lattice parameters were calculated from the XRD data. The
lattice parameters slightly increased from a = 3.252 Å and c =
5.210 Å for pristine ZnO to a = 3.253 Å and c = 5.212 Å for
Fe-doped ZnO, accompanied by a slight increase in the unit
cell volume from 47.724 to 47.771 Å. These results are
consistent with the slight shift of the diffraction peaks toward
lower 2θ values (Figure 2b), indicating a minor increase in the
interplanar spacing (d-spacing) after Fe incorporation. The
observed variation in lattice parameters further supports the
successful incorporation of Fe into the ZnO lattice,
accompanied by slight lattice distortion without altering the
wurtzite crystal structure.
42
Furthermore, the absence of impurity phases is further
supported by the XRD pattern of the Fe precursor synthesized
under the same conditions, which shows the formation of
crystalline Fe
2
O
3
(Figure S1). This indicates that any iron
species introduced during doping are fully incorporated or
highly dispersed within the ZnO lattice rather than forming
separate secondary phases in the Fe-doped ZnO sample. The
systematic peak shift combined with the absence of secondary
phases confirms that Fe ions are successfully incorporated
substitutionally into the ZnO lattice, indicating effective
doping rather than phase segregation.
43
The crystallite size
calculated using the Williamson−Hall (WH) method shows
that pristine ZnO possesses an average crystallite size of 57.8
nm, whereas Fe-doped ZnO exhibits a smaller crystallite size of
53.3 nm. This reduction suggests that Fe incorporation inhibits
crystal growth, likely due to lattice distortion induced by ionic
substitution.
44
Furthermore, peak broadening analysis reveals
that the lattice strain slightly increases from 4.00 × 10
−4
for
pristine ZnO to 4.25 × 10
−4
for Fe-doped ZnO. This increase
in strain indicates the presence of additional internal distortion
within the crystal lattice, which can be attributed to the
substitution of Zn
2+
ions by Fe ions with different ionic radii.
These results confirm that Fe doping is successfully
incorporated into the ZnO lattice, leading to modified

For the Fe-ZnO/AC composites (4%, 8%, and 15%), the
ZnO diffraction peaks remain observable but with reduced
intensity compared to the Fe-doped ZnO sample (Figure 2a).
This decrease in intensity is attributed to the presence of
amorphous activated carbon (AC), which lowers the overall
crystallinity of the composite system. In addition, all composite
samples exhibit two broad diffraction features centered at
approximately 2θ ≈ 24° and 43°, corresponding to the (002)
and (100) planes of graphitic carbon, respectively. These broad
peaks are characteristic of amorphous or turbostratic carbon
and are consistent with previously reported XRD patterns of
EFB-derived carbon.
30
The coexistence of broadened carbon-
related peaks and retained ZnO diffraction peaks confirms the
successful dispersion of Fe-doped ZnO within the activated
carbon matrix. Moreover, the absence of additional crystalline
phases in the composite patterns indicates that the structural
integrity of Fe-doped ZnO is preserved during composite
formation.
In contrast, the AC sample exhibits a broad diffraction
feature centered around 22°, which is typically associated with
the amorphous carbon structure and partially disordered
graphitic domains of biomass-derived activated carbon.
Interestingly, the intensity of this broad carbon-related feature
gradually decreases with increasing Fe-doped ZnO incorpo-
ration and becomes nearly indistinguishable in the 15%-Fe-
ZnO/AC composite. This behavior suggests that the crystalline
ZnO phase progressively dominates the diffraction response as
the oxide loading increases, while the carbon signal becomes
relatively masked due to the higher crystallinity and stronger
scattering intensity of the ZnO component. The XRD patterns
of the Fe-doped ZnO/AC composites are predominantly
governed by the characteristic diffraction peaks of the ZnO
crystalline phase, as the incorporation of activated carbon does
not disrupt the ZnO crystal structure.
FESEM analysis was performed to examine the surface
morphology and microstructural features of the synthesized
Fe-doped ZnO samples. The Fe-doped ZnO sample reveals a
three-dimensional nanoflower-like morphology, composed of
numerous thin nanosheet-like petals that assemble into a
layered, flower-shaped architecture (Figure 3a,b). This
hierarchical nanoflower structure provides a high surface area
and abundant active sites, which are highly beneficial for
facilitating redox reactions, making it promising for super-
capacitor applications. In the Fe-ZnO/AC composite (Figure
3c−e), the FESEM images show the presence of porous
activated carbon derived from EFB, characterized by its
irregular pore network. Embedded within this carbon matrix,
the nanoflower-like Fe-doped ZnO with its distinct petal-like
nanosheets remains clearly visible (Figure 3c−e). This
indicates that the morphology of Fe-doped ZnO is preserved
after compositing without structural deformation or collapse.
The increase in the loading mass of Fe-doped ZnO led to a
higher distribution of Fe-doped ZnO particles on the carbon
matrix surface, accompanied by the partial coverage of the
carbon pores without causing significant changes in the overall
morphology of the composite. The coexistence of the porous
carbon and intact Fe-doped ZnO nanoflowers confirms that
the composite formation involves physical integration while
maintaining the original architecture of Fe-doped ZnO.
The EDX analysis in weight percent (Figure 3f) confirmed
the presence of Fe, Zn, and O elements in the Fe-doped ZnO
sample, indicating the successful formation of the Fe-doped
ZnO material. Meanwhile, the Fe-ZnO/AC composite samples
(4%, 8%, and 15%) exhibited the presence of Fe, Zn, O, C, and
Si elements, which originated from the Fe-doped ZnO particles
and the activated carbon derived from EFB. These results
verify the successful incorporation and distribution of Fe-
doped ZnO onto the AC matrix in all composite samples,
which is also in good agreement with the elemental mapping
results shown in Figure S4.
The observed Si content does not exhibit a linear trend with
the carbon content, which decreases as Fe-doped ZnO loading
increases. This behavior is likely attributed to the heteroge-
neous distribution of naturally occurring silica within the
biomass-derived carbon matrix.
33,46
In addition, the O content
shows a tendency similar to that of the Si content, suggesting
that part of the detected oxygen originates from residual silica
(SiO
x
). Therefore, local variations in silica distribution,
together with the localized nature of EDX analysis, may lead
to fluctuations in both the Si and O contents among the
samples.

4.2. Electrochemical Properties of the Electrodes in the
Three-Electrode System
The CV profile of the Fe-ZnO/AC composites, recorded at
scan rates ranging from 5 mV s
−1
to 100 mV s
−1
(Figure 7b−

(Figure S6d), indicating that the charge storage process is
predominantly governed by EDLC, as presented in Figure 7b−
e. The overall shape, even at higher scan rates, suggests
favorable charge propagation and good electrochemical
reversibility within the electrode structure. In contrast, Fe-
doped ZnO itself exhibits weaker electrochemical activity,
accompanied by intermediate capacitive behavior (Figure S6a).
The incorporation of Fe into the ZnO lattice induces structural
defects and additional active sites, which contribute to a mild
pseudocapacitive redox reaction compared to pristine ZnO
(Figure S6c), where the redox activity remains minimal (Figure
S6b).
25
Among the investigated samples, the 8%-Fe-ZnO/AC
composite (Figure 7c) exhibits a slightly larger CV area than
that of the 4%-Fe-ZnO/AC (Figure 7b), suggesting improved
charge storage capacitance. A more pronounced expansion in
the CV area is observed for the 15%-Fe-ZnO/AC composite
(Figure 7d), indicating enhanced charge storage at extended
ion diffusion times. This trend demonstrates that increasing
the Fe dopant from 4% to 15% contributes additional
capacitive behavior while maintaining EDLC as the dominant
mechanism of the carbon framework. The gradual enlargement
of the CV area further confirms the synergistic interaction
between Fe-doped ZnO and the AC matrix. However, when
the Fe-doped ZnO loading is increased to 25%, the
electrochemical response deteriorates noticeably (Figure 7a).
The CV area decreases and becomes comparable to that of the
4%-Fe-ZnO/AC sample, indicating a reduction in the effective
capacitance. This behavior may be attributed to excessive Fe-
ZnO accumulation, which can partially block the conductive
pathways and hinder electrolyte ion transport within the
porous carbon network. Consequently, the balance among
conductivity, ion diffusion, and active site accessibility becomes
less favorable at higher Fe-ZnO contents.
To clarify this behavior, electrochemical impedance spec-
troscopy (EIS) analysis of all samples was performed and
compared with ZnO and Fe-doped ZnO. The Nyquist plots
shown in Figure 7f reveal that all samples exhibit relatively low
overall impedance. Nevertheless, a gradual shift of the intercept
along the real axis (Z′) toward higher values in the real
impedance region is observed with increasing Fe-doped ZnO
loading (Figure 7f). The ESR values were determined to be
1.66 Ω for 4%-Fe-ZnO/AC, 1.73 Ω for 8%-Fe-ZnO/AC, 1.94
Ω for 15%-Fe-ZnO/AC, and 1.96 Ω for 25%-Fe-ZnO/AC,
indicating a gradual rise in the overall resistive response.
Furthermore, the 25%-Fe-ZnO/AC composite shows a
pronounced increase in the imaginary component (Z″),
particularly in the low-frequency region, suggesting hindered
ion diffusion and slower charge transfer kinetics.
42
This
interpretation is supported by the EIS response of Fe-doped
ZnO alone (Figure S7a), which exhibits a more resistive
characteristic compared to AC (Figure S7b). These results
confirm that Fe-doped ZnO, as a semiconducting metal oxide,
possesses a higher intrinsic electrical resistivity, whereas the
carbon matrix boosts electron transport in the electrode
system. Therefore, increasing the Fe-doped ZnO fraction in
the composite reduces electronic conductivity and impedes
efficient charge transport, leading to the observed decrease in
CV area and electrochemical performance at the 25%
composition.

Mechanistically, the Fe−C bonds act as robust interfacial
bridges that covalently anchor the Fe-doped ZnO nano-
particles onto the activated carbon framework, thereby
strengthening the coupling between the active components
and the conductive carbon matrix. During repeated charge−
discharge processes, this strong interfacial interaction effec-
tively mitigates volumetric stress induced by continuous ion
insertion and extraction, restricting nanoparticle detachment,
agglomeration, and pulverization while suppressing irreversible
structural degradation. The intimate interfacial coupling, as
evidenced by the seamless phase boundaries observed in the
HRTEM analysis (Figure 4), also establishes efficient electron-
transfer pathways between the Fe redox centers and the carbon
framework, preserving electrical contact and facilitating rapid
charge transport throughout prolonged cycling.
50−52
Con-
sequently, the strengthened Fe−C interfacial coupling
enhances redox reversibility, maintains the structural integrity
of the electrode, and improves capacitance retention during
long-term operation. This interpretation is consistent with
previous studies demonstrating that Fe−C and Fe−N−C
interfacial bonding plays a crucial role in stabilizing electrode
microstructures, promoting charge-transfer kinetics, and
preventing the degradation of active sites during extended
electrochemical cycling.

The accumulated mechanical stress over thousands of cycles
can induce structural fatigue, including partial pulverization of
the nanoparticles and weakening of their attachment to the
activated carbon matrix. Consequently, the deterioration of
interparticle contact and conductive pathways contributes to
the increased ESR observed after cycling.
In contrast, the relatively stable and slightly decreased R
ct
after prolonged cycling highlights the importance of the Fe−C
interfacial interaction in maintaining charge-transfer kinetics.
The strong covalent Fe−C anchoring bonds effectively
preserve intimate contact between the Fe-containing active
species and the conductive carbon framework, preventing
severe interfacial separation and maintaining accessible
electron-transfer pathways during repeated cycling. Therefore,
although some structural degradation occurs within the
pseudocapacitive component, the stabilized interface helps
prevent catastrophic electrode failure and preserves electro-
chemical reversibility. Similar roles of metal−carbon interfacial
interactions in improving structural stability and charge-
transfer behavior have been reported for advanced composite
electrodes.
53
The capacitance retention of 72% after long-term cycling
(Figure 9e) therefore results from the combined effects of
gradual degradation of Fe-doped ZnO, partial loss of
electrochemically active sites, and increased internal resist-
ance.
59
The FESEM images after the durability test further
support this interpretation (Figure S3). Compared with the
pristine electrode (Figure S3a,c), the cycled electrode exhibits
noticeable surface cracking and increased fragmentation of Fe-
doped ZnO particles (Figure S3b,d), indicating structural
damage caused by repeated faradaic reactions and volume
variation. These morphological changes may reduce the active-
site availability and weaken electrical contact within the
composite electrode, ultimately contributing to capacitance
decay. Nevertheless, the retained capacitance demonstrates
that the Fe-ZnO/AC composite maintains a considerable
structural integrity and electrochemical reversibility after
prolonged cycling. These degradation phenomena, including
structural instability, active material loss, and interfacial
deterioration, are widely recognized as the primary origins of
capacitance fading in pseudocapacitive electrode materi-
als.

derived from oil palm empty fruit bunches (Fe-ZnO/AC) was
successfully synthesized via a hydrothermal method and
evaluated as an electrode material for asymmetric super-
capacitors. The synergistic combination of Fe-doped ZnO and
biomass-derived activated carbon significantly enhanced the
