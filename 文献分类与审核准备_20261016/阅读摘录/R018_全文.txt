Biomass‑derived carbon electrodes from sugarcane bagasse 
for supercapacitor applications
Kajal Gautam1 · Mohit Bhatt2 · Akarsh Verma3  · Anil Kumar Sinha2
Received: 21 January 2025 / Revised: 8 March 2025 / Accepted: 19 March 2025 / Published online: 26 March 2025

Abstract
This research investigates the application of carbon materials derived from sugarcane bagasse, a form of biomass, for the 
advancement of supercapacitor electrodes. Carbon electrodes were synthesized by carbonizing biomass waste at 500 °C with 
varying durations (3, 4, 5, and 10 h) and pre-treatment using glacial acetic acid, HCl, and NaOH. The resulting materials were 
designated as C-1, C-2, C-3, and C-4 respectively. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to analyze the structural properties 
of the materials, while Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy were used 
to evaluate their optical characteristics. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to examine the morphology and 
structural characteristics of all samples. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to surface chemistry, and 
elemental composition of materials. Electrochemical performance was assessed using cyclic voltammetry (CV), charging 
discharge, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). Sample C-4, synthesized at 500 °C for 10 h, exhibited the 
best electrochemical performance, with high specific capacitances of 226.7 F/g at 1 mV/s, excellent rate capability with 65% 
capacitance retention at 100 mV/s, and excellent cyclic stability and coulombic efficiency of 100% over 5000 cycles. These 
findings underscore the effectiveness of biomass-derived carbon electrode for high performance supercapacitor applications.
Keywords Biomass-derived carbon · Sugarcane bagasse · Supercapacitors · Electrochemical performance
1 Introduction
Supercapacitors, also known as electrochemical capacitors, 
have gained substantial recognition for their exceptional 
power density, rapid charge–discharge capabilities, and 
impressive cycle life, positioning them as critical compo-
nents in energy storage technologies [1 ]. Unlike conven-
tional batteries, supercapacitors excel in applications that 
demand swift energy delivery and high-power output, such 
as regenerative braking systems in electric vehicles, grid 
energy storage, and various consumer electronics [2 , 3]. 
Supercapacitors, unlike the conventional capacitors, deliver 
significantly more energy density because of adsorption of 
charges during charging cycle. This is unlike the storage 
mechanism in conventional capacitors, which store energy 
only by the electrical polarization of dielectric [4]. Carbon-
based materials are extensively employed as electrodes in 
supercapacitors due to their high electrical conductivity, 
large surface area, and excellent electrochemical stability 
[5–7]. These properties are crucial for achieving high capac-
itance and efficient charge storage. Various forms of carbon, 
like activated carbons [8], carbon nanotubes (CNT) [9], gra-
phene [10], reduced graphene oxide [11], and carbon-based 
composites [5 ], have been used as electrodes for superca-
pacitors, each offering unique advantages and limitations [5, 
12]. Activated carbons, derived from various sources includ-
ing biomass, are the most common electrode materials for 
supercapacitors. Activated carbon, also known as activated 
charcoal or activated coal, is highly porous form of carbon 
with high surface area exceeding 2000  m2/g [13], tuneable 
pore morphology, and amenable for surfaces functionalized,

Additionally, these materials find applications in water/air

Carbon nanotubes and graphene offer superior electrical 
conductivity and mechanical strength, making them suit -
able for high-performance supercapacitors [5 , 21, 22]. The 
sustainability of energy storage materials is an increasing 
concern, and biomass-derived carbon materials are emerging 
as an eco-friendly alternative [15, 23]. Sugarcane bagasse, a

biomass source that can be converted into carbon materials 
through carbonization. This process not only recycles agri-

derived from bio-waste, which contributes to sustainability 
and reduces material cost [26]. Second, the synthesis process 
allows morphology engineering like pore size and surface 
functionalisation to maximize device charge storage capac-
ity. Finally, the use of biomass reduces environmental impact 
by lowering carbon footprints and mitigating greenhouse 
gas emissions associated with traditional carbon production 
[24, 27].
Biomass-derived carbon materials, such as those 
obtained from sugarcane bagasse, grass, wood, and other 
plant-based sources, have emerged as promising electrode 
materials for supercapacitors due to their high surface area, 
abundant functional groups, and sustainable nature [ 23, 28, 
29]. These biomass-derived carbons often possess a hierar -
chical porous structure, which enhances ion diffusion and 
electrolyte accessibility, leading to superior electrochemi-
cal performance [30]. Additionally, the intrinsic heteroatom 
doping (e.g., oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur) in these materials 
improves charge storage capacity by introducing pseudo-
capacitive behavior and increasing electrical conductivity 
[31]. Studies have demonstrated that sugarcane bagasse-
derived activated carbon exhibits a high specific capaci-
tance and excellent cyclic stability, making it a competitive 
alternative to conventional carbon-based electrodes [13, 
15]. Furthermore, grass-derived biochar has been shown to 
deliver outstanding electrochemical performance due to its 
high graphitic content and tunable porosity [32]. The renew-
able and cost-effective nature of biomass-derived carbons 
makes them highly attractive for sustainable energy stor -
age applications in supercapacitors [ 28, 32]. In this quest, 
Anand et al. [33] synthesized heteroatom-doped activated 
carbon from walnut shells, achieving a high specific sur -
face area and a hierarchical pore structure that enhanced 
ion transport and electrochemical performance. Their mate-
rial exhibited a gravimetric capacitance of 271.4 F/g at 0.5 
A/g. In another work, Alwi et al. [34] investigated waste 
sugarcane bagasse (WSB) as a cost-effective and sustain-
able precursor for porous carbon in supercapacitors. To 
enhance its electrochemical performance, they hybridized 
WSB-derived carbon with silicon dioxide (SiO₂), a low-cost 
pseudocapacitive material. The optimized nanocomposite 
electrode demonstrated a high specific capacitance of 362.3 
F g⁻ 1 at 0.25 A/g, demonstrating the potential of WSB-
derived composites for high-performance energy storage 
applications. Singh et al. [35] developed a series of highly 
porous sulfur-doped activated carbon (WSC/S) derived 
from sugarcane bagasse. The WSC/S exhibited a large 
specific surface area with high mesoporosity, enhanced 
wettability, and abundant redox-active sites, which con-
tributed to superior supercapacitor performance. The high 
mesopore content facilitated efficient ion transport, ena -
bling the WSC/S electrode to achieve a remarkable spe-
cific capacitance of 282.25 F g⁻ 1 at 0.1 A g⁻ 1 with excel-
lent cycling stability, retaining over 96% capacitance after 
10,000 cycles. These materials demonstrate the potential of 
biomass-derived materials as efficient energy storage solu-
tions for energy storage applications. In this study, activated 
(AC) carbon electrodes were synthesized from sugarcane 
bagasse by treatment with glacial acetic acid, hydrochloric 
acid, and sodium hydroxide to remove impurities from the 
raw material and then carbonizing the biomass at 500 °C 
for different time durations 3, 4, 5, and 10 h. The resulting 
electrode materials were designated as C-1 (500 °C, 3 h), 
C-2 (500 °C, 4 h), C-3 (500 °C, 5 h), and C-4 (500 °C, 
10 h). The structural properties of the carbon materials 
were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD), revealing a 
graphitic structure of material. Fourier-transform infrared 
(FTIR) provided insights into the functional groups and 
photoluminescence (PL) provides optical characteristics of 
the materials. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) con-
firmed that sample C-4, synthesized at 500 °C for 10 h, 
exhibits a crossed sheet-like morphology. This morphol-
ogy is consistent with the characteristics of hard carbon, 
which is beneficial for enhancing ion transport and elec-
trochemical performance in energy storage devices such 
as batteries or supercapacitors. Hard carbon typically has 
a disordered structure with micro- and mesopores, facili-
tating the insertion and extraction of ions during charge 
and discharge cycles. The crossed sheet-like morphology 
provides a large surface area, improving electrolyte access 
and reducing the diffusion path for ions, thereby enhancing 
electrochemical performance. Electrochemical tests dem-
onstrated that sample C-4 achieved specific capacitances 
of 226.71 F/g at 1 mV/s and 147.80 F/g at 10 A/g, surpass-
ing the performance of several other biomass-derived car -
bon materials reported in the literature (shown in Table  1). 
Carbon electrodes derived from agricultural residues gen-
erally exhibit lower capacitance compared to the perfor -
mance of our materials [36]. Additionally, carbon materials

2  Preparation of sugarcane bagasse‑derived 
activated charcoal
Step 1: Initial charcoal preparation
Sugarcane bagasse-derived activated carbon was treated 
to remove impurities and reduce ash content. The procedure 
involved mixing 1 g of the charcoal with 3 mL of glacial ace-
tic acid. This mixture was left to react, allowing the acetic 
acid to dissolve impurities and ash components. After the 
reaction, the charcoal was filtered, washed thoroughly with 
deionized water to remove residual acid, and then oven-dried 
at 100 °C for 2 h. Schematic of synthesis procedure has 
shown in Fig.  1.
Step 2: Chemical activation
The dried charcoal underwent chemical activation to 
improve its porosity and surface characteristics. An equimo-
lar solution of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and hydrochloric 
acid (HCl) was prepared, ensuring balanced molarity. The 
charcoal was immersed in this solution, allowing the chemi-
cals to react with the carbon structure. The mixture was then 
heated in an oven at 100 °C for 3 h to facilitate activation.
Step 3: Carbonization
Following chemical activation, the treated charcoal was 
transferred to a ceramic boat for carbonization. The boat was 
placed inside a tubular furnace under a nitrogen atmosphere 
to prevent oxidation during the carbonization process. The 
furnace was heated at a controlled rate of 5 °C/min until 
reaching the target temperature of 500 °C. The carboniza-
tion was conducted at this temperature for varying durations 
to study the effect of time on the properties of the activated 
charcoal.
Step 4: Post-carbonization treatment
After the carbonization process was completed, the 
furnace was allowed to cool naturally to room tempera-
ture while maintaining the nitrogen atmosphere. This step 
ensured that the samples did not oxidize upon cooling. The 
resulting activated charcoal samples were then collected and 
labeled based on the carbonization time:

3  Characterization
The carbonized samples were characterized using several 
advanced techniques. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, 
Make: JEOL, Model: JSM-7610 F Plus) was utilized to 
observe the surface morphology, and energy-dispersive 
X-ray (EDX) analysis was conducted for elemental composi-
tion analysis. X-ray diffraction (XRD, Make: Bruker, Model: 
D8 Advanced ECO) provided information on the crystalline 
structure, while photoluminescence (PL, Model: LAMBDA, 
Make: Perkin Elmer) analysis was used to study the optical 
properties. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, 
PerkinElmer Frontier) was employed to identify the func-
tional groups present on the carbon surface. Additionally, 
X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS, Make: Thermo 
Fisher Scientific, Model: K-Alpha) was used to analyze 
the surface chemical composition and bonding states of 
the material. For electrochemical analysis, cyclic voltam-
metry (CV) was used to measure specific capacitance and 
assess the electrochemical stability. These electrochemical 
measurements were carried out using a biologic potentiostat 
(Model: SP-150e) in a three-electrode setup.
4  Electrode preparation
The working electrode was prepared by mixing active mate-
rial, carbon black, and PVDF in an 8:1:1 ratio, respectively 
using a mortar and pestle. The mixture was then dispersed 
in ethanol and subjected to sonication for 8 h to ensure uni-
form dispersion. After proper mixing, a viscous solution 
was obtained and drop-casted onto a carbon paper substrate 
(1 × 1  cm2) using a pipette. The coated electrodes were then 
dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C overnight to remove resid-
ual solvent. Once dried, the electrodes were directly used 
as working electrodes for electrochemical measurements. 
Platinum mesh served as the counter electrode, and a calo-
mel electrode was used as the reference electrode in 1 M 
KOH electrolyte.

5.3  Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
SEM for all samples (C-4, C-3, C-2, and C-4 have shown in 
Fig.  4a, b, c, and d respectively) was employed to analyze the 
morphology of the activated carbon material, revealing the 
influence of synthesis time on their structural characteristics. 
SEM imaging provides detailed views of surface features at 
a microscale, which is crucial for evaluating electrode mor-
phology [62]. The SEM images demonstrate that longer syn-
thesis times result in the development of layered structure in 
the carbon electrodes (sample C-4) as shown in Fig. 4a. This 
layered morphology is particularly advantageous for super-
capacitor applications due to its significant enhancement of 
the surface area available for ion adsorption and improved 
ion transport efficiency, leading to better electrochemical 
performance. The stability of this layered structure is nota-
ble, contributing to consistent and reliable electrochemical 
behavior over multiple charge–discharge cycles. In contrast, 
shorter synthesis times fail to produce a well-defined layered 
structure and instead lead to considerable agglomeration of 
the material as shown in Fig.  4a, forming large clusters that 
block ion movement and reduce the effective surface area 
for electrochemical reactions. This can be further verified 
by the electrochemical performance evaluation, which shows 
that the agglomerated structure compromises overall perfor-
mance. Thus, extended synthesis durations are essential for 
achieving a stable morphology that supports enhanced and 
consistent electrochemical efficiency. Figure  4 (EDX-(a)) 
presents the EDX mapping of the activated carbon material 
(sample C-4), specifically highlighting the elemental compo-
sition. The EDX analysis indicates that the material consists 
of 62.77% carbon, 31.27% oxygen, and 5.95% nitrogen. The 
incorporation of nitrogen during the carbonization process 
plays a crucial role in enhancing the performance of the 
material. Nitrogen doping introduces additional active sites, 
improves the conductivity, and enhances the wettability of 
the carbon surface, which collectively contribute to better 
electrochemical performance. The presence of nitrogen can 
also enhance the pseudo capacitance, further boosting the 
overall energy storage capability of the material.
5.4  X‑ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
The XPS analysis of the C-4 sample provides insights into 
its surface chemical composition and bonding states, as 
depicted in Fig.  5a for the C 1 s spectrum and Fig.  5b for the 
O 1 s spectrum. The deconvoluted C 1 s spectrum reveals a 
primary peak at 284.4 eV, corresponding to the C–C bond, 
indicative of graphitic carbon [63]. Additionally, the pres-
ence of oxygen-containing functional groups is evident, with 
peaks at 285.6 eV assigned to C–O bonds from hydroxyl or 
carboxyl functionalities, and at 287.8 eV, corresponding to 
C = O bonds from quinone and carbonyl species [63]. The 
O 1 s spectrum exhibits two distinct peaks: one at 531.5 eV, 
attributed to C–O bonding, and another at 533.0 eV, associ-
ated with C = O functionalities [63]. Elemental composition 
from XPS reveals 64.8% carbon and 38.2% oxygen, whereas 
EDX analysis shows 62.77% carbon and 31.23% oxygen. 
The slight variation between XPS and EDX results can 
be attributed to the differences in surface sensitivity, with 
XPS primarily detecting the outermost atomic layers while 
EDX provides a bulk composition analysis. These findings, 
in conjunction with FTIR results, confirm the presence of 
oxygen-functionalized carbon structures, which significantly 
influence the electrochemical properties of the material.
5.5  Photoluminescence (PL)
For CDs, which have crystallite sizes smaller than the size 
of the exciton radius, the PL originates from the conju-
gated π domains, and the PL wavelengths are size depend-
ent. These emissions are from carbon core band to band 
transitions and have photon energies > 3.5 eV [64]. But in 
many cases, PL depends on the transitions from the sur -
face states originating from the synergistic hybridisation 
between the  sp2 carbon and the functional groups, surface 
defects, and heteroatom doping [65]. For application of 
biochar produced carbon in electrochemical energy storage, 
the surface defects and surface functionalisation are impor-
tant; we have used excitation photon energy of 375 nm 
(= 3.3 eV < core excitation energy of 3.5 eV), to selectively 
excite the surface state transitions. Figure  6 shows the PL 
spectra of all the samples (C-1 to C–4). In Fig.  6a–d, the 
PL spectra are fit with Gaussian peaks. For sample C–1 
(Fig.  6a), we find three PL emissions at 430, 484, and 
548 nm. The emission at 430 nm is attributed to nitrogen 
related functional groups [64, 65] attached to the carbon

(Rct), which are critical for evaluating the performance of the 
electrode material. Solution resistance (Rs) primarily reflects 
the resistance of the electrolyte and the electrode–electrolyte 
interface, where lower Rs values indicate better conductivity 
and efficient ion transport. Charge transfer resistance (R ct) 
is associated with the difficulty of electron transfer at the 
electrode surface during redox reactions. For carbon-based 
electrodes, minimizing Rct is crucial for enhancing specific 
capacitance and overall efficiency, as carbon materials often 
rely on surface reactions for energy storage [67, 76].

kinetics post-CV cycling. This improvement in charge trans-

6  Conclusions
This work demonstrates the potential of biomass-derived 
carbon electrodes synthesized from sugarcane bagasse, a 
waste material, for supercapacitor applications. Optimized 
carbonization conditions (500 °C for 10 h) resulted in a 
material (C-4) with superior electrochemical performance, 
including a specific capacitance of 226.7 F/g at 1 mV/s and 
147.80 F/g at 100 mV/s respectively, retaining 65% of its 
initial capacitance shows high retention at high scan rate. 
Structural analyses revealed a porous architecture and nitro-
gen doping that enhanced ion transport and charge storage 
capacity. Photoluminescence analysis revealed surface states 
and defect structures, with red-shifted emissions indicating 
amorphous shell formation, which enhances electrochemi-
cal behavior. EDX together with XPS shows an almost 
equal atomic percentage of identified elements, confirm -
ing the uniform composition of material. Additionally, the 
material exhibited excellent charge–discharge reversibility, 
maintaining nearly 100% coulombic efficiency over 5000 
cycles. Even after a long stability test over 5000 cycles, 40% 
capacitance was retained at 2.5 A/g, demonstrating the dura-
bility of electrode. These insights emphasize the synergistic 
effects of structural and surface chemistry modifications on 
the functional properties of material. Our study highlights 
the potential of sugarcane bagasse as a sustainable and cost-
effective precursor for high-performance supercapacitor 
electrodes, paving the way for greener energy storage tech-
nologies while addressing environmental concerns.
