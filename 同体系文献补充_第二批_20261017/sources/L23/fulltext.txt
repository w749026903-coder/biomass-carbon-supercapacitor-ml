[0 article-title None] Kitchen waste-derived activated carbon from Murraya koenigii seeds as an electrode material for supercapacitors
[1 p Par1] The growing demand for sustainable, high-performance energy storage systems has spurred intensive research into biomass-derived carbon materials as environmentally friendly electrode candidates for supercapacitors. In this study, Murraya koenigii (curry plant) seed powder was employed as a novel biomass precursor for the synthesis of activated carbon in the presence of ferrous chloride (FeCl 2 ) as an activating agent (MKAC). The FeCl 2 -mediated activation, followed by pyrolysis at 900 °C under an inert atmosphere, facilitated the formation of a hierarchically porous carbon structure rich in electrochemically active sites. Comprehensive structural and chemical characterisation via BET analysis, FTIR, XRD, SEM-EDAX, and Raman spectroscopy confirmed the formation of partially graphitised carbon with an enhanced surface area of approximately 1605.5 m 2 g − 1 . Symmetric supercapacitor cells were assembled using a conductive polymer gel electrolyte based on poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVdF-HFP). Electrochemical evaluation demonstrated that FeCl 2 activation synergistically contributed to low internal resistance and a superior specific capacitance of 221.3 F g − 1 at a current density of 0.5 mA cm − 2 , with an energy density of 15.37 Wh kg − 1 at a power density of 217 W kg − 1 . The fabricated symmetric EDLC device retains ~ 88% of its initial capacitance after 10,000 consecutive charge-discharge cycles with a Coulombic efficiency of ~ 88%, confirming the long-term electrochemical stability and charge reversibility of the MKAC electrode. This green and economically viable approach not only adds value to agricultural waste but also offers a viable pathway toward developing advanced electrode materials for sustainable supercapacitor applications.

[4 title None] Introduction
[5 p Par2] The growing energy demand in the world and the dwindling fossil fuels have heightened the quest for sustainable and clean energy storage systems. Electrochemical supercapacitors have also become an option of interest among other alternatives because of their high-power density, fast charge-discharge rate, and extended cycle life, among other factors. They are intermediaries between traditional dielectric capacitors and rechargeable batteries 1 ─ 3 . The selection of electrode material and electrolyte system is the main factor that defines the overall performance of the supercapacitors 1 . Here, the emergence of inexpensive, renewable, and energy-dense electrode materials based on biomass feedstock has received considerable interest in recent years 3 .
[6 p Par3] The valorisation of agricultural and food waste into high-performance carbon electrode materials represents a strategically important and scalable pathway for sustainable energy storage. Recent advances have demonstrated that biomass-derived carbons encompassing plant residues, crop waste, and food by-products can be engineered through chemical and physical activation to yield hierarchically porous architectures with large surface areas, tunable pore distributions, and intrinsic heteroatom functionalities that collectively enhance both double-layer and pseudocapacitive charge storage. The translation of laboratory-scale biomass carbonisation processes to pilot and commercial scales is increasingly feasible, as the raw materials require no elaborate purification, the synthesis protocols are compatible with conventional industrial furnaces, and the activating agents such as FeCl 2 , ZnCl 2 , and KOH are commercially available at low cost. These attributes, combined with the zero-cost nature of agricultural residues and their year-round availability across agrarian economies such as India, collectively position biomass-derived carbon as a commercially compelling alternative to synthetic or mined electrode materials for next-generation energy storage devices.
[7 p Par4] The development of activated carbon made from biomass is of great importance. Due to its renewability, availability, and inherent heteroatom functionality, it has the potential to increase the electrochemical activity of electrochemical devices. In addition, the functional properties of activated carbon produced from biomass possess the electronic, porous, and chemically active surfaces required to optimize the electrode materials of electrochemical devices. The way activated carbon has been published can also be seen in the surface area, porosity, and surface chemistry properties that have been designed by different activation techniques, both physical and chemical, to create the porous structure necessary for high-performance electrochemical devices 4 . The physical method of activation, steam, carbon dioxide (CO₂) or air, will create the pores in the carbon structure through gas-phase etch. The resulting pores created by the physical activations are generally larger (meso- to macropores), and the surface area created by the activation is moderate (approximately 1300-1500m 2 g − 1 ) in most cases of activated oil palm shell 5 . In contrast, chemical activation integrates carbonisation and activation into a single process at temperatures of approximately 450 to 850 °C, resulting in significantly higher surface areas and ultramicroporosity 6 . Chemical activating agents are substances such as acids (e.g., phosphoric or sulfuric acid), bases (e.g., potassium or sodium hydroxide), and metal salts (e.g., zinc chloride or iron (III) chloride) used to increase the porosity and surface area of carbonaceous materials 6 . In this process, the activating agent is typically impregnated into a carbon-rich precursor (such as wood or biomass) and then heated under an inert atmosphere to promote activation. Chemical activation by using transition metal salts has been identified as an effective strategy for causing hierarchical porosity and enhancing electrical conductivity 7 . Specifically, it has been found that ferrous chloride (FeCl 2 ) can be used as a dual-functional agent, not only by catalytically graphitising during pyrolysis but also by etching carbon skeletons, and hence synthesising fully developed porous carbons with partially graphitic domains 7 , 8 .
[8 p Par5] FeCl 2 -activated carbons are characterised by the fact that the iron salt not only produces a high BET surface area and a well-developed microporous structure but also adds iron and oxygen-containing surface groups that serve as redox-active sites, thereby enhancing pseudocapacitive contributions 9 . In comparison to the more traditional KOH activation, which results in the largest surface areas (often exceeding 2,000 m 2 g − 1 ) and provides a specific capacitance of 244 F g − 1 in a 1 M H₂SO₄ electrolyte for molasses-derived carbon material 10 derived from FeCl 2 achieves similar or even higher capacitance in organic and ionic-liquid electrolytes (with reported values reaching approximately 334 F g − 1 ) 11 . Due to the synergistic effect of Fe-induced redox activity and enhanced wettability.
[9 p Par6] The zinc chloride (ZnCl 2 ) activated carbon produced by this process has very good porosity and more acidic oxygenated groups than phosphoric acid (H 3 PO 4 ) activated carbons. However, zinc chloride-activated carbons lack high specific capacitance because their surface areas are very low. Phosphoric acid-activated carbons have many mesoporous pores and a large number of phosphorus functional groups, which are useful for transporting ions between the carbon surface and the electrolyte, yet typically achieve specific capacitance values between 200 and 250 F g − 1 12 , which are lower than the values produced by ferrous chloride (FeCl 2 ) activated carbons. Therefore, carbon produced from FeCl 2 activation is a combination of a very high surface area and redox-active iron sites, providing excellent specific capacitance properties, especially when used with non-aqueous electrolytes.
[10 p Par7] Murraya koenigii (L.) Spreng. commonly known as curry tree is a perennial aromatic shrub or small tree within the Rutaceae family, and widespread across most of the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia. The leaves, seeds, bark and roots of this plant all have many quantities and types of bioactive and carbon-rich compounds and for those reasons hold great value from a plant biology point of view. The seeds of the Murraya koenigii plant are very unique compared to other seeds because they have a very tightly arranged structure made up primarily of organic matter, which contains a high percentage of the following types of lignocellulosic material (cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin) and phytochemical compounds (carbazole, alkaloids, flavonoids and phenolics) and several types of less abundant compounds that have heteroatoms 13 ,─ 15 .
[11 p Par8] A plethora of research supports the ability of Murraya koenigii as a potential renewable material source for carbonaceous functional materials. The leaves, stems, and seeds of Murraya koenigii have been extensively studied for their use as activated carbons and biochars for numerous purposes, including environmental remediation-based applications, biomedical uses, and energy-related applications, with mesoporous structures being particularly noteworthy due to their substantial anti-tumour, anti-inflammatory, and anti-diabetic effects, thus demonstrating good biocompatibility and active surfaces in 16 . Activated carbons obtained from stems and seeds have also been investigated as relatively inexpensive adsorbents to adsorb organic dyes, including methylene blue, Acid Red 18 and crystal violet from water, demonstrating good adsorption kinetics and surface affinity 17 ─ 19 . Our research group has also recently identified activated carbon materials created from Murraya koenigii seeds, also microporous in nature, as potential electrode materials for supercapacitors using ZnCl 2 as an activation agent 20 , validating the potential of this biomass for electrochemical energy storage.
[12 p Par9] However, ZnCl₂ and FeCl₂ represent fundamentally different activation chemistries with distinct structural outcomes. ZnCl 2 functions primarily as a Lewis acid catalyst, promoting dehydration reactions and selective pore formation, typically yielding mesopore-dominant structures. FeCl 2 , on the other hand, serves a dual role: it catalyses partial graphitisation through iron-carbide intermediate pathways during pyrolysis while simultaneously etching the carbon framework to generate a highly microporous architecture. Importantly, the residual iron species was confirmed by EDAX. The present study, therefore, represents a systematic investigation of how the choice of metal salt activating agent fundamentally governs pore architecture, surface chemistry, and electrochemical response of the same biomass precursor, a mechanistic comparison of significant scientific value.
[13 p Par10] The seeds of Murraya koenigii (often referred to as an underutilised biowaste material with natural heteroatomic, alkaloids and lignocellulosic biomass, and thus suitable as a precursor resource to sustainably develop electrode materials 2 , 20 , 21 . There are, however, very few reports concerning their valorisation on energy storage devices. This paper has used Murraya koenigii seed powder activated via FeCl 2 and then carbonised to produce a porous carbon material, which has increased textural and electrochemical characteristics. The surface and structural properties were examined with different characterisation techniques, and the results proved the creation of a partially graphitised and porous carbon structure.
[14 p Par11] Polymer gel electrolytes have been characterised by high ionic conductivity, leakage-proof, non-corrosive, good interfacial contact with electrodes, and mechanical flexibility, thus can be used in practical supercapacitor applications 22 ─ 25 . The interaction between the FeCl 2 -activated carbon derived from Murraya koenigii seeds (MKAC) electrodes and the polymer gel electrolyte yields a superior electrochemical response because of the balance between the conductivity, ion transportation, and stability of the structure.
[15 title None] Materials and methods
[16 title None] Preparation of activated carbon electrode material
[17 p Par12] The reagents of this study included ferrous chloride tetrahydrate (FeCl 2 .4H 2 O), ethanol, hydrochloric acid and acetone, which were acquired at Fisher Scientific, Avarice Chemicals and Qualigens, respectively. Murraya koenigii seeds were locally obtained from Mahendergarh, Haryana, in India. The first step was to prepare a sample from the seeds, which was done similarly in our previous work 20 . For the activation of the seeds, the powdered seeds were mixed with FeCl 2 in a weight ratio of 1:2, and the mixture was mixed in a solvent, ethanol, to form a uniform slurry. To achieve full soaking and chemical interaction, this slurry was left to air dry at room temperature over a period of about 6 to 7 days. The pyrolysis was also maintained at ∼900 °C in the presence of nitrogen (N 2 ) gas with a ramp rate of 5 °C min − 1 and a dwell time of 30 min. The inert environment played an imperative role in avoiding contamination by oxygen. The material was cooled to room temperature following the pyrolysis and ground into a fine powder. The rest of the inorganic was removed by washing in diluted HCl, and then the deionised water until a neutral pH was achieved. The dried material was then dried in a vacuum at ∼100 °C overnight, and kept in air-tight containers so as not to absorb moisture. [20 title None] Fabrication of supercapacitor cell
[21 p Par13] The FeCl 2 -activated carbon derived from Murraya koenigii seeds (MKAC) powders was used in the preparation of supercapacitor electrodes for device assembly. A homogenous slurry was prepared by mixing 95 wt% of selected active material and 5 wt% poly (vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVdF-HFP), a binder. This slurry was sprayed on flexible sheets of graphite, approximately 250 μm thick, which were the current collectors, and are resistant to corrosion and have mechanical strength. The electrodes were coated and dried overnight in a vacuum at ∼100 °C, and the active loading of the material was maintained at approximately 1 mg cm − 2 . Murraya koenigii seed-derived activated carbon (MKAC) electrodes were used to construct symmetric supercapacitor cells with a 1 × 1 cm 2 area. The polymer electrolyte membrane was prepared in the form of a liquid electrolyte mixture of ethylene carbonate (EC), propylene carbonate (PC) and sodium perchlorate (NaClO 4 ) using PVdF-HFP host polymer by solution casting 26 . [27 title None] Structural and morphological studies
[28 p Par19] The Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherm shows that the activated carbon derived from Murraya koenigii seeds (MKAC) material is a high-surface-area, micro-mesoporous carbon with an H4-type hysteresis loop and slit-like pores. At higher pressures, the slope shows increased uptake of adsorbate as pores become filled. The material has a very large BET area, with the majority of the total pore volume being made by micropores, as shown in Table 1 . The adsorption-desorption isotherm exhibits steep uptake at extreme relative pressure P/P o less than 0.05, which is typical of micropore filling, then multi-layer adsorption, and a relatively small hysteresis loop over the mesopore region, which is typical of a mixed Type I(b) and IV(a) isotherm 27 , 24 . The loop form is equivalent to the hysteresis of the type of material with slit-like pores and micro-mesopores structures that IUPAC correlates with the presence of sharp uptake and narrow loop shapes initially seen in Fig. 3 (a). The adsorption branch was used to determine the surface area of the BET, finding the linear region that meets the Rouquerol consistency criteria of positive C, monotonically increasing n (1-P/P o ) with P/P o and a self-consistent monolayer capacity, usually within 0.05 ≤ P/P o ≤ 0.30 with N 2 at 77 K according to IUPAC guidelines 24 , 25 , 28 ─ 30 .

[42 p Par29] The wide bands at ∼3630 and ∼3757 cm − 1 are attributed to O-H vibrations. The rather sharp peak at around 3757 cm − 1 represents non-hydrogen bonded hydroxyl groups, the peak at around 3630 cm − 1 represents hydrogen-bonded hydroxyls or phenolic O-H. Their existence shows that the phenolic and hydroxyl functionalities which are formed by the natural phenols and flavonoids of Murraya koenigii , remain intact even after chemical activation and high-temperature carbonisation, contributing to the surface polarity of the activated carbon. The C-H region has two bands, around 2981 and 2897 cm − 1 , which are said to be aliphatic C-H stretching (-CH 2 /-CH 3 ). Such bands are a reflection of the leftover aliphatic fragments of the biomass 31 , 33 , 34 . The band at ∼2325.55 cm − 1 is typically found in porous carbonaceous materials and is attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibration of adsorbed or atmospheric CO 2, which is commonly detected in FTIR spectra 35 . The peak at ∼2095 cm − 1 could be a definite cumulative bond character (conjugated C = C), which could be obtained through the condensed aromatic derivatives in the starting material 35 . The peak at ∼1547 cm − 1 is likely to be a result of the aromatic ring vibrations 34 . The occurrence of this band indicates the structure of the material to be composed of a highly condensed and polyaromatic structure of carbon.
[43 p Par30] After that the structure and the degree of graphitisation of the activated carbon derived from Murraya koenigii seeds (MKAC) were studied using Raman spectroscopy. Figure 4 (b) displays the deconvoluted Raman spectrum in the wavelengths of 200–2500 cm − 1 and the two distinct bands are the D band at ∼1353 cm − 1 and the G band at ∼1590 cm − 1 , which is typical of carbonaceous materials. The D band at ~ 1353 cm − 1 is a result of the A 1g breathing mode of six-membered carbon rings, and can only occur in the presence of disorder. The G band around ~ 1590 cm − 1 can be attributed to E 2g stretching of sp 2 bond, observed in both the ordered and disordered carbons. An elevated I D /I G ratio indicates more chaos and defects in the carbon framework, and the increase in disorder-defect density increased number of sp 2 ring-breathing defect mode also linked to greater microporosity, heteroatom integration and edge location, capable of improving capacitance through wettability 36 . Estimation of the level of defect in carbons is generally done using the I D /I G ratio. As the I D /I G ratio exceeds unity, the material is regarded as highly disordered and has a lower degree of graphitic ordering. This may be attributed to the processes of activation of the material by introducing heteroatoms, oxygenated groups or edges. A Similar trend has been reported for FeCl 2 -activated carbon derived from Murraya koenigii seeds (MKAC), where the I D /I G ratio increased to ∼2.26, compared to 0.82 for the non-activated carbon (MKC) 20 , which signifies the presence of more defects and lower graphitic order in activated carbon derived from Murraya koenigii seeds (MKAC) 32 , 37 . Interestingly, the expansion of the D band also validates that there are also many structural defects and functional groups that are oxygenated, which are beneficial in electrochemical processes 38 ─ 43 . In brief, the Raman examination shows that the activation of the biowaste precursor using FeCl 2 has able to achieve a balanced microstructure of both ordered graphitic and defect-rich amorphous carbon.

[50 p Par35] Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to analyse the charge transport properties and the capacitive behaviour, along with the resistive behaviour of the prepared electrodes of MKAC in electrochemical devices. The Nyquist, Bode and phase angle plot, and the equivalent circuit fit can be seen in Fig. 8 (a-d) in the frequency range of 100 kHz to 10 mHz. Figure 8 (a) indicates that in the high-frequency region of the Nyquist plot, the small value at the real axis corresponds to the series resistance (R s ) of the electrolyte and the electrode-electrolyte interfaces. It was found that the value of R s across has been about ~ 7.7 Ω cm − 2 , indicating that the proper contact at the electrode-electrolyte interface, and polymer gel electrolyte provides sufficient ions 48 . [55 p Par36] The impedance spectra were analysed using a modified Randles equivalent circuit obtained from Zview2 software, which includes a series resistance (Rs), two combinations of parallel resistance and constant phase elements ((R₁‖QPE₁) and (R₂‖CPE₂)), followed by a Warburg diffusion element (W s ) 53 , as depicted in Fig. 7 , and values are mentioned in the fitting parameter Table 3 . The series resistance is almost matched with respect to the experimentally observed values mentioned above in Table 2 . The relatively low value of R s indicates effective interfacial contact and efficient ionic conduction through the PVdF-HFP-based gel electrolyte. The first parallel branch, defined by R₁ and CPE₁, is associated with the rapid interfacial charge transfer process and the non-ideal formation of the double-layer at the porous carbon surface. The high n value 0.88, which is close to unity, suggests a primarily capacitive response with minimal surface heterogeneity. EDLCs utilising porous carbon electrodes (AC, MWCNTs, SWCNTs, GNP) indicate that the value of n is reported to range from 0.6 to 0.8 54 . The second parallel branch, consisting of R₂ and CPE₂, relates to deeper ion penetration and distributed capacitance within the hierarchical pore network of the activated carbon, indicating increased surface roughness and variability in pore size 53 . The finite-length Warburg short element (W s ) was selected in the equivalent circuit model in preference to the open Warburg element (W o ), based on the physical diffusion behaviour of the present system. The open Warburg element (W o ) is physically appropriate only when ion diffusion occurs across a semi-infinite, unbounded diffusion layer, a condition typically applicable to planar, open electrode surfaces in bulk liquid electrolytes. In contrast, the MKAC electrode possesses a well-defined hierarchical microporous architecture with finite pore lengths, within which electrolytic ion diffusion is geometrically constrained and bounded by the pore walls. Under these conditions, ions cannot diffuse to infinite depth, and the diffusion layer thickness is limited by the physical dimensions of the micropores. The Warburg short element (Ws) accurately models this bounded, finite-length diffusion behaviour, where ion transport occurs within the microporous channels. This physical justification is consistent with the predominantly microporous character of the MKAC electrode confirmed by BET analysis (69.5% microporosity) 55 , 56 .

[60 p Par37] The Bode plot of the MKAC-based symmetric EDLC device, presenting the frequency dependence of the total impedance magnitude log|Z| (left Y-axis) and the phase angle (right Y-axis) over the frequency range of 10 − 2 Hz to 10 5 Hz, is illustrated in Fig. 8 (b). At high frequencies, the impedance magnitude attains a minimum plateau value, reflecting the dominance of the equivalent series resistance (Rs) of the system, while the phase angle approaches 0° characteristic of purely resistive behaviour at elevated frequencies. With progressive decrease in frequency, log|Z| increases monotonically, indicative of the capacitive charge storage becoming increasingly dominant at lower frequencies. Correspondingly, the phase angle shifts systematically toward more negative values, reaching approximately − 74° to −76° at the lowest measured frequency (10 − 2 Hz), the deviation from the ideal − 90° is attributed to the distributed capacitance within the microporous architecture and minor ion diffusion limitations within the narrow pore channels of the MKAC electrode. The characteristic response frequency (f o ), defined as the frequency at which the resistive and capacitive impedances are equal and identified at a phase angle of −45°, was determined to be 0.1468 Hz from the Bode phase plot. From this, the relaxation time constant (τ₀) was calculated as 6.81 s. This relaxation time constant represents the minimum time required to discharge more than 50% of the stored energy from the device with high efficiency, and the relatively low τ₀ value obtained confirms the rapid charge-discharge kinetics and good frequency response of the MKAC-based symmetric supercapacitor, attributable to the well-developed hierarchical microporous network facilitating efficient electrolytic ion transport within the PVdF-HFP gel electrolyte matrix 47 , 51 , 57 ─ 61 . Figure 8 (c) illustrates the frequency dependence of the imaginary component of capacitance (Cʺ) for the EDLC. A distinct maximum in Cʺ is noted in the low-frequency range, which corresponds to the characteristic relaxation process of the electrode-electrolyte system. This peak signifies the transition from resistive behaviour at high frequencies to capacitive charge storage at low frequencies, where electrolyte ions have adequate time to reach the porous carbon surface and establish a stable electric double layer 62 . The movement of the Cʺ maximum towards lower frequencies suggests a relatively short relaxation time constant, indicating effective ion transport within the hierarchical micro-mesoporous network formed by FeCl 2 activation.

[82 p Par48] The long-term electrochemical stability of the MKAC-based symmetric supercapacitor cell was evaluated by performing continuous galvanostatic charge-discharge cycling over 10,000 consecutive cycles, and the results are presented in Fig. 10 (e). The device demonstrates commendable cycling stability, retaining approximately 93–94% of its initial capacitance up to ~ 9000 cycles, followed by a gradual decline to ~ 88% at the end of 10,000 cycles, indicating that the hierarchical microporous architecture of the FeCl₂-activated carbon effectively sustains the electrode integrity under prolonged electrochemical stress 48 . The Coulombic efficiency (CE), plotted on the right Y-axis, remains consistently close to ~ 99% throughout the majority of the cycling window, with only a marginal decline to approximately ~ 88% observed in the final cycles (beyond ~ 9,500 cycles), which is attributable to minor kinetic irreversibilities and gradual pore blockage at extended cycle numbers a commonly reported phenomenon in microporous carbon-based EDLCs under long-term operation. The inset of Fig. 10 (e) provides a magnified view of the cycling performance. This stable cycling behaviour validates the compatibility of the FeCl₂-activated Murraya koenigii seed-derived carbon electrode with the quasi-solid PVdF-HFP gel electrolyte system, and validates the practical viability of the fabricated device for long-term energy storage applications.

[84 title None] Conclusion
[85 p Par52] The present work carries significance on multiple scientific and technological fronts. From an environmental standpoint, it successfully valorises Murraya koenigii seeds, an underutilised agricultural residue endemic to the Indian subcontinent, into a high-performance carbon electrode material through a straightforward, single-step FeCl 2 -assisted carbonisation at 900 °C, effectively diverting bio-waste from landfill within a circular bioeconomy framework. The novelty of this work lies in the dual-functional role of FeCl 2 as both a graphitisation catalyst and a micropore-generating agent, yielding an MKAC electrode with a BET surface area of 1605.5 m 2 g −1 and 69.5% microporosity, without any external heteroatom doping and in the demonstration of its successful electrochemical integration with a PVdF-HFP quasi-solid polymer gel electrolyte in a separator-free symmetric EDLC architecture, a configuration rarely reported for microporous biomass-derived carbons. In terms of scalability, the single-step synthesis using a low-cost, zero-waste precursor is inherently compatible with industrial-scale carbonisation processes, requiring no exotic reagents or multi-stage processing. Compared to recently reported biomass-derived carbon supercapacitor systems employing conventional liquid electrolytes (Table 4 ), the MKAC-based quasi-solid device delivers competitive specific capacitance (221.3 F g −1 ), energy density (15.37 Wh kg −1 ), and cycling stability (~ 88% retention over 10,000 cycles) while simultaneously offering the practical advantages of leak-proof, flexible, and safe device operation, attributes critical for next-generation portable and wearable energy storage applications.
[86 p Par53] The practical applicability of the fabricated MKAC-based symmetric quasi-solid-state EDLC is strongly supported by several device-level attributes. The all-solid-state configuration employing the PVdF-HFP polymer gel electrolyte eliminates the fundamental limitations of conventional liquid electrolyte systems, where the device is well-positioned to power low-to-medium consumption portable electronics, wearable health-monitoring sensors, and IoT devices. Furthermore, the low-cost, abundantly available Murraya koenigii seed precursor, combined with the straightforward single-step synthesis, supports scalable and cost-effective manufacturing, a prerequisite for commercial viability within sustainable energy storage frameworks.
