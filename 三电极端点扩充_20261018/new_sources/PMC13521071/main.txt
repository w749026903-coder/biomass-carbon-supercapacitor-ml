[P0001] As global energy demand continues to rise, the large use of existing fossil fuels has further exacerbated problems such as the greenhouse effect and environmental pollution. Renewable energy sources, such as solar, wind, and hydropower, are strongly influenced by weather and seasonal conditions, resulting in variable power output and challenges in maintaining a stable and continuous energy supply throughout the year.1,2,3 Based on this background, among the existing energy storage technologies, the electrochemical energy storage (EES) system was considered one of the viable solutions for sustainable energy generation and storage.4,5
[P0002] Researchers have identified zinc-ion batteries (ZIBs) as a promising option for next-generation EES systems. Owing to their high specific capacity, favorable energy density and strong safety profile.6 Factors such as the dissolution problem of anode materials, insufficient power density, and poor cyclic stability still limit the practical application.7 In contrast, supercapacitors (SCs) can achieve efficient energy storage with their fast charging and discharge capacity, high power density and excellent long cycle life, though they typically suffer from relatively low energy density.8,9 As a novel hybrid EES device, the zinc-ion hybrid capacitor (ZIHC) had attracted wide attention by integrating the high energy density characteristic of ZIBs with the high-power density advantage of SCs.10,11 Compared with traditional ZIBs, ZIHC shows higher safety and lower cost. At the same time, achieving better energy density compared to traditional electrochemical capacitors.12,13,14
[P0003] Biomass has benefits including wide sources, low cost, and high carbon content. It was considered an ideal renewable precursor for fabricating carbon materials and is widely utilized in EES devices.15,16,17 Carbon-based materials such as activated carbon, porous carbon, and graphene have been widely used in ZIHC,18,19 due to their high specific surface area, adjustable pore structure, favorable chemical stability, and excellent electrical conductivity.20,21,22 Cao et al.23 prepared B/N co-doped hierarchically porous carbon material using lignin as the carbon source through in situ carbonization combined with KOH activation method. This hierarchical porous structure provides efficient ion-transport pathways. B/N co-doping regulates the electronic configuration of porous carbon and introduces additional pseudocapacitance to enhancing energy storage performance. In view of the insufficient energy storage performance caused by the low energy density and wide operating temperature range of zinc ion hybrid capacitors. Ma et al.24 proposed the use of hydrogen bond-guided self-assembly method to synthesize heterodoped coral-hierarchical porous carbon materials with heterotomic acid with sodium lignin sulfonate and chitosan as raw materials. In ZIHC, the specific capacity of 202 mAh g−1 is achieved at 0.1 A g−1, and shows excellent cycle performance, retaining 97.5% of its capacity after 10,000 cycles. The introduction of heteroatoms into biomass-derived carbon can change the electronic structure and surface properties, improve its surface wettability, increase active sites, and enhance the pseudo-capacitance.25 He et al.26 developed an in situ stripping strategy to successfully synthesized porous carbon nanofiber membranes rich in pyridine nitrogen and pyrrole nitrogen. Nitrogen heteroatoms facilitate the formation of N–Zn–O bonds, thus reducing the binding energy between carbon materials and Zn2+, and improving the chemical adsorption capacity of carbon material on Zn2+. Tang et al.27 used bamboo scrap as a precursor to prepare multi-stage porous carbon with N, P co-doping through a one-step method. N, P co-doping introduced abundant defects and active sites, optimizing the pore structure and improving material wettability. The specific capacitance reached 386.6 F g−1 at 0.2 A g−1. In ZIHC, it showed high energy density (128.9 Wh kg−1) and strong cycle stability (97.1% after 10,000 cycles). Du et al.28 used the hydrothermal activation strategy to prepare N, O co-doped stratified porous carbon. The co-doping introduced numerous active sites and builds a three-dimensional porous structure, thus improving the electrochemical properties. At 0.5 A g−1, its specific capacitance reached 546.08 F g−1, in the symmetrical SC, the energy density of 16.48 Wh·kg−1 is achieved at 125.03 W kg−1.
[P0004] While biomass-derived carbon materials hold great promise as electrode for SCs, several limitations remain. They usually have a natural porous structure (three-dimensional carbon skeleton). Its pore network is connected to each other, but the internal space is limited, leading to low utilization of the specific surface area and inadequate electrical conductivity. These factors jointly affect the overall electrochemical properties. In addition, the uneven distribution of heteroatoms in biomass precursors also makes it difficult to achieve uniform doping bands. Based on the above shortcomings, it is urgent to explore simple and efficient strategies for precursor structural design and surface modulation to enhance the electrochemical performance of ZIHC.
[P0005] In this research, a hierarchical porous carbon material was prepared through the hydrothermal-KOH activation-N doping cooperative strategy using forest and grass waste (Weigela japonica, WJ). This strategy was employed to achieve coordinated optimization of hierarchical porosity and N/O-containing functional groups. Compared with conventional direct carbonization/activation or simple N-doping approaches,29,30,31,32 this strategy emphasizes the balance between ion-accessible pores, active defect sites, surface wettability, and charge-transfer capability, thereby improving the electrochemical behavior of the biomass-derived carbon electrode. The resulting nitrogen-doped hierarchical porous carbon (NWJPC-2), achieved a high specific surface area of 2,916 m2 g−1. In the three-electrode system, a high specific capacitance of 318.8 F g−1 is shown at 1 A g−1. In SSCs, when the power density is 245.0 W kg−1, its energy density reaches 24.7 Wh kg−1. In addition, the ZIHC assembled with NWJPC-2 as the cathode pole has a specific capacity of 109.5 mAh g−1 at 0.5 A g−1, and shows outstanding cycle stability (99.9% capacity retention after 10,000 cycles) and an energy density of 44.7 Wh kg−1. These findings highlight the great potential of carbon derived from forest and grass crops as a high-performance cathode for next-generation of zinc-based energy storage systems, and the high-efficiency and high-value utilization of biomass provide important guidance.

[P0012] The influence of hydrothermal temperature was further investigated by FTIR. As shown in Figure S4, the broad peak centered at approximately 3,430 cm−1 is assigned to O–H stretching vibrations, while the weak band near 2,921 cm−1 corresponds to aliphatic C–H stretching. The absorption peak at 1,711 cm−1 is associated with C=O groups derived from hemicellulose, whereas the band at 1,621 cm−1 is attributed to aromatic skeletal vibrations. The peaks at 1,321 and 1,057 cm−1 are assigned to C–H vibrations associated with cellulose/hemicellulose and aromatic structures, respectively.33,34 With increasing hydrothermal temperature, the C=O and C–H related signals gradually weaken, indicating the progressive decomposition of hemicellulose and the disruption of the compact cross-linked structure formed by cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin in WJ. This is conducive to the penetration of KOH into the interior of the carbon material during the activation process.35 However, excessive hydrothermal treatment may induce over-condensation, loss of oxygen-containing functional groups, and partial pore collapse, ultimately deteriorating the electrochemical performance.36,37 Therefore, keeping the hydrothermal temperature at 180°C is more conducive to the preparation of porous carbon materials with excellent electrochemical properties.

[P0027] When the mass ratio of melamine and hydrothermal precursor was different, the microstructure formed by NWJPC also showed obviously differences. Without the addition of melamine, the porosity of NWJPC is relatively low, and its surface showed a dense and thick-walled honeycomb-like large pore structure (Figure 2A), which was caused by the activation of KOH and the etching of the carbon skeleton. With the increase in the addition of melamine, the structure of NWJPC-1 was refined (Figure 2B). The original thick-walled pores are further opened, the hole wall become thinner, and smaller holes were gradually formed. When the proportion of melamine was increased to 2:1, it presented the best microscopic morphology (Figures 2C and 2F) and a more complex three-dimensional hierarchical porous morphology. This structure not only enhanced the storage capacity of electrolyte, but also can obviously shorten the ion diffusion path and improved electrochemical properties.39,40 However, as shown in Figures 2D and 2E, as the proportion of melamine continued to increase, the microstructure gradually deteriorated. This structural change may be due to excessive pyrolysis and gas etching at high temperature of excessive melamine, leading to the disintegration and rupture of the carbon framework and the formation of loosely bound particle clusters TEM analysis, presented in Figures 3A and 3B, further confirmed the three-dimensional porous architecture of NWJPC-2. The structure was mainly composed of mesoporous and microporous, and the surface showed disordered and dense porous characteristics, and there was no obvious particle aggregation phenomenon, which was consistent with the hierarchical porous structure observed by SEM. The existence of micropores increased the specific surface area of the material and promoted the rapid transmission and adsorption of ions, thus improving the specific capacitance. The elemental distribution diagram showed (Figures 3C–3F) that C, O and N were evenly distributed in the three-dimensional carbon skeleton, indicating that the doping of N atoms had been successfully achieved with melamine as a nitrogen source. This doping made the surface of the carbon material had more active sites,32 which further enhanced the electrochemical properties of the material.
[P0028] To further investigate the pore structure of NWJPC materials, nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherm analysis was carried out. As shown in Figure 4A, the prepared NWJPC samples exhibit a Type IV isotherm with an H4 hysteresis. Within the range of relative pressure P/P0 < 0.1, a sharp increase in nitrogen uptake is observed, indicating that a large amount of micropores inside the material. Within the P/P0 range of 0.1–0.9, NWJPC (1–4) showed an obvious H4-type hysteresis loop, indicating that the material contained mesoporous and irregular pore structure. The mesopore diameter of the material was mainly concentrated in the range of 2.0–3.5 nm (Figure 4B), which may be owed to the joint effect of N doping and high-temperature activator etching. In the high-pressure zone (P/P0 > 0.9), the isotherm showed a slight upward trend, indicating that the material had a certain large pore structure. The above analysis confirmed that the prepared NWJPC carbon material had a good hierarchical pore structure, consistent with SEM and TEM observations. Among all the prepared materials, exhibits the highest specific surface area of 2,916 m2 g−1 (Table 1) The NWJPC-2.5 shows lower textural parameters (Figure S2; Table S1) than NWJPC-2 but higher values than NWJPC-3 and NWJPC-4, demonstrating that an appropriate amount of melamine promotes pore development during KOH activation. However, when the melamine content is further increased at a fixed KOH dosage, it may limit the effective participation of N-containing species in the activation reactions and reduce the development of porosity.41,42 Meanwhile, intensified KOH etching can induce pore widening, pore-wall thinning, and local structural collapse, thereby decreasing the specific surface area.41 Therefore, the reduced specific surface areas of NWJPC-3 and NWJPC-4 are reasonably attributed to the combined effects of an excessive melamine dosage and severe KOH activation. Therefore, the combination of an appropriate amount of N doping and high-temperature activation treatment can effectively enlarge the specific surface area, provide sufficient active sites, promote the formation of hierarchical porous structures, and improve the permeability of the electrolyte. [P0041] The crystal structure of the prepared NWJPC sample was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), and combined with the analysis of Raman spectroscopy to analyze its degree of graphitization and defect characteristics. As observed in Figure 4C, all samples had wide and weak diffraction peaks near 2θ ≈ 23° and 43°, which correspond respectively to the (002) and (100) crystal planes of disordered carbon. This suggests that the prepared porous carbon was a typical low-graphite amorphous carbon structure. With rising levels of heteroatom doping, the (002) diffraction peak shifts toward higher angles and becomes narrower, indicating a progressive increase in internal structural defects within the material. As depicted in Figure 4D, the Raman spectra of the samples exhibit two characteristic peaks located at approximately 1,361 cm−1 and 1,591 cm−1, corresponding to the D and G bands, respectively. The D band is associated with structural defects, edge sites, and disordered carbon, whereas the G band is assigned to the in-plane vibration of sp2-bonded graphitic carbon. To obtain reliable ID/IG values, the Raman spectra were further analyzed by baseline correction and peak deconvolution. The ID/IG ratio is an important indicator that characterizes the structural disorder of carbon materials.44,45 The results showed that the ID/IG ratios of NWJPC-0, NWJPC-1, NWJPC-2, NWJPC-3, and NWJPC-4 were 1.01, 1.10, 1.15, 1.26 and 1.32 respectively. With the increase of melamine addition, the degree of defect gradually increased. The trend that closely aligns with the XRD analysis findings. This indicates that nitrogen doping can effectively increase the porosity of the material, while make the carbon skeleton structure tend to be disordered. However, excessive disorder may also reduce the graphitization degree and weaken electronic conductivity.46 Therefore, an appropriate defect level is essential for achieving balanced ion transport, charge storage, and electrical conductivity.

Pyridinic-N, located at the edge or defect sites of the carbon framework, can act as adsorption site and promote Zn2+ adsorption through local charge redistribution. Pyrrolic-N can participate in reversible surface redox reactions and contribute additional pseudocapacitance.48 Graphitic-N is beneficial for improving electronic conductivity and charge-transfer kinetics. [P0052] To improve the electrochemical properties of Weigela japonica-based porous carbon, N-doping was performed on the hydrothermal precursor using melamine to prepare the NWJPC-X series samples. The effect of N-doping amount on energy storage performance was systematically examined, as shown in Table 3 and Figure 6. In a three-electrode system, electrochemical tests were conducted on NWJPC-0, NWJPC-1, NWJPC-2, NWJPC-3, and NWJPC-4. At 50 mV s−1, the CV curves of all electrode materials in Figure 6A. All exhibited a distinct rectangular shape, demonstrating excellent rate performance and rapid ion transport rate. Among them, the CV curve of NWJPC-4 had the smallest area. That might be due to the release of a large amount of NH3 and other gases during the high-temperature thermal decomposition of melamine, causing excessive carbon skeleton etching and structural collapse, contributing to a decrease in specific surface area (Figure 4A). The closed area of the CV curve of NWJPC-2 was the largest, due to its high specific surface area, which could provide abundant ion adsorption sites, and N doping could effectively enhance the electron transfer rate, thus exhibiting excellent capacitive performance. The GCD curve of NWJPC-X at 1 A g−1 is shown in Figure 6B. The specific capacitances of NWJPC-0, NWJPC-1, NWJPC-2, NWJPC-3, and NWJPC-4 were 129.0, 133, 318.8, 107.1, and 91.3 F g−1, respectively. These curves all showed a highly symmetrical trend, indicating that the Weigela japonica-based porous carbon material has excellent electrochemical reversibility. Within the scan rate range of 1–200 mV s−1, the CV curve of NWJPC-2 retained a regular rectangular shape, indicating stable rate capability (Figure 6D). The GCD curves at different current (Figure 6E) densities presented a symmetrical triangular shape, consistent with typical double-layer capacitance behavior. At 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 A g−1, the specific capacitances were 318.8, 218.2, 180.2, 166.9, and 156.8 F g−1, respectively, and the capacity retention rate at 20 A g−1 reached 49.2%, demonstrating rate characteristics (Figure 6C; Table 3). EIS tests were conducted within the frequency range of 0.1–100 kHz. As shown in Figure 6F, the Nyquist curves of all samples were similar in shape. In the high-frequency region, NWJPC-2 showed a larger slope, indicating lower ionic diffusion resistance and faster charge response, and better conductive and ionic transport performance. However, the Nyquist plots exhibit a slight distortion in the low-frequency region. This phenomenon is likely attributed to ion-transport polarization and distributed impedance within the hierarchical porous carbon structure. [P0064] Given the outstanding electrochemical performance of NWJPC-2 in the three-electrode system, it was employed as both the positive and negative electrode materials to assemble the NWJPC-2//NWJPC-2 SSC. Cyclic voltammetry measurements revealed that the stable operating voltage window for this SSC is 0–1 V. When the voltage was expanded to 1.1 V, a significant polarization phenomenon occurred (Figure 7A). This phenomenon is mainly associated with the voltage exceeding the stable operating window of the KOH-based aqueous symmetric carbon SC, which may trigger parasitic water decomposition reactions, including possible OER at the positive electrode and HER at the negative electrode, thereby causing the CV curve to deviate from the ideal rectangular shape.52,53 Within the potential window of 0–1 V, the CV curves recorded at various scan rates retained a quasi-rectangular geometry with no notable distortion, indicating its excellent capacitance characteristics (Figure 7B). The GCD curves at different current densities display nearly symmetrical isosceles triangular profiles, demonstrating the good reversibility and high Coulombic efficiency of the N-doped carbon material (Figure 7C). The slight deviation from the ideal shape may be associated with restricted ion transport in the hierarchical porous framework, partial pseudocapacitive contributions from N/O-containing functional groups, and possible irreversible surface reactions, which may increase interfacial resistance and affect long-term cycling stability.54,55 According to calculations based on the GCD curve (Figure 7D), the specific capacitance of the NWJPC-2-based SSC reached 178.3 F g−1at 0.5 A g−1. [P0074] Based on the remarkable property of NWJPC-2 in symmetric SCs, an aqueous zinc-ion hybrid capacitor ZIHC (denoted as Zn//ZnSO4(aq.)//NWJPC-2) was assembled. Using NWJPC-2 as the positive electrode and zinc foil as the negative electrode, with Whatman separator and 2 M ZnSO4 aqueous solution as the electrolyte. The application potential of ZIHC within a zinc-ion energy storage system was explored. Through CV tests under different voltage windows, established that the device operates stably within a range of 0–1.8 V (Figure 8A); extending the voltage to 2.0 V resulted in noticeable polarization. In Figure 8B, the CV curves at different scan rates consistently retained maintained a quasi-rectangular shape, indicating prominent rate performance. A weak redox peak at 1.4 V/0.8 V corresponded to the reversible Faraday reaction between N-doped sites and Zn2+, proving a hybrid energy storage mechanism that integrates both electric double-layer capacitance and pseudo-capacitance.25,26 The GCD curves in Figure 8C display quasi-isosceles triangle, illustrating prominent electrochemical reversibility and high Coulombic efficiency of the assembled ZIHC. The slight deviation from the ideal symmetric shape may arise from the kinetic mismatch between the fast surface-controlled ion adsorption/desorption at the porous carbon cathode and the relatively sluggish Zn2+ plating/stripping at the Zn anode. Moreover, possible side reactions at the Zn anode, including hydrogen evolution reaction, Zn corrosion, and dendrite growth in the aqueous ZnSO4 electrolyte, may increase interfacial resistance and affect long-term cycling stability.30,56,57 Figure 8D showed that the specific capacity of ZIHC reached 109.5 mAh g−1 at 0.5 A g−1, and maintained good rate performance as the current density increased. This is attributed to the doping of N and O elements, which effectively enhanced the wettability of the NWJPC-2 positive electrode material, introduced pseudo-capacitance and increased the charge density, thereby notably improving the energy storage performance of the carbon material. In Figure 8E, the device delivered an energy density of 44.7 Wh kg−1 at 225.0 W kg−1. Even when the power density was raised to 8,727.0 W kg−1, the energy density was still be maintained at 19.6 Wh kg−1. In particular, the energy density of ZIHC is higher to those of several reported porous carbon-based materials (Table S5). This favorable performance is mainly attributed to the high specific surface area, hierarchical porous structure, and abundant N/O-containing functional groups of NWJPC-2, which jointly provide abundant active sites, enhance surface wettability, promote ion transport, and contribute pseudocapacitance. Additionally, the AC impedance test results of ZIHC were shown in Figure 8F. The Nyquist plot displays the typical features of a hybrid energy storage device, with a semi-circle in the high-frequency region and an inclined straight line in the medium and low-frequency regions. The diameter of the semicircle reflected a high charge transfer resistance, which was closely associated with the redox reaction of Zn2+ at the electrode interface. [P0090] Based on the above Equations. 4 and 5, the kinetic behavior of NWJPC-2 in ZIHC was analyzed. As shown in Figure 9A, the b-values calculated from the anodic and cathodic peaks are 0.90 and 0.95, respectively, indicating that the charge-storage process of NWJPC-2 is dominated by surface-controlled kinetics. Dunn method (Equations. 6 and 7) analysis further reveals that the capacitive contribution reaches 80.1% at 100 mV s−1 (Figure 9B). With increasing scan rate, the capacitive contribution gradually increases, whereas the diffusion-controlled contribution decreases (Figure 9C), suggesting faster charge storage on accessible surface/near-surface sites at high rates. It should be noted that this surface-controlled behavior may involve not only physical ion adsorption/desorption but also defect-assisted Zn2+ adsorption and pseudocapacitive reactions related to N/O-containing functional groups.59,60 These results demonstrate that the hierarchical pore structure and abundant active sites of NWJPC-2 effectively promote rapid ion transport and high-rate charge storage. Additionally, after 10,000 charge-discharge cycles at 5 A g−1, the ZIHC achieved a coulombic efficiency nearing 100% and retained 99.9% of its initial specific capacitance (Figure 9D), demonstrating outstanding cycling stability. Compared with SC, this ZIHC exhibited superior cycling stability, which can be largely attributed to the unique structure of nitrogen-doped hierarchical porous carbon. The structure can provide adequate charge storage active sites for the Zn2+ adsorption; mesopores can shorten the ion diffusion distance and promote ions transmission; and macropores as ion buffer pools and transmission channels.61,62
[P0091] This work successfully prepared a series of N-doped hierarchical porous carbon materials derived from Weigela japonica (NWJPC-X), using hydrothermal pretreatment, KOH activation and N-doping coordination strategies based on forest and grass as raw materials. Among them, the NWJPC-2 sample presented an optimal hierarchical porous structure, achieving a specific surface area of 2,916 m2 g−1 along with desirable electrochemical performance. In the three-electrode system, it achieved a specific capacitance of 318.8 F g−1 at 1 A g−1. Even under a high current density of 20 A g−1, its capacity retention rate was still 49.2%. The SSC assembled with NWJPC-2 saves as both positive and negative electrodes, had an energy density of 24.7 Wh kg−1 at 245.0 W kg−1. Further assembled with NWJPC-2 as positive electrode and zinc foil as negative electrode, the capacitor reached the specific capacity of 109.5 mAh g−1 at 0.5 A g−1, and the energy density can reach 44.7 Wh kg−1 at the power density of 225.0 W kg−1. Even at an elevated power density of 8,727.0 W kg−1, the energy density was still maintained at 19.6 Wh kg−1. In addition, after 10,000 cycles at 5 A g−1, the specific capacitance retention rate was as high as 99.9%, with Coulomb efficiency approaching 100%, demonstrating cycling stability superior to that of symmetric SCs. This research provided a convenient and efficient strategy for developing sustainable energy storage materials, aiming to improve the practical application of sustainable ZIHCs in the future. It also affords a new insight into for the high-efficient and high-value utilization of forest and grass waste biomass resources.
[P0092] The NWJPC-2 exhibits favorable electrochemical performance in zinc-ion hybrid capacitors, indicating the potential of biomass-derived porous carbon in zinc-based energy storage. However, factors such as zinc corrosion, hydrogen evolution, dendrite formation, and depletion of electrolyte may affect the long-term stability of the device. Therefore, although the proposed approach shows promise for enhancing electrochemical performance, further optimization is required to meet the requirements of practical applications, which is crucial for promoting the development of zinc-ion hybrid capacitors.

[P0106] The synthesis diagram of synthesizing hierarchical porous carbon through a hydrothermal-KOH-N doping activation process is shown in Figure S1. Weigela japonica biomass is washed three to four times with deionized water, then dried in an oven at 105°C, and grind it into a powder with a particle diameter of less than 0.5 mm. This powder is mixed with deionized water at a ratio of 1:10 (solid to liquid) and transferred into a stainless-steel autoclave. The mixture was subjected to hydrothermal treatment at varying temperatures (140, 160, 180, 200, and 220 °C) for 4 h. The product was washed repeatedly with deionized water until the filtrate becomes clear, then dried at 105 °C for 24 h to obtain the preliminary hydrothermal precursor. This precursor was thoroughly ground with KOH at a mass ratio of 1:3, and the mixture was placed in a tube furnace. In a nitrogen atmosphere, the temperature was raised to 800°C at 5°C/min and maintained for 2 h. Rinse repeatedly with 1 M HCl solution and deionized water in turn until the solution reaches neutrality, then drying in an oven at 80 °C for 24 hours, hydrothermal porous carbon (WJHC-T) was obtained.
[P0107] The hydrothermal precursor, KOH, and melamine were combined in a mortar at a mass ratio of 1:1:1, thoroughly ground and mixed until homogeneous. This mixture is transferred into a tube furnace and heated under a nitrogen atmosphere to 800 °C, with a heating rate of 5 °C/min for 2h. Then wash it with 1 M HCl and deionized water until the solution is neutral, and drying at 80 °C for 24 h. Finally, N-doped weigela japonica porous carbon (NWJPC-1) is obtained. To investigate the influence of melamine concentration on the product, prepare NWJPC-X (X = 1, 2, 3, 4,) samples according to the same steps above, where X refers to the ratio of melamine to hydrothermal precursor. For comparison, a melamine-free sample (NWJPC-0) is synthesized under identical conditions, using a mass ratio of hydrothermal precursor to KOH of 1:3, with no melamine added.
