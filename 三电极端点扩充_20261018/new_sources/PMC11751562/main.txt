[P0001] With the transition from fossil fuels to renewable energy, the demand for energy storage devices continues to emerge in this process due to the intermittency of certain clean energy sources, such as wind and solar power [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. Different from the generally short service life and safety risks of batteries, supercapacitors have gradually become one of the most promising energy storage technology solutions due to their merits of outstanding power density, long cycle life and high reliability [6], [7]. Electrode materials are a critical component of supercapacitors which determine their performance, and porous carbon (PC) has become the mainstream choice due to its large specific surface area (SSA), abundant source, low price and stable chemical properties [8], [9], [10]. Biomass, including plants, animals and their biodegradable components, is considered one of the sustainable and environmentally friendly candidates for commercially viable PC precursors [8], [11].
[P0002] Due to their natural micro-layered structure, PCs with well-developed pore structure can typically be obtained from biomass (such as scallion peel [12], sorghum [13], feather finger grass flower [14], corn straw [15] and walnut shell [16], etc.) through chemical activation, eliminating the need for expensive template methods. However, most natural plant cell walls are primarily composed of lignin, cellulose and hemicellulose. Lignin serves as a binder, forming a stable cross-linked structure between cellulose and hemicellulose. This rigid structure, along with its barrier effect, impedes the infiltration of activators during the carbonization-activation process, resulting in limited activation efficiency and porosity [17], [18]. Consequently, the microstructure of customized precursors before carbonization-activation through pretreatment, especially chemical pretreatment, has been extensively studied. For example, Yakaboylu et al. used KOH aqueous solution to chemically pretreat Miscanthus grass to control the microstructure, chemistry and active functional groups [19]. Hu et al. constructed PC with high porosity by degrading portions of lignin and hemicellulose with a mixture of Na2SO3 and NaOH solutions [20]. While chemical pretreatment is effective, biological pretreatment (degradation) is a notably greener, more sustainable, and simpler approach.
[P0003] At present, many researchers have reported biodegradation based on fungi to prepare PCs, including yeast [21], Pleurotus ostreatus [22], Neurospora crassa [23] and Trichoderma [24]. However, the harsh culture conditions and environmental factors affecting microorganisms have led to increased interest in enzymes with similar effects, such as cellulase and amylase. For instance, Peng et al. treated wood with cellulase to form a large number of prefabricated micropores to prepare layered porous carbon plates, which exhibited excellent catalytic activity in oxygen reduction and oxygen evolution reactions [25]. Wang et al. developed carbon materials from cottonseed meal using a straightforward enzymolysis strategy with cellulase, the optimized PC has a multistage porous structure with high O doping and delivers a high specific capacitance of 364 F/g [26]. These findings suggest that cellulase pretreatment enhances the porosity of PCs while offering the advantages of being green and scalable. Additionally, ultrasound, a common enhancement technology in the biological field, is widely employed to assist in processes such as organic solvent extraction and enzymolysis, improving extraction efficiency and yielding greater product quantities [27], [28], [29]. Nevertheless, there have been few studies on the preparation of high-performance PC from biomass using ultrasonic-cellulase synergistic pretreatment to date.
[P0004] Shandong is the leading garlic-producing province in China, with an annual output of approximately 4 million tons. It has been reported that solid waste, such as garlic peels generated during garlic production, accounts for about 22–27 % of the total output [30]. Therefore, efficient disposal and utilization methods for this waste are urgently needed. In this study, we utilized garlic peels as a carbon source to prepare PCs with ultra-high SSA as a supercapacitor electrode, employing a dual-frequency ultrasonic-assisted enzymolysis strategy. The effects of enzymolysis temperature and ultrasonic frequency on the performance of PCs were analyzed, and the potential synergistic mechanism of ultrasound and cellulase pore-forming was elucidated in this paper. Hence, the resulting PC exhibits ultrahigh SSA, competitive capacitive performance and energy density that exceed those of previously reported biomass-based PCs. Overall, the ultrasound-assisted enzymolysis strategy proposed in this work is expected to be extended to common plant precursors, and holds significant promise for developing high-performance energy storage materials with controlled pore structures.
[P0005] The raw material (garlic peel) was a thin layer that wraps around the garlic clove and sourced from the plantations in Shanghe County, Jinan City, Shandong Province. Unless otherwise specified, deionized water was used throughout entire experiment, and analytical reagents (KOH, HCl and so on) were supplied by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China. Cellulase (50 U/mg, obtained from Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd) was produced through the fermentation of Trichoderma viride and other excellent strains. The inert protective gas utilized in the experiment is high-purity nitrogen (>99.999 %) provided by Jinan Deyang Special Gas Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China), and the single/dual-frequency ultrasonic generator is a laboratory ultrasonic cleaner manufactured by Yunyi Ultrasonic (Shenzhen) Co., LTD (China).
[P0006] The washed garlic peel was placed in a drying oven at 105 °C for 24 h to facilitate crushing. After drying, the garlic peel was processed in a crusher for 3 min and then passed through an 80-mesh sieve to obtain a powder with a particle size of less than 178 μm. Next, 5.0 g of garlic peel powder was added to a buffer containing 0.5 g of cellulase and maintained at a constant temperature (45, 50, 55 ℃) for 30 min (solid–liquid ratio: 1:40 g/mL, pH: 5.5). Following centrifugal filtration, the cellulase was recovered, while the solid residue was washed to neutral pH and dried at 105 ℃ for 12 h.
[P0007] The washing, drying, crushing, filtration and other processes for garlic peel were consistent with those described in section 2.2.1. However, the cellulase extraction pretreatment process was conducted in a constant-temperature ultrasonic bath for 30 min (ultrasonic power: 240 W, ultrasonic frequency: 28/40 kHz).
[P0008] The pretreatment method is the same as described in section 2.2.2, but the ultrasonic frequency is set to 28 kHz + 40 kHz.
[P0009] The dried residue was carbonized in a tube furnace at 600 °C (5 °C/min) for 2 h, then mixed with KOH in an atmospheric furnace at a mass ratio of 1:4 and activated at 800 °C (5 °C/min) for 2 h. Finally, the activated product was washed with 1 M HCl until the pH reached 6–7 and dried at 105 °C for 24 h to obtain the final sample. Specifically, the PC prepared from garlic peel pretreated with cellulase was designated as CGPC-X, where X represents the enzymolysis temperature. The PC prepared using single and dual-frequency ultrasound-assisted enzymolysis pretreatment were named SUGPC-Y (where Y represents the ultrasonic frequency) and DUGPC, respectively. Meanwhile, the PC prepared using the direct carbonization-activation method served as the control group (GPC).
[P0010] The SUPRATM 55 scanning electron microscope (SEM, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) and transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100 F, Japan) were used to observe the micromorphology and microstructure of PCs. The specific surface and pore size analyzer (JW-BK132F, JWGB, China) was performed to determine the structural parameters of samples. Precisely, the SSA, total pore volume (VT) and micropore volume (Vmicro) were calculated by Bruner-Emmet-Teller (BET) method, Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method and t-plot method respectively. Moreover, the measurement of micropores was based on the Horvath-Kawazoe (HK) method while the distribution of mesopores and macropores was analyzed according to the BJH method. The phase composition, structure and crystallinity of samples were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku D/MAX-2500PC, λ = 1.5406) and Raman spectroscopy (Renishaw/RM2000, 514 nm). The element composition and surface chemical properties of PCs were conducted by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo/ESCALAB 250 XI). The hydrophilicity of PCs was measured by an optical surface tensiometer (Theta Flex, Biolin, Sweden) equipped with a GigE zoom digital camera (3009 fps). Meanwhile, the metal impurities content of the samples was determined by an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS, Agilent 7700, USA).
[P0011] Firstly, PC, polytetrafluoroethylene emulsion (PTFE, 60 wt%) and conductive graphite were mixed at a mass ratio of 80:10:10 in ethanol to prepare the slurry. For the two-electrode system, the evenly dispersed slurry (no less than 4 mg of active material) was coated on a circular nickel foam with a diameter of 16.2 mm to form the electrode sheet. After drying for 12 h at 80℃ in a vacuum drying oven, the electrode sheet was taken out and pressed at 15 MPa for 1 min. Finally, a CR2016 coin-type supercapacitor was assembled in 6 M KOH solution by two electrode sheets with similar loads and a cellulosic separator (NKK-MPFAC30, Japan). The devices were first placed at room temperature for 24 h to ensure that active materials were fully wetted, and then tested using the electrochemical workstation (CHI660D, Shanghai Chenhua, China). In the three-electrode system, the electrode sheet prepared by the above process was served as the working electrode, while the counter and reference electrodes were a platinum wire and saturated calomel electrode, respectively.

[P0017] Temperature and pH are important factors that affect the specificity and decomposition efficiency of enzymes for different substrates, and they have been reported to have synergistic effects [31], [32]. Therefore, it is essential to determine the optimal enzymolysis temperature at the corresponding pH for to exert the activity of cellulase. Herein, this study investigated the effect of enzymolysis temperature on the properties of garlic peel-based PC under the condition of pH fixed at 5.5. As can be seen from Fig. S1 and Table S1, both SSA and pore volume of samples reach the maximum value when the enzymolysis temperature is 50 ℃, and the corresponding specific capacitance of samples also shows the same trend (Fig. S2 & Table S2), indicating that the activity of cellulase is the highest at 50 ℃ when pH was 5.5. Consequently, in the next exploration of the optimum ultrasonic frequency, the enzymolysis temperature is determined to be 50 ℃.
[P0018] It can be inferred from Fig. S3 and Table S3 that SSA and pore volume of DUGPC prepared by dual-frequency ultrasound-assisted enzymolysis are the largest, and 40 kHz is superior to 28 kHz under single frequency conditions. This may be due to the fact that oscillation of the cavitation bubble in the dual-frequency ultrasonic field is more unstable than that in the single-frequency ultrasonic field, and it is easier to collapse [33], [34], [35]. Therefore, dual-frequency ultrasound (28 kHz + 40 kHz) produces a stronger cavitation effect than single-frequency ultrasound at the same time, thus promoting the enzymatic hydrolysis process and playing a better “pre-activation” role. Similarly, compared with SUGPC-28 and SUGPC-40, DUGPC exhibits better capacitive performance (Fig. S4 & Table S4). As a result, DUGPC is selected for a comparative study with CGPC-50 and GPC as the control group to further explore its structure-performance relationship. [P0021] The intermediate products and final morphology of all PC samples were observed by SEM (Fig. 1 & Fig. S5). As illustrated in Fig. S5a, the garlic peel still maintains its original bundle structure after carbonization, and the supporting role of cellulose is not significantly affected. However, after pretreatment with cellulase, part of the cellulose was hydrolyzed into small molecule compounds and lost to the outside of the cell wall, so the bundle structure of the carbonized products shrank and some pores were created on the surface (Fig. S5b). When dual-frequency ultrasound is applied, the binding of cellulase to the substrate is enhanced, and the enhanced cavitation effect of ultrasound itself “cuts” the cell wall, resulting in partial damage to the tube bundle structure of the final carbonized product and more uniform dense pores on the surface (Fig. S5c).

[P0025] N2 adsorption–desorption test was conducted to characterize the pore structure of PC samples, and the result was depicted in Fig. 2. According to the IUPAC classification, the isotherms of all three samples are considered to be type IV-B (Fig. 2a). The significant increase of adsorption capacity in the low-pressure region can be attributed to the filling process of micropores, while the hysteresis loop (H4 type) in the middle pressure region is caused by the capillary condensation of mesopores [36]. As the relative pressure continues to increase to P/P0 = 1, the adsorption capacity still has a straight rise trend, which indicates the existence of macropores.

[P0033] Then, the XRD pattern was used to determine the phase composition of three samples (Fig. 3a). In comparison to GPC and CGPC-50, the gradually reducing intensity of DUGPC at the initial low diffraction angle indicates a decrease in the proportion of micropores [36], further supporting the results presented in Fig. 2 and Table 1 above. In addition, all three samples illustrate a (002) peak at ∼ 22.5° and (101) peak at ∼ 43.6°, which correspond to the degree of graphitization. CGPC-50 and DUGPC almost have no miscellaneous peaks compared with GPC, and the lowest full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of DUGPC highlights the beneficial effect of ultrasonic cellulase in enhancing the purity and orderability of materials. As shown in Fig. 3b, Raman spectra was employed to analyze and compare the crystalline structures of three samples. All samples display a D band of ∼ 1346 cm−1 and a G band of ∼ 1598 cm−1, and the intensity ratio between D and G peaks (ID/IG) is indicative of the defect degree of carbon materials. It is obvious that all three samples exhibit a high intensity ratio (ID/IG＞0.8), demonstrating that the etching of KOH during the activation process generates a significant number of defects and active sites, facilitating subsequent charge adsorption and ion migration. Meanwhile, DUGPC shows the smallest value of ID/IG (0.86), indicating a significant improvement in the ordering of the material after ultrasound-cellulase pretreatment. Therefore, a well-structured and ordered garlic peel-derived PC can be fabricated by modifying enzymolysis temperature and ultrasonic frequency.

[P0036] XPS was performed to characterize the surface chemical properties and composition of the samples. All three samples contain C, N and O elements on the surface as shown in Fig. S6, while the O and N contents of DUGPC are both the highest (Table S5). On the one hand, this may be due to the adhesion of surface heteroatoms caused by ultrasonic-cellulase hydrolysis of cellulose and extraction of non-structural compounds, and the small amount of N contained in the remaining cellulase may also lead to the increase of N content. C1s spectra can be further deconvoluted to C=C (284.5 eV), C–C (285.1 eV), C–O (286.2 eV) and O=C–O (289.0 eV) (Fig. 4a–c), O1s spectra can also be divided into C=O, C–OH&C–O–C (C–O), O–C=O and H2O&O2 (chemisorbed oxygen) at 531.5, 532.4, 533.9 and 535.5 eV, respectively (Fig. 4d–f). It should be noted that DUGPC does not contain chemisorbed oxygen, which may be attributed to the powerful oscillation of dual-frequency ultrasound causing surface oxygen molecules to fall off before forming stable chemical bonds. N1s spectra can be deconvolved into three peaks: N-5 (pyrrole N, 399.8 eV), N-Q (graphite N, 400.6 eV) and N-X (oxide N, 402.5 eV), and DUGPC forms a stable N-6 (pyridine N, 399.2 eV) under the strong cavitation of dual-frequency ultrasound. These O/ N-containing functional groups floating on the surface increase the hydrophilicity of PC, and the change of contact angle in Fig. S7 also proves the fact that the wettability is enhanced. Impressively, it is reported that the increase of N-5 and N-6 will also contribute to the formation of pseudocapacitance and thus increase the total capacitance of the DUGPC [37], [38]. Moreover, ICP-MS was executed to detect the metal impurity content of samples which has an adverse effect on the long cyclic performance [39]. As can be seen in Table S6, metal impurities in DUGPC are significantly less than in the other two and contents remaining below 100 ppm. This indicates the effective synergistic removal of impurities through the combined action of ultrasound and cellulase. In summary, DUGPC has the largest SSA, appropriate pore structure, the lowest levels of metal impurities and excellent wettability. Therefore, it is considered a promising candidate for use as an electrode material in advanced supercapacitors.

[P0039] The electrochemical performance of three PC samples as supercapacitor electrodes was tested in a two-electrode system using 6 M KOH as the electrolyte at room temperature. Fig. 5a shows the GCD curves of three samples at 1 A/g, all of which are approximately isosceles triangular shapes. According to Eq. (2), the gravimetric specific capacitance of GPC, CGPC-50, and DUGPC is 304.46, 350.92, and 408.77 F/g, respectively. Clearly, DUGPC exhibits superior capacitive performance than CGPC-50 and the control group. In addition, the CV curves obtained at 100 mV/s (Fig. 5b) all maintain a symmetrical rectangular shape, which means that the specific capacitance contribution of three samples is dominated by electrochemical double-layer capacitance (EDLC) and the adsorption–desorption of electrolyte ions on the surface of electrode material is reversible. DUGPC also has the largest integral area, which corresponds to the result of GCD curves. In detail, the charge storage mechanism of DUGPC is deeply explored by electrochemical kinetic calculations. As can be seen in Fig. S8, the contribution of EDLC to total capacitance in DUGPC gradually increases with the enhancement of scan rate, even reaching 93.1 % at 100 mV/s. The specific capacitance at different current densities (i.e., rate performance) of all samples is shown in Fig. 5c, DUGPC is consistently superior to the other two samples apparently. To expand, the capacitance retention rate of DUGPC is still as high as 81.8 % within the range of 1–50 A/g, while the GPC and CGPC-50 are 63.0 % and 76.6 %, respectively. Moreover, the DUGPC also demonstrates extraordinary EDLC performance in the three-electrode system as expected, with a specific capacitance of 456.57 F/g at 1 A/g (Fig. S9). Impressively, the capacitive performance of DUGPC exceeds that of most recently reported biomass-based PCs (Table S7), and its outstanding capacitive performance can be attributed to: 1) the highest SSA, sufficient micropores and abundant hierarchical pore structure of DUGPC contribute to excellent EDLC, and a small number of N/O functional groups also produce pseudocapacitance due to faradaic reactions; 2) the excellent wettability facilitates the diffusion of electrolyte ions, and the high proportion of mesopores provides a high-speed channel for ion transport, which guarantees its superior rate performance at the high current density.

[P0042] As can be seen from Fig. 5d, the curves of three samples all intersect the X-axis in the high-frequency region and present an arc shape of approximately semicircle. The intercept of the plot with the X-axis corresponds to the equivalent series resistance (Rs), while the radius of the arc reflects the magnitude of the charge transfer resistance (Rct). It can be calculated from the fitted equivalent circuit model that the Rs (0.15 Ω) and Rct (0.51 Ω) of DUGPC are both the smallest, much lower than that of GPC (0.24, 0.82 Ω) and CGPC-50 (0.21, 0.73 Ω). Furthermore, the slope of the line in the low-frequency region is correlated with the Warburg impedance and all three curves are nearly vertical lines, which means that although there is a small amount of pseudocapacitance, the EDLC characteristics are still overwhelmingly dominant [12]. Meanwhile, the lower diffusion impedance also indicates outstanding charge and electrolyte ion transfer performance, which is attributed to the low interface resistance (high hydrophilicity) and well-developed pore structure induced by ultrasonic-assisted enzymolysis.
[P0043] Although the above physical and electrochemical characterization has demonstrated that DUGPC can be used as a potential electrode material candidate for supercapacitors, its performance in practical applications remains to be further investigated. For this purpose, we constructed an aqueous symmetric supercapacitor (ASSC) in 6 M KOH based on two DUGPC electrodes with the same load and a cellulosic separator (Fig. 6a). As shown in Fig. 6b, GCD curves of ASSC at different current densities all exhibit obvious EDLC behavior, which means that electrolyte ions can achieve rapid reversible adsorption–desorption on the surface of DUGPC. There is almost no voltage drop at the instant of switching from charging to discharging, which further confirms that DUGPC has a smaller series resistance. CV curves at different scanning rates maintain a regular rectangular shape (Fig. 6c), indicating the high-speed transfer of ions inside the electrode, which also corresponds to the EDLC characteristics in Fig. 6a. The response rate of the stable platform is faster when the scanning voltage direction is changed, which proves that the dispersed capacitance effect is smaller. Impressively, the ASSC device still retain 95.5 % of its initial capacitance after 10,000 cycles at 5 A/g and the GCD curve of the 10,001 cycle is well-symmetrical (Fig. 6d), largely owing to the well-distributed stable layered pore structure of DUGPC that enables ion buffering and rapid transport. From the SEM images of DUGPC electrode before and after 10,000 cycles (Fig. S10), it can be seen that there is almost no morphological change and structural collapse. Even after 20,000 cycles, the capacitance retention of ASSC is still up to 94.9 % (Fig. S11). In addition, it should be noted that the abnormal increase in specific capacitance during the initial cycles may be attributed to enhanced pseudocapacitance induced by rising temperatures, and the electrode wetting and self-activation should not be overlooked. As the number of cycles increases, the pseudocapacitance generated by faradic reaction of certain oxygen-containing functional groups is irreversibly lost, resulting in partial degradation of the specific capacitance [40].

[P0047] Moreover, to achieve a high energy density, a 2.8 V high-potential symmetric supercapacitor was assembled using DUGPC in a 1 M TEABF4/AN electrolyte. In Fig. S12a, the GCD curves still present an approximately symmetric triangular shape, indicating that energy storage mechanism of the device in the organic system remains unchanged. However, compared to aqueous systems, organic supercapacitors display a noticeable IR drop and asymmetry. This may be attributed to the challenges associated with larger ionic desorption and the polarization effects of the organic electrolyte environment. Correspondingly, the CV curves in Fig. S12b maintain an approximately rectangular shape. Fig. S12c illustrates the rate performance of DUGPC electrode in both aqueous and organic electrolytes. DUGPC electrode can deliver a specific capacitance of 180.71 F/g at 1 A/g in TEABF4/AN electrolyte, and capacitance retention reaching as high as 84.9 % (compared to 81.8 % in KOH electrolyte) even at 50 A/g. Although the larger ions cannot penetrate the smaller micropores resulting in lower specific capacitance, the transport distance in the larger micropores and mesopores is significantly shortened, thus improving the ion migration rate and obtaining a better rate performance. Benefiting from the high potential window of the organic electrolyte, the DUGPC//TEABF4//DUGPC configuration achieves a high energy density of 58.96 Wh kg−1 at a power density of 139.86 W kg−1, far exceeding that of aqueous devices (Fig. S12d). It is anticipated that this straightforward strategy, commonly employed in biochemistry and pharmaceutical fields, will excel in the synthesis and modification of biomass-based PCs for applications in adsorption, catalysis, and energy storage, thereby facilitating the high-value and multilevel utilization of biomass.
[P0048] Based on the analysis and discussion of the comparative experiments presented above, it can be concluded that dual-frequency ultrasound and cellulase work synergistically in the “pre-activation” of garlic peel, as illustrated in Scheme 2. To be specific, this sonobiocatalytic synergy can be summarized in two aspects: 1) Ultrasound-induced microjets and shear forces impact and exfoliate the “bunkers” (lignin, hemicellulose, etc.) wrapped around the cellulose, increasing the contact area between the cellulase and substrate. Further, ultrasonic cavitation also partially breaks the hydrogen bond between the cellulose and exposes more active sites, thereby improving the accessibility and affinity between cellulase and substrate [29], [49]; 2) The change of secondary conformation induced by ultrasonic cavitation bubble rupture promoted the stretching of the enzyme structure and the binding of the substrate to the active center of the enzyme, thus improving the activity of cellulase and enzymolysis kinetics [50], [51]. For PC, the synergistic effect of the two causes cracks and pores on the surface of the precursor and within the channels. This promotes the development of pores in PC, allowing it to store and transport electrolyte ions in large quantities. Additionally, the mass transfer of impurities and extractable components from the garlic peel cells reduces impurity content, enhancing the stability and connectivity of PC. Furthermore, the –OH and other oxygen-containing groups produced by enzymatic hydrolysis significantly increase the hydrophilicity of PC, thereby boosting the ion transport rate. Consequently, this synergistic effect of sonobiocatalysis-induced changes in the precursor’s structure and composition ultimately contributes to substantial improvements in the capacitive performance of PC.

[P0051] In summary, we have successfully demonstrated a dual-frequency ultrasonic-assisted enzymolysis strategy utilizing garlic peel for the preparation of hierarchical PC. Specifically, the degradation of cellulose induced by ultrasonic-cellulase results in the formation of numerous original pores on the surface and within the inner tube bundle of precursor. In addition, this synergistic effect of sonobiocatalysis generates free oxygen-containing groups and facilitates the removal of internal impurities. As a result, the obtained PC (DUGPC) exhibits an ultra-high SSA, low metal impurity content and high wettability, all of which contribute to its outstanding electrochemical performance. Consequently, DUGPC shows a specific capacitance of 408.77 F/g at 1 A/g and a retention of 81.8 % even at 50 A/g, which is superior to most reported biomass-based PCs. The assembled ASSC has a high energy density of 15.78 Wh kg−1 when power density is 50.04 W kg−1, and delivers an excellent stability of 95.5 % after 10,000 cycles at 5 A/g. Impressively, the assembled 2.8 V organic symmetric supercapacitors have an energy density of up to 58.96 Wh kg−1 (corresponding power density of 139.86 W kg−1). This work provides a new idea for structural control of high-performance PC, and is expected to integrate with bio-refining technology to enable the comprehensive utilization of biomass waste.
