[P0001] The increasing demand of wearable electronic devices highlights the need to develop energy storage systems with exceptional electrochemical properties, but also robust and flexible.1 All-solid-state flexible supercapacitors (SSFSCs) constitute a potential option to be assembled in wearable devices as smart watches, smart clothes, sensor systems, and any other fabric demanding energy storage ability.2,3,4 Carbon fabrics can be an excellent substrate for electrodes in these applications due to their outstanding conductive properties, robustness, accessibility, and cost.5 However, the main drawback in chosen woven carbon fiber (WCF) as electrodes for supercapacitors is their poor specific surface area (SSA) even in highly rough commercial fibers. To overcome this issue, different modifications of carbon fibers have been proposed to improve their surface Brunauer, Emmett, and Teller (BET) area and enhance their energy storage performance.6,7,8,9 One of the most successful surface modifications of WCFs, in terms of improving their surface area, is carbon aerogel (CAG).10,11,12 We have recently reported the introduction of CAG and other surface modifications on carbon fiber and their application as electrodes in flexible supercapacitors.13 Despite the specific capacitance of the modified fibers using CAG being very promising (∼2–5 F/g) and comparable to other fancy modifications, the introduction of these CAGs requires multiple fabrication steps (preparation of the Resorcinol-Formaldehyde resin or RF polymer, curing, aerogel formation, and pyrolysis, among others). As an alternative, some biomass-derived porous carbon materials have been explored for energy storage devices.14 Lignin, characterized as an aromatic heteropolymer, holds promise as a prospective reservoir of organic compounds and synthetic polymers. Lignin hydrogel is emerging as a sustainable matrix in composite materials.15 Lignin has also been used as a starting polymer to substitute Polyacrylonitrile (PAN) in the synthesis of carbon fibers.16 Porous carbon from lignin can be obtained by various methods, including pyrolysis and template techniques.17 Furthermore, lignin has been used to fabricate three-dimensional hierarchical porous lignin-derived carbon for supercapacitor electrodes, yielding materials with outstanding SSA.18,19,20,21 In addition to enhancing surface area, lignin biopolymer has been used as a green protective coating on carbon fiber to improve mechanical properties and prevent fiber degradation.22,23 For instance, Szabó et al.24 reported the use of lignin as a functional green coating in carbon fiber-reinforce polymers to improve the interfacial adhesion between the fibers and resins. In these cases, lignin is grafted onto the surface of carbon fibers through chemical bonds with epoxide groups and tosylated lignin.25 More recently, Kaya and coworkers used lignin to protect cotton fibers from degradation in composites.26 Despite lignin’s great potential as a starting material in flexible supercapacitors,27 its use remains limited, likely due to the time-consuming, expensive, or environmentally hazardous modification procedures. To overcome these challenges, a more sustainable surface modification of lignin can be envisaged as an opportunity to increase the SSA of carbon fibers to use them as electrodes in energy storage devices.
[P0002] Herein, we report an easy procedure to modify the surface of WCFs using lignin and a sizing agent as a binder. We present the electrochemical and morphological characterization of the modified fibers and use them as electrodes for solid-state flexible supercapacitors in a H3PO4/Poly(Vinyl Alcohol) (PVA) matrix electrolyte. We have discussed the properties of the fabricated devices and demonstrated a proof of concept, where the system exhibiting the best electrochemical performance powers on red and yellow light-emitting diodes (LEDs).
[P0003] WCFs were modified by applying a suspension containing different amount of lignin (3, 6, and 10 wt %). A commercial sizing solution, which includes a polymer dispersant, was used as a binder to enhance the adhesion of the lignin to the fibers. The fabrication procedure was straightforward: the lignin suspension was directly applied to the fibers, either by dip (DC) or spray coating (SC), after a washing and drying step. A scheme of the procedure used to modify the fibers is depicted in Figure 1. Most samples underwent a standard vertical dip-coating process. For comparison, one sample was modified by horizontally dipping the fiber (DCh) to observe any differences. [P0006] Some samples were calcined to obtain a more porous lignin structure that could improve their electrochemical behavior. The condition used for the calcination was an initial step of heating using a heating rate of 5°C/min from 30°C to 600°C, 30 min at 600°C, and then cooling down at the same rate (5°C/min). The furnace was connected to a continuous argon stream to avoid oxidation of the fibers. This calcination condition was stablished considering the known effects of calcination on plain WCFs, which significantly reduces its mechanical performance even in an inert atmosphere.10 We have analyzed the samples by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) to assess their behavior toward the temperature. Also, plain WCF and commercial lignin were tested for comparison. In the TGA of the commercial lignin used (Figure S1, see supplemental information), the calcination procedure at 600°C led to the disappearance of almost 45 wt % of the solid. To check the functional groups lost during calcination, commercial lignin was analyzed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy before and after the calcination process (See Figure S2). From the spectroscopic data previously found in the literature,19,28 it was possible to assign the different functional groups to the infrared (IR) transmittance peaks. After calcination at 600°C, the lignin charcoal residue mainly contains aromatic C=C groups and C=C-O groups, meaning that the methoxy, aldehydes, and some ketones disappear during the process but still some phenols remain in the structure. These results agree with the previous study reported by Cao and coworkers.29 The TGA of the commercial WCF used, also depicted in Figure S1, shows a more pronounced weight loss beyond 600°C, probably associated with an undesirable oxidation process caused by the oxygenated sizing and lignin.

[P0020] As the scan rate increases, the area of the potential scanning also rises, as would be expected for typical supercapacitor behavior. This behavior was also observed for the rest of the modified fibers. The specific capacitances showed that spray coating is more effective than dip coating and it affords values over ∼4 F/g even at smaller concentrations (Table 1; Figure 3C).
[P0021] The lignin loadings in wt % for samples DCx4-10C, SCx4-10, and DChx4-10 were determined by TGA, and the values were 20.5, 16.8, and 15.8 wt %, respectively. Since the lignin loadings are very similar, the observed difference is likely related to the coating distribution rather than the amount of lignin. For these non-viscous lignin suspensions, the dip coating could not be the most profitable method since all particles are dragged to the bottom of the carbon fiber as soon as it leaves the bath. This observation also applies for the horizontal dip coating, where the specific capacitance seems to be independent of the orientation in the coating process (Table 1, entry 6 Csp 3.45 F/g vs. entry 9 Csp 3.51 F/g, at 10 mVs-1). On the contrary, spray coating appears highly suitable as the carbon fibers lie horizontally on a surface, receiving and retaining the sprayed lignin suspension. In this scenario, all sprayed particles adhere to the fibers, rather than remaining in the suspension, as is the case with the dip-coating process.
[P0022] The calcined carbon fibers (DC, DCh, and SC) were examined using Field Emission Gun Scanning Electron Microscope (FEGSEM). Figure 4 displays the analysis of fibers with 6 wt % lignin and four layers, while Figures 5 and S3 (supplemental information) illustrate fibers with 10 wt % lignin and four layers. The images look very similar, with an aleatory distribution of lignin through the surface, but the samples with 10 wt % of lignin appear to be more covered than the samples with 6 wt %. In all cases, blocky structures can be observed, typical of lignin’s microstructure, and some needle-like structure, which are attributed to the insoluble lignin remaining in the suspension. The attempts to measure the surface BET area of the carbon fibers were unsuccessful. They were not reproducible, but in all cases the values were one order of magnitude higher than that of the plain fiber (∼0.2 m2/g). For comparison the SSA of calcined and commercial lignin was measured by BET method, and the measurements for both samples afforded similar values (0.35 m2/g; Figures S4 and S5; Table S1). The electrochemical behavior of a carbon electrode in supercapacitors is determined by the surface area, keeping the ions adsorbed, and the specific capacitance is directly related to it. In this study, the enhanced performance of the electrodes after calcination suggested that the sizing (polymer dispersant) improves the area of the covering (soluble lignin in aqueous sizing), by acting as a template. [P0045] The GCD test depicted in Figures 6C and 6D for LIG-DP and LIG-SC, respectively, showed the typical charge/discharged curves for a supercapacitor. The charged process of the sample LIG-SC shows more variability at higher current densities (Figure 6D). Also, at high current densities the electrode’s IR drop is significant, especially for the spray-coating device. This behavior is usual when current densities increase.31 However, when it is too remarkable, it can be attributed to an increase in the resistances of the circuit, due to a poor contact between the device components (electrodes-electrolyte, electrodes-current collector, etc.), which leads to energy losses and reduced efficiency. As mentioned earlier (Table 3), the device fabricated with spray coating presents higher resistances values than its homologs.
[P0046] From the tests parameters and according to the mass of the electrodes, it was possible to calculate the specific capacitance (Csp), the energy density (E), and the average power density (Pa) according to Equations shown in STAR Methods details. Conversely, at a comparable current density, the LIG-SC (entry 5) demonstrates double the capacitance at 2.13 F/g at 0.22 mA/g (yielding an E of 296.1 mWh/kg and a Pa of 110.0 mW/kg).

For current density of 1.35 mA/g (entry 8), the value of the specific capacitance is equivalent to the value obtained through Equation 1 using CV test data. In general, the sample modified by spray coating exhibits a better specific capacitances and power densities.

[P0060] Potential application of lignin, as a renewable and unlimited source of aromatic natural polymer to modify and improve the surface of carbon fibers, opens the possibilities of environmentally benign procedures. We have introduced superficial modifications by incorporating lignin and subsequently subjecting it to pyrolysis to enhance the electrochemical response of the WCFs. Two distinct methods for this modification were investigated. According to electrochemical assessments, produced devices using lignin via dip and spray coating exhibit comparable behavior, achieving 5.4 F/g at 10 mVs-1 in the best fiber found. The modified carbon fiber, infused with lignin, was utilized as electrodes in the fabrication of SSFSCs with H3PO4/PVA as electrolyte. These SSFSCs demonstrated commendable electrochemical performance (up to 2,131.6 mF/g at 0.22 mA/g and up to E = 296.06 mWh/kg and Pa of 664 mW/kg), a superior performance compared to SSFSCs prepared with CAG-WCFs embedded in the same matrix. A proof of concept involving a yellow LED powered on for more than 6 min and a bending test were conducted, revealing that the capacitor could recover 99% of its capacitance when returned to its original unbent position.
[P0061] The procedure reported in this article clearly reveals the improvement of the electrochemical properties of the plain fiber after the modification. However, the WCF is not the most porous material, and it limits the application of these electrodes to a commendable electrochemical performance capacitor. The method reported here opens the possibility to modify the surface of carbon fibers in a cheaper mode (instead of CAG for instance) and to use it as coatings in a more porous substrate than can exhibit an outstanding electrochemical behavior.

[P0071] In all cases, fabric was washed with ethanol before any modification and dried in the oven at 60°C. Lignin suspensions (3, 6 and 10 wt. %, respectively) were prepared in distilled water and sizing solution (5 wt. %) and stirred at room temperature for 30 min before their use.
[P0072] The carbon fiber fabrics were placed in contact of the lignin suspension by dip or spray coating procedures and dried in the oven at 80°C for 10 more minutes until solvent evaporation. [P0079] The devices were assembled following a sandwich type configuration carbon fiber/separator/carbon fiber all embedded in gel polymer electrolyte. The electrolyte was prepared by mixing H3PO4 (3 g) and PVA (3 g) in distilled water (30 mL) under stirring at 85°C for 30 min. The carbon fibers, previously attached to a copper wire collector (silver ink), and the glass fiber were immersed in the electrolyte during a few minutes. Then, the layers were piled up over a metallic mold in this order: carbon fiber/separator/carbon fiber and left to cast at 60°C for 8 h.
