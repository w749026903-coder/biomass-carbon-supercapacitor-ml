[P0001] Energy storage is becoming more critical as large-scale renewable energy projects come online. Electrochemical energy storage (EES) systems, particularly supercapacitors, are widely studied due to their efficiency, reliability, and environmental benefits. Supercapacitors are especially attractive for their high power density, fast charge–discharge rates, and long cycle life. − Supercapacitors can operate on two distinct mechanisms, or a combination of the two: (a) electric double-layer capacitance (EDLC), based on electrostatic charge accumulation at the electrode–electrolyte interface, (b) pseudocapacitance from Faradaic redox reactions. ,
[P0002] The internal resistance of an electrochemical device is an important factor that directly affects its performance. In supercapacitors, this internal resistance is often referred to as the equivalent series resistance (ESR). The key factors that contribute to ESR are the interfacial resistance between neighboring parts of the device, including the electrode–current collector interface, electrode–electrolyte interface, and connections among active and inactive materials. The resistance is also associated with ion transport in the electrodes and electrolyte, and the resistance encountered by ions as they pass through the separator.
[P0003] Engineering the electrode to possess certain properties could reduce the internal resistance. High electrical conductivity supports efficient charge transfer, and a well-developed porous structure facilitates shorter ion diffusion paths, reducing ion transport resistance. − Various porous carbon structures utilizing graphene, carbon nanotubes, carbon nanofibers, and variations of activated carbon (AC) have been studied for their potential as EDLC electrode materials. − ACs have long been the preferred materials for fabricating supercapacitor electrodes due to their affordability, large specific surface area, and excellent long-term stability. , However, their electrochemical performance is highly dependent on their pore structure, and, due to their defective and disordered structure, the conductivity of ACs is lower than that of highly ordered, graphitic carbon materials. ,
[P0004] Graphene, by contrast, composed entirely of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms arranged in a two-dimensional structure, has garnered significant research interest owing to its high electrical and thermal conductivity, mechanical strength, large theoretical specific surface area, and strong chemical stability. ,,− However, several challenges still need to be addressed. Graphene sheets are prone to agglomeration and restacking due to strong van der Waals forces and π–π interactions, which significantly reduce their accessible surface area, effective specific capacitance, and electrical conductivity. Additionally, the synthesis processes for graphene are often complex and expensive, which restricts its potential for large-scale applications. To address these limitations, the integration of graphene into AC-based electrodes has been proposed as a viable approach to enhance their electrochemical performance. The combination leverages the high conductivity of graphene to compensate for the poor conductivity of ACs, while also preserving a favorable porous structure for ion transport.
[P0005] Graphene can be synthesized through a variety of methods. One promising top-down approach is the electrochemical exfoliation of graphite, in which ions are intercalated between the individual sheets, causing expansion of interlayer spacing and eventually exfoliation to graphene. However, the production of graphene from this method faces challenges, particularly the availability of graphite as the feedstock. Graphite is in high demand, particularly as a key component in lithium-ion batteries. For example, the United States relies almost entirely on trade for its graphite supply. As a promising domestic alternative, coal-derived graphite exhibits favorable graphitization and electrical conductivity, making it suitable for electrochemical exfoliation. Despite its potential, limited studies have explored coal-derived graphite for graphene production, and even fewer have evaluated its performance in practical device applications.
[P0006] Previous studies have reported incorporating graphene into carbon-based supercapacitor electrodes; however, most systems rely on graphene oxide (GO) or GO-derived materials obtained through chemical reduction, hydrothermal treatment, doping, or other modification strategies ,,− for coin-cell supercapacitors and − for two-electrode supercapacitors. The synthesis route influences defect density, conductivity, surface functionality, and interlayer structure, which directly affect electrochemical performance. In this work, graphene produced by electrochemical exfoliation of coal-derived graphite without further modification is evaluated as an additive in activated carbon electrodes, and the resulting performance improvement is quantified through direct comparison with a baseline electrode fabricated and tested under identical conditions. While graphene-based supercapacitors are promising, an optimum graphene–AC ratio is required, as excessive graphene loading can reduce devices’ performance, as shown in recent works. ,,,
[P0007] Therefore, exfoliated coal-derived graphene is incorporated into activated carbon electrodes at 0, 3, 5, and 10 wt % to determine the graphene content that maximizes supercapacitor performance. The electrochemical behavior is evaluated in symmetric coin cells with KOH electrolyte using cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). ESR is determined using both electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and direct current internal resistance (DCIR) measurements. Long-term cycling stability is also assessed over 100,000 charge–discharge cycles, which exceeds the cycle numbers commonly reported in graphene additive studies (typically 5,000–10,000 cycles ,,− ).
[P0008] Activated carbon and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) were purchased from MSE Supplies (Tucson, AZ), and MTI Corporation (Richmond, CA), respectively. N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, biotech grade, ≥ 99.00%) was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. All materials were used as received without further purification. The graphene used in this study was electrochemically exfoliated coal-derived graphene produced in our laboratory. For the exfoliation process, graphitized foamed coal supplied by CONSOL Innovations were used as the working electrode, while a platinum mesh served as both the counter and reference electrode. More details about the electrochemical exfoliation procedure and characterization of coal-derived graphene can be found in this group’s previous work. In the present study, nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis was performed to evaluate the pore characteristics. The coal-derived graphene exhibited average pore diameters in the range of 15.45–25.29 nm based on the (Brunauere-Emmette-Teller) BET method and 26.55–35.95 nm based on the (Barrett–Joyner–Halenda) BJH method, indicating a predominantly mesoporous structure.
[P0009] An electrode slurry was prepared by mixing 94 wt % active material with 6 wt % PVDF binder dissolved in NMP. The active material was formulated in four different compositions as listed in Table S1, incorporating varying amounts of graphene (0, 3, 5, and 10 wt %) with AC. The mixture was homogenized using a FlackTek 330–100 PRO mechanical stirrer at 3000 rpm for 2 min to ensure uniform dispersion of components. The resulting slurry was coated onto nickel foil using the doctor blade method, targeting a wet film thickness of approximately 25 μm. The electrodes were dried at 80 °C for 24 h to remove residual solvent. After drying, the electrodes were compressed using a roller press to ensure uniform thickness and then punched into discs. These discs were used to assemble CR2032 coin cells in a symmetric two-electrode configuration. A borosilicate glass microfiber membrane with a 0.6 μm pore size was employed as the separator, and 6 M KOH was used as the electrolyte. The cells were sealed using an MSK-110 hydraulic crimping machine. Following assembly, the cells were allowed to rest overnight to ensure electrolyte wetting.
[P0010] Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were conducted at scan rates ranging from 5 to 100 mV/s, with a voltage window of 0–1 V. Galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) tests were subsequently performed at currents from 5 to 100 mA, corresponding to ∼0.3–7 A/g for our electrodes. Direct current internal resistance (DCIR) testing was also conducted between GCD cycles, applying current steps from 5 to 100 mA (0.3–7 A/g) with a pulse duration of 1 ms to evaluate dynamic resistance under transient load conditions. Cycling stability was also assessed over 100,000 GCD cycles at 50 mA. CV, GCD, and DCIR were conducted using an Arbin LBT21084 battery tester. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed separately using a Gamry Interface 5000E instrument across a frequency range of 100 kHz to 1 Hz, with an AC current amplitude of 0.01 A (rms).

[P0017] To evaluate the impact of incorporating exfoliated coal-derived graphene into activated carbon (AC)-based electrodes, symmetric supercapacitor coin cells (CR2032 type) were assembled using 6 M KOH electrolyte. The electrochemical performance of these devices was assessed through CV, GCD, DCIR, ESR, and cycling stability analyses. Finally, the performance of the coal-derived graphene-based electrodes was compared with previously reported literature.

[P0026] Regarding specific capacitance, a key indicator of energy storage capability, all electrodes demonstrated moderate values between ∼13 and 17 F/g across the tested currents. As shown in Table (at 50 mA), 3 wt % exfoliated coal-derived graphene showed a ∼5% improvement, indicating overall positive impacts from graphene inclusion. This may be due to the greater porosity and conjugation structure of graphene, which improves fast ion movements during the charge–discharge process. In contrast, electrodes with higher graphene loadings exhibited slightly lower capacitance values, showing a similar trend to the findings of Redondo et al.
[P0027] A similar trend was observed for energy density, which was highest at lower currents and decreased at higher currents. At a low current of 5 mA, all electrode compositions exhibited relatively similar energy densities, ranging from 2.31 to 2.39 Wh/kg, with the highest values observed for electrodes containing 3 and 5 wt % exfoliated coal-derived graphene. As summarized in Table , at a constant current of 50 mA, the inclusion of graphene generally enhanced energy storage capabilities.
[P0028] The GCD profiles of both the baseline and electrodes with some graphene content (Figures S1 and 2c) display nearly ideal triangular shapes, consistent with electrostatic charge storage typical of EDLCs. Additionally, the similarity in charging and discharging durations points to high Coulombic efficiency.

[P0030] As shown in Tables S2 and S3, ESR values remained stable across different current levels. The baseline electrode exhibited the highest resistance (∼0.54 Ω). The addition of graphene reduced ESR in all samples, with values ranging from 0.36 Ω to 0.43 Ω. [P0035] To validate the findings, EIS data for the coin cell supercapacitors were analyzed using an equivalent circuit model, R S–(CPE1 || R ct–Wo1), where R S is ESR, CPE1 is a constant phase element representing the double-layer capacitance and surface heterogeneities, R ct is the charge transfer resistance, and Wo1 accounts for ion diffusion through the porous electrode. Fitting was performed using Python’s impedance.py library (Figure S2). In the Nyquist plot, the high-to-mid frequency semicircle corresponds to R ct and CPE1. Due to deviations from ideality, CPE is used instead of a pure capacitor to account for factors like electrode roughness and pore distribution. The CPE has two parameters: α (ideal behavior) and C (capacitance). A value of α = 1 indicates ideal capacitive behavior, while α = 0.9–1 is typical for EDLCs with porous surfaces. At lower frequencies, the slope of the inclined line represents the Warburg impedance. A steeper slope indicates efficient electric double-layer formation, whereas a smaller slope suggests ion diffusion limitations. Warburg elements yield two parameters: W-R (diffusion resistance) and W-T (diffusion time constant). For finite-length diffusion, the W-T parameter is defined as W−T=L2D , where L is the diffusion length of cations within the electrode and D is their chemical diffusivity.
[P0036] The fitted parameters for all samples are summarized in Table . The CPE1-derived α values increased with graphene addition. The baseline electrode exhibited an α of 0.91. Exfoliated coal-derived graphene showed higher α values at all loadings: 0.97 at 3 wt %, 0.95 at 5 wt %, and 0.97 at 10 wt %, indicating nearly ideal capacitive behavior.

[P0045] Capacitance retention data are shown in Figure b. Capacitance retention further confirms the long-term stability of all electrodes, as the LOB refers to the point at which the capacitance retention drops to 80%. Electrodes with 3, 5, and 10 wt % graphene exhibited stable cycling, retaining 97.80%, 85.39%, and 89.54% of their initial capacitance, respectively, after 100,000 cycles. However, the results indicate that increasing graphene content beyond the optimal level does not necessarily enhance cycling stability.
[P0046] To benchmark the electrodes containing some coal-derived graphene, performance was compared to previously reported graphene-based coin cell devices, as summarized in Table . Capacitors fabricated using 3 wt % electrochemically exfoliated coal-derived graphene showed lower specific capacitance than rGO-based systems reported in the literature. However, they exhibited lower ESR, which contributed to higher power output and faster charge–discharge behavior. The devices also maintained stable performance over 100,000 cycles, indicating potentially better long-term durability compared to earlier studies. These results suggest that coal-derived graphene, while having lower capacitance, can still provide practical advantages in terms of rate capability and power delivery in aqueous electrolyte systems.

[P0050] This study investigates the electrochemical performance of supercapacitor electrodes incorporating different loadings of exfoliated coal-derived graphene, produced from domestic coal-derived graphite foam via a simple anodic exfoliation technique using an aqueous electrolyte. The results show that graphene enhances the performance of activated carbon-based electrodes. At 3 wt %, electrodes demonstrated the lowest ESR.
[P0051] DCIR results show that electrodes with 3, 5, and 10 wt % exfoliated coal-derived graphene reduced ESR by 18–34% compared to the baseline. Power density increased, with 3, 5, and 10 wt % samples reaching improvements of about ∼52%, ∼27%, and ∼35%, respectively. Specific capacitance and energy density also increased, with 3 and 5 wt % loadings increasing by approximately 3.3–5%, while 10 wt % showed a slightly lower gain (∼2.3%). EIS analysis confirmed reduced ESR, with the lowest ESR (0.23 Ω) at 3 wt % loading. Furthermore, exfoliated coal-derived graphene electrodes demonstrated excellent cycling stability, retaining over 85% capacitance after 1,00,000 cycles at ∼3 A/g.
[P0052] These results highlight coal-derived graphite foam as an effective precursor for exfoliated coal-derived graphene, which enhances electrode conductivity, capacitance, and power density, making it a promising additive for advanced supercapacitors.
