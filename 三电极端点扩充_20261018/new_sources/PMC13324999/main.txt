[P0001] Rapid industrial growth, technological breakthroughs, and expanding global economies have sharply increased the demand for energy, raising urgent concerns over sustainability and environmental preservation. The repeated use of conventional fossil-based fuels has accelerated issues such as greenhouse gas emission and progressive ozone depletion, which pose serious risks to ecological safety. In this direction, the design of energy storage and adaptation systems that are resourceful and recyclable plays an essential role in achieving cleaner and more resilient energy in the future.1 Among various technologies, supercapacitors (SCs) are referred to as electrochemical capacitors, which have attracted a lot of attention owing to their green energy storage capability with remarkable features, extended life cycle, high electrical conductivity and rapid charge and discharge cycles. Considering the approach to charge storage, SCs may be divided into two categories based on their charge storage mechanism: faradaic pseudocapacitors and electric double-layer capacitors (EDLCs). Moreover, they undergo a non-faradaic redox process to store charge and are occasionally referred to as pseudocapacitors, hybrid capacitors, and EDLCs. The charge at the interface between a conducting electrode surface and an electrolyte is separated and electrostatically stored in a Helmholtz double layer.2,3 Supercapacitors still have a lower energy density than batteries. To overcome this limitation, researchers have concentrated on developing innovative electrode materials that can increase capacitance and energy storage capacity. This pursuit has resulted in an immense amount of research into various nanomaterials to create high-performance supercapacitor electrodes.3 Biomass has been recognized as a possible precursor for the production of activated carbons because it is an abundant, renewable, and green carbon resource that has a natural biological structure for the development of porous nanostructures.4 The requirement for fossil fuels can be replaced by a variety of renewable energy alternatives including solar, wind, geothermal, and biomass-based carbon energy. Recent studies have demonstrated that the biomass-derived activated carbon is regarded as an affordable and efficient electrode material for SCs. Additionally, the efficient development of sustainable energy and environmental protection are facilitated by the use of biomass derivatives.5 Perez-Chavez et al.6 stated that the activated carbon derived from the waste of corn husk and agave bagasse has a better capacitance capacity because it has better porosity. Mendoza-Jimenez et al.7 established that graphene oxide obtained from spent lithium-ion batteries increases conductivity and electroactive surface area. Garces-Patino et al.8 utilized the recycled battery materials to develop some electrolytes, and this enhanced the ionic conductivity and device performance. Similarly, Ojeda et al.9 demonstrated that the integration of photosensitive electrodes has the ability to expand supercapacitor capacitance. Amongst several other items, peanut shell (PS) is the key by-product of the manufacture of peanuts, which is an agro-industrial waste, and particularly attractive due to its high carbon content, intrinsic porosity, and renewable nature. It is one of the most extensively grown oil-producing crops worldwide. While millions of tons of peanut shells are disposed of each year, 40 million metric tons of peanuts, an oil-bearing crop, are produced worldwide each year.10 The activated carbon (AC) can be prepared by physical activation at 700–1200 °C in a two-step process, but chemical activation occurs at 400–1000 °C, which combines activation and carbonization in a single step at a comparatively low temperature. In most cases, ZnCl2, H3PO4, H2SO4, and KOH are used for chemical activation.11 Amongst these, NaOH activation efficiently creates a hierarchical porous structure that includes mesopores and micropores essential for improving supercapacitor ion transport and storage capacity. These substances enable carbonization and activation to occur simultaneously by acting as oxidizing and dehydrating reagents. Two key benefits of chemical activation are its high yield and ability to be activated at quite low temperatures. A well-developed porous structure with both micropores and mesopores is obtained by NaOH-AC, and mesopores are more likely to form under NaOH activation than under KOH activation.12,13 Currently, a lot of research is being done on the development of carbon-based electrode materials from biomass, which have quite high surface areas that approach 2000–3000 m2 g−1 and moderate specific capacitance values in the range of 200–300 F g−1. For instance, in order to create porous carbon materials with a characteristic three-position interleaved structure, Cao et al.14 prepared walnut shell-derived carbons using potassium chloride (KCl) as a template with CO2 activation. When exposed to 6 M KOH at a current density of 0.1 A g−1, the resultant carbon showed a high specific surface area of 1958 m2 g−1 and a specific capacitance of 245 F g−1 (ref. 14). In the development of high-performance supercapacitor electrodes, binary and ternary metal oxide composites, including NiO/MnO2 (ref. 15) and Co3O4@SiO2/rGO,16 have attracted a lot of attention. Here, we effectively created new MgO/SiO2 binary nanocomposites (NCs) and MgO/SiO2/GO ternary nanocomposites comprising GO, SiO2, and MgO using green carbon derived from peanut shells. The carbon framework's surface area and porosity were successfully increased by the activation process using NaOH. MgO and SiO2 have attracted interest as functional additives in supercapacitor electrodes because of their special qualities that improve the electrochemical performance. The incorporation of GO into the MgO/SiO2 composite produced a highly conductive matrix that greatly enhanced the kinetics of charge transfer.17 By adding more active sites for charge storage, it can improve ion diffusion pathways in composite electrodes. For instance, using a hydrometallurgical process, Aydin et al., created a ZnO–MgO hybrid material from ferrochrome ash waste. The supercapacitor containing the anode calcined at 750 °C exhibited a high specific capacitance of 486 mF cm−2 at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1 and retained 93% of its initial capacitance after 5000 charge–discharge cycles at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2. This performance was attributed to the synergistic effect of MgO with ZnO, enhancing the electrochemical properties of the electrode.18 SiO2 has become a significant component for supercapacitor electrode materials because of its high surface area, chemical stability, and capacity to improve active material dispersion in the electrode matrix. SiO2 can increase the energy density and capacitance by lowering internal resistance and improving electrolyte accessibility. Lo et al. showed that adding 6 weight percent of 100 nm SiO2 microspheres to CMK-3 carbon mesoporous materials enhanced the specific capacitance from 133 Fg−1 to 298 Fg−1 and enhanced the capacitance retention from 68.04% to 91.53% after 1000 cycles, respectively.19 MgO–SiO2 composites are becoming a growing trend for usage in supercapacitors due to their affordability, availability, and environmental friendliness. Combining SiO2 and MgO increases the structural stability and surface area, which facilitates effective ion transport. According to Ali et al., the fabricated NiO/MgO–SiO2 composites demonstrated exceptional long-term stability with a high specific capacitance of 344.12 F g−1 at 1.5 A g−1 and 88.9% capacitance retention after 40 000 cycles.20 GO has become a key element in improving the performance of supercapacitor electrodes because of its remarkable mechanical stability, large surface area, and electrical conductivity. When GO is incorporated into ternary nanocomposites with metal oxides like SiO2 and MgO, it promotes better electron transport and structural integrity, which results in enhanced electrochemical characteristics. GO's conductive network can provide effective electron transport.21,22 In this study, we successfully synthesized novel binary and ternary nanocomposites composed of MgO, SiO2, and GO, utilizing NaOH-activated carbons derived from peanut shells as a green carbon source. The activation process effectively enhanced the porosity and surface area of the carbon framework. The integration of GO in the MgO/SiO2 composite provided a highly conductive matrix that significantly improved the charge transfer kinetics. When comparing the MgO/SiO2/GO ternary electrode to its MgO/SiO2 binary electrode, electrochemical characterization revealed that the ternary MgO/SiO2/GO (NC's) had a remarkable specific capacitance. These works emphasise the importance of carbons based on biomass, conductive graphene materials and system integration in improving the performance of supercapacitors. In this regard, the current study is focused on the synthesis of MgO/SiO2 and MgO/SiO2/GO nanocomposites mixed with the peanut shell-derived activated carbon, which is a synergistic scaffold with improved electrochemical characteristics and high potential for use in the more advanced and flexible energy storage systems. These findings highlight the synergistic interaction of GO, where MgO and SiO2 contribute to the pseudocapacitive nature. The unique coral-like morphology observed in the binary NCs and origami-like morphology in the ternary NCs are reported here for the first time in this material system and synthesis route. It enhances mechanical stability during repeated cycling. This work introduces a novel, eco-friendly strategy for fabricating advanced nanostructured supercapacitor electrodes from agricultural waste-derived carbons and underscores eco-friendly synthesis for next-generation energy storage materials.
[P0002] Magnesium nitrate hexahydrate (Mg(NO3)2·6H2O, purity: 99%) and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) of SiO2 (98%) were purchased from Sisco Research Laboratory Pvt. Ltd. Graphene oxide (99%) was obtained from Adnano Technologies Pvt. Ltd. The chemicals were used without further purification. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was obtained from Nice Chemicals and nitric acid (HNO3) from Avra Synthesis Pvt. Ltd, which were used to adjust the pH. No further purification was carried out because all of the chemicals utilized were of analytical grade. Peanut shells were bought from a local market in Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India.
[P0003] The peanut shells (Arachis hypogaea) were removed, meticulously cleaned with water from the faucet to eliminate any dust or impurities from their surface, dried and then crushed using a mortar and pestle. The ground peanut shells were kept in a hot-air oven at 110 °C for 24 hours. The dried peanut shells were pulverized and then passed through a sieve to exclude bigger, shattered fragments. A carbon matrix was produced. Then, they were exposed to NaOH for chemical activation for 4 hours in a hot air oven at 100 °C in a 1 : 2 impregnation ratio of carbon and NaOH for desiccation. After two hours of activation at 700 °C, the mixture was allowed to settle at ambient temperature. The obtained activated carbon (AC) was continuously washed with distilled water until the pH of the filtrate fell between 8 and 11. Eventually, the AC was dried at 110 °C for 3 h to accomplish the activation process. The synthesis of nanocomposite was then carried out directly using this newly made activated carbon source.
[P0004] Subsequently, a solution was prepared by mixing 70 mL of distilled water, 30 mL of ethanol, 5 mL of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) and 0.4 M Mg(NO3)2·6H2O. The relative composition was determined as 25.52% of MgO, 24.93% of SiO2, 26.62% of carbon, and 24.81% of GO based on the concentrations of precursors used as follows: 5.12 g of MgO, 5 mL of SiO2 precursor, 5.34 g of carbon and 5.1 g of graphene oxide (GO). The pH was adjusted by adding 0.05 M HNO3 slowly and stirring using a magnetic stirrer for 1 hour. Then, the AC derived from peanut shells was added into it and heated for two hours at 60 °C in a hot-air oven. The precipitate was dried and the substance that had developed was scraped. Subsequently, the dried precipitate was transferred to a muffle furnace and calcined for two hours at 700 °C to obtain a MgO/SiO2, black binary nanocomposite. Simultaneously, a ternary nanocomposite of MgO/SiO2/GO was prepared by integrating GO into the MgO/SiO2 matrix. The precursors were mixed together in 100 mL distilled water and stirred for 1 hour using a magnetic stirrer. This procedure was repeated to prepare the MgO/SiO2/GO nanocomposite. The prepared samples were utilized for further characterization.

The pointed and diffracted peaks of MgO were found at 2θ = 37.2°, 42.24°, and 61.68° attributed to the unit cell planes (1 1 1), (2 0 0), and (2 2 0), respectively. Furthermore, the distinct peaks at 21.08°, 37.16°, and 52.14° were assigned to the (1 0 1), (2 0 0), and (3 0 1) crystallographic planes of SiO2, confirming its presence with the α-cristobalite pattern of the tetragonal structure (JCPDS card no: 39-1425).24 Moreover, around 11.04° and 41.3° which are indexed to the (0 0 2) and (1 0 0) planes, the primary GO signal matches with JCPDS card no: 75-2078, (ref. 25) confirming its incorporation. The βcos θ vs. sin θ curve was fitted using a linear least squares method. It displays a comparison of the lattice strain and computed crystallite size using two distinct approaches. The presence of anomalies or crystallographic defects inside a crystal structure also significantly affects the properties of the material. These flaws or anomalies are referred to as dislocation in position. For binary NCs of MgO/SiO2, the dislocation density is 0.764 × 10−15 m−2, and for ternary NCs of MgO/SiO2/GO, it is 1.331 × 10−15 m−2. The ternary nanocomposite exhibits a higher dislocation density than that of the binary NC, suggesting a decrease in crystallite size. Defects are more common at larger crystallite sizes, which lead to reduced strain in binary nanocomposites. The lower lattice strain in the binary nanocomposite is attributed to its lower dislocation density and reduced surface-to-volume ratio, as fewer defects and a larger crystallite size maintain a more stable crystal lattice. In contrast, the slightly higher strain in the ternary composite arises from the increased dislocation density and higher surface-to-volume ratio, which represents smaller crystallite sizes, where a larger fraction of surface atoms experience lattice distortion. Moreover, interfacial interactions that occur due to the introduction of GO impose localized deformations on the lattice, and thus, help to increase the observed strain increase.28

[P0024] Moreover, the O 1s XPS spectrum was used to determine the relative concentration of oxygen vacancies. The O 1s spectrum was deconvoluted into two peaks at the points of about 530.4 eV and 531.8 eV, which are attributed to the oxygen atom in the lattice and oxygen vacancy-related species, respectively. The presence of the energy component of higher binding energy proves the existence of oxygen vacancies. The relative size of the defect-related peak also demonstrates that oxygen vacancies contribute substantially to the overall capacitance. These defects promote the diffusion of ions and charge transfer, thereby improving the electrochemical performance of the MgO/SiO2/GO composite. The presence of the bridging oxygen peak significantly supports the formation of Mg–O–Si linkages and suggests a chemically related interaction between the two oxides. The formation of Mg–O–Si linkages promotes better charge transfer kinetics, reduces interfacial resistance, facilitates efficient electron transport, and improves ion diffusion, thereby leading to the improved capacitance and overall stability of the electrode. In supercapacitor applications, these interactions are anticipated to enhance the charge transfer efficiency and electrochemical performance.
[P0025] The electrochemical study was carried out using CV, EIS, GCD and stability tests. The working electrodes were fabricated by grinding the binary and ternary nanocomposites of MgO/SiO2 and MgO/SiO2/GO (85 weight percent) using a pestle and mortar. Furthermore, ethanol and 10% weight percent activated carbon were mixed with the active ingredient to create a functional electrode. After the mixture was well ground, 5 weight percent polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) binder was added, and the mixture was crushed again to obtain a slurry paste. The obtained slurry was affixed to a current collector composed of (1 × 1 cm2) graphite sheets. A platinum wire was used as the counter electrode and Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode. After preparation, the electrode was dried at 60 °C for 3 hours. A 6 M KOH electrolyte solution was used as the environment for the electrochemical tests as it has high ionic conductivity and low internal resistance, and has been extensively used in alkaline supercapacitor systems, and the electrolyte is important for the determination of ion movement, charge storage system, and capacitance. The use of aqueous KOH makes the diffusion of OH- ion fast, thus improving the electrochemical activity of the electrode materials. In a three-electrode setup used to evaluate the material's electrochemical properties, the active mass loading on the electrode was evaluated by comparing its mass before and after coating with the active material (0.7 mg).
[P0026] The CV was compared with two different samples of MgO/SiO2 binary NC and MgO/SiO2/GO ternary NC. The characteristic faradaic reactions were described by anodic and cathodic peaks, and the cyclic voltammetry curves of both MgO/SiO2 and MgO/SiO2/GO nanocomposites at different scan rates ranging from 5 mV s−1 to 100 mV s−1 are illustrated in Fig. 6(a and b), and the CV curves have a quasi-rectangular form and show a progressive rise in current response with the increasing scan rate. A surface-controlled charge storage mechanism is suggested by the lack of distinct redox peaks and the maintenance of the general shape across various scan rates. This phenomenon is typical of materials that are pseudocapacitive, which involves swift and reversible faradaic reactions on the electrode surface. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) study shows that the MgO/SiO2/GO materials have a substantially larger integral area than that of the MgO/SiO2 materials. Significantly, at different scan speeds, the specific capacitance of the MgO/SiO2/GO electrode is maximum compared to the MgO/SiO2 electrode. Notably, the specific capacitance of MgO/SiO2/GO is higher than that of the MgO/SiO2 nanocomposite. There is oscillation in the scan rate from 5 to 100 mV s−1. The specific capacitance of the MgO/SiO2 binary nanocomposite was found to be 365, 360, 356, 340, 338, 335, and 311 F g−1 at scan rates of 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mV s−1. The specific capacitance values of 473, 470, 464, 460, 454, 414, and 404 F g−1 were high for the MgO/SiO2/GO nanocomposites. The control mechanism of the electrode can be ascertained from the b value that the slope produces. A b-value close to 0.5 indicates a battery (diffusion-controlled), whereas a value close to 1.0 suggests a process that is mostly pseudocapacitive. The slope derived from the log(i) vs. log(v) plot produces the b value. The anode peaks for MgO/SiO2 showed a b-value of 0.822, and for MgO/SiO2/GO, a slightly higher b-value of 1 is observed, indicating even better capacitive behaviour, as shown in Fig. 6(c and d). This implies that the distinct capacitances of the electrodes are based on both pseudo capacitive and diffusion-controlled properties. Fig. 6(e) shows the relation between the particular capacitance values and the scan rates. The electrode with the highest specific capacitance values at each scan rate has been shown to be MgO/SiO2/GO nanocomposites. This implies that the electrodes with distinct capacitances will be established on both pseudo-capacitive and diffusion-controlled characteristics. To understand the energy storage mechanism, the CV data were next analysed using Dunn's method (Fig. 6(f)). At high scan rates, the capacitive storage exhibits extraordinary behaviour due to the superior reversibility and rate generated by quick ion intercalation and deintercalation.

A reduction in the inner resistance by the incorporation of GO results in a conductive network that promotes the rapid transport of electrons, thereby improving the charge transfer kinetics at the electrode–electrolyte interface. The EIS analysis shows that the ternary composite has a smaller semicircle in the high-frequency region, which represents the lesser charge transfer resistance than that of the binary MgO/SiO2 electrode. The existence of oxygen-containing functional groups on GO also manifests this by providing stronger interfacial interaction with the electrolyte, facilitating efficient ion diffusion, as shown by the steeper Warburg region in the low-frequency domain. It is interesting to note that the electrode exhibits a significant energy density of approximately 57 Wh kg−1 at such a high power density of approximately 7500 W kg−1, demonstrating its great rate capability. Such an exceptional energy-power operation indicates the synergetic effect of the composite and validates its high potential for future high-level supercapacitor applications.

[P0037] Binary and ternary nanocomposites of MgO/SiO2 and MgO/SiO2/GO were successfully synthesized via a green-mediated approach using peanut shells (Arachis hypogaea L.). Their structural, morphological, chemical and elemental analyses were performed using XRD, FTIR spectroscopy, SEM, and XPS techniques. The crystallite size was found from the XRD study by two different methods: Debye–Scherrer and Williamson–Hall plot method. According to the Debye–Scherrer method, the crystallite size of the binary nanocomposite is around 36.1 nm and 27.4 nm for the ternary nanocomposite. Simultaneously, using the W–H plot, the crystallite size was found to be 39.3 nm and 28 nm for the binary MgO/SiO2 and ternary MgO/SiO2/GO nanocomposites, respectively. The functional groups and the bands were detected by FTIR spectroscopy studies. The SEM study revealed a coral-like structure for the binary electrode and an origami-like folding structure with size distribution for the ternary electrode. EDX confirms the elemental compositions of Mg, O, Si, and C, along with K, Ca, and Na, indicating that peanut shell-derived activated carbon elements are present. XPS substantiates the electronic states and chemical composition of both the binary and ternary nanocomposites. The electrochemical studies of the CV curves show that, at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1, the ternary electrode has a higher specific capacitance of 473 F g−1 than the binary electrode of 365 F g−1. Similarly, GCD measurements at a current density of 1 A g−1 show that the ternary nanocomposite of the MgO/SiO2/GO electrode exhibits synergistic effects with a higher specific capacitance of 482 F g−1 than that of the binary MgO/SiO2 nanocomposite of 372 F g−1. Furthermore, the electrode exhibits remarkable stability for the ternary nanocomposite, and it maintains a capacitance retention and coulombic efficiency of approximately 94.14% and 95% after 5000 cycles for MgO/SiO2/GO, and for the binary nanocomposite of MgO/SiO2, the capacitance retention and coulombic efficiency are around 92% and 94.65%. The MgO/SiO2/GO composite exhibits a capacitance of 482 F g−1, with an energy density of approximately 67 Wh kg−1 at 2415 W kg−1 and around 57 Wh kg−1 at 7500 W kg−1, underscoring its superior rate capability and desirability for high-performance supercapacitors. These findings demonstrate that the MgO/SiO2/GO ternary electrode with peanut shell biomass-derived carbon has the potential to serve as a high-performance electrode material for supercapacitors, which holds promise for advanced energy storage applications.
