ABSTRACT
Onions, scientifically known as Allium cepa L., are commonly used as a vegetable and offer a variety of health 
benefits. However, this study explored a novel part of the onion plant, such as the flower seed cover often 
regarded as waste, by converting it into biochar (BC) via pyrolysis. Through careful optimization and activation 
with potassium hydroxide, the resulting novel biochar exhibited a unique graphitic nanosheet-like structure with 
an impressive surface area of 2538.31 m
2
/g. To serve as a control, rice straw, one of the most commonly used 
biomasses, was also activated using the same protocol. Various techniques, including XPS, HRTEM, SAED, FTIR, 
BET, XRD, and Raman, were used to comprehensively understand the surface and structural properties of the 
biochar. When the novel biochar was tested as an energy storage material, it exhibited a specific capacitance of 
200.37 F/g at 1 A/g and 143.82 F/g at 5 A/g in a 1 M H
2
SO
4 
electrolyte with a three-electrode system. In a two- 
electrode system, it showed 158 F/g at 1 A/g and 102 F/g at 5 A/g, indicating its effectiveness at high rates. The 
long-term cyclic stability analysis demonstrated 100 % capacity retention over 1000 cycles in the three-electrode 
system, and 90.77 % and 74.40 % retention after 5000 cycles and 10,000 cycles, respectively, in the two- 
electrode system. The fabricated symmetric supercapacitor yielded a power density of 500 W/kg and an en -
ergy density of 21.94 Wh/kg at a current density of 1 A/g. These findings provide valuable insights into the 
valorization of biomass in addressing concerns regarding the current energy storage dilemma.
1. Introduction
It is reported that the natural world produces about 50 billion tons of 
land-based biomass each year. Out of this, about 16.7 billion tons are 
used for human purposes, such as feed for livestock, food, grazing, and 
specialized crops for other purposes. What is left off after the con -
sumption of these 16.7 billion tons is about 8.1 billion tons, which can be 
sustainably harvested and recycled into value-added products [ 1 ]. 
However, due to logistical and operational challenges, many of these 
biomasses are left untouched, eventually becoming a menace to the 
environment [ 2 ]. Owing to the high cost of commercially activated 
carbon and the vast tonnages of agricultural biomasses that pile up in the 
environment, scientists have resorted to the valorization of this agri -
cultural biomass waste into biochar to be more economical and sus -
tainable due to the abundance of biomass in the environment [ 3 ].
Biochar is a beneficial substance notably rich in carbon and lignin, 
produced under oxygen-deprived conditions via the thermochemical 
conversion of biomass [ 4 ]. Many plant-based agricultural residues are 
regarded as waste, and human approaches to dealing with this waste 
tend to pollute the environment. A practical and environmentally sus -
tainable way of managing these tons of agricultural waste is by pyro -
lyzing the waste in an anoxic environment to produce biochar [ 5 ]. The 
properties of biochar, such as its large surface area, surface functional 
groups, high porosity, and stability, make it a viable alternative for

energy applications [ 6 ]. These essential properties are predominantly 
established by the type of feedstock used, the treatment processes 
employed, and the conditions under which pyrolysis occurs.
Furthermore, the drastic depletion of non-renewable fossil fuels for 
energy has become a growing concern, and there is a massive advocacy 
to unearth the potential of renewable resources as a replacement for this 
fossil fuel [ 7 , 8 ]. However, this would need time to come to full light. In 
the meantime, supercapacitors have been introduced as the future of 
energy storage devices [ 9 ]. Many researchers have employed varieties of 
agricultural biomasses such as, corn straw [ 10 ], pinecone [ 11 ], rice 
straw [ 12 – 15 ] rice husk [ 16 ], and peanut shell [ 17 ] into forming biochar 
for supercapacitor applications [ 18 ]. Amongst these, rice straw is one of 
the most commonly used biomasses; hence, it was employed as a control 
sample in this study while detailed attention was given to the novel 
biomass. To strike a balance between cost and performance, agricultural 
biomass has been converted to activated carbon to fabricate electrodes, 
commonly referred to as electric double-layer capacitor (EDLC), for 
supercapacitor applications [ 19 ]. However, these biomass-derived 
electrodes suffer from low energy densities and low-rate capabilities 
but are powerful at possessing high power densities and good cycling 
stability [ 20 ] Additionally, the literature reveals significant variations in 
converting these biomasses into carbonaceous materials for super -
capacitor applications, resulting in prolonged processing times, 
increased energy consumption, relatively low capacitances, and the 
utilization of diverse chemicals for each technique.
For instance, a study conducted by Musyoka et al. [ 21 ] explored the 
transformation of onion peel first into hydrochar, followed by pre ‑ car -
bonization and finally activation using KOH in an argon environment. 
This process yielded porous carbon material that showed promise for 
applications in supercapacitors. Similarly, Dai et al. [ 22 ] investigated 
the synthesis of activated carbon from argy wormwood using two 
different chemicals, H
3
PO
4 
and KOH for supercapacitor applications. In 
a different approach also, García-Mateos et al. [ 23 ] developed activated 
carbon electrodes from lignin fibers through H
3
PO
4 
activation using an 
electrospinning technique. Subsequently, the electrodes underwent 
thermal stabilization at 200 
◦
C, carbonization at 900 
◦
C, and O
2 
treat -
ment under a moderately oxygen-containing N
2 
atmosphere. However, 
it should be noted that these sequential steps in the production process 
of activated carbon led to an increase in its energy consumption and 
lengthened the overall process.
To bridge the deficiencies in EDLC's several researchers have 
developed supercapacitors as a composite of biomass and metal oxides 
known as pseudocapacitors [ 24 – 26 ]. These developed pseudocapacitors 
also tend to increase production costs, and the incorporation of metal 
oxides is also non-renewable sources, thus having a mix of forward and 
backward steps towards attaining sustainable energy. Additionally, 
doping of biochar with heteroatoms such as nitrogen, boron, sulfur, and 
phosphorus also tend to improve the EDLC's energy density while 
maintaining its power densities. However, to some extent, these doping 
strategies distort the characteristic morphology of the developed bio -
char and do not largely improve capacitances in all cases. Hence, con -
stant research about the optimization of EDLC is imperative. To achieve 
optimal energy storage, it is important to have electrode materials that 
possess a high surface area, a good combination of micro and mesopores, 
and a characteristic morphology. This enables the electrode to have a 
large number of active sites, allows for fast transfer of ions from the 
electrolyte to the porous electrode, and provides good cycling proper -
ties, ultimately resulting in a high specific capacitance.
Recently, there has been a rise in the demand for fresh onions, both 
for consumption and medicinal purposes. As a result, onions have 
become the second most important horticultural crop, with around 47 
million tons cultivated annually [ 27 ]. However, onion cultivation also 
produces approximately 500,000 tons of waste each year in Europe [ 21 ]. 
Because onion peels have a strong smell and are unsuitable for landfill 
disposal, finding ways to use them is essential. Many researchers have 
focused on the peel of onions as agricultural waste, but the woody 
biomass that houses the flower seeds has been neglected. This results in 
waste from the flower seed covers, which could be transformed into a 
high-value-added product using optimal processing techniques. More -
over, despite the abundance of onion peels, they also have quite a good 
yield when pyrolyzed, and this could be an added reason for their wide 
usage. For example, Musyoka et al. [ 21 ] made porous carbon from onion 
peel by converting it to hydrochar, pre ‑ carbonizing it, and further 
activating it with KOH in an argon atmosphere for supercapacitor 
application. Krishnappa et al. [ 28 ] and Prajapati et al. [ 29 ] also 
employed onion peel biomass by converting it to biochar to treat a va -
riety of textile dyes, including MB, MO CR, and Cr (VI), of which it was a 
success. It is, therefore, essential to mention that there is currently no 
accurate data on the quantity of biomass generated by onion flower seed 
covers. However, it is known that waste is produced with every onion 
plant harvested. In future studies, research will be conducted to explore 
the potential of this unique biomass in greater detail, and accurate sta -
tistical data will be made available. Until then, this work lays the 
groundwork for fully exploring this unique agricultural biomass.
This study, therefore, explored the chemical activation of novel 
onion flower seed cover and rice straw biomasses via optimal temper -
ature, ramping rate, and mix ratio. The study investigated the suitability 
of the developed biochars as energy storage devices in a three-electrode 
and two-electrode setups. Various analytical techniques, including cy -
clic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD), electro -
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), capacitance retention, and 
response time, were thoroughly investigated. The study placed greater 
emphasis on the novel biochar's surface and structural properties, with 
rice straw serving as the control due to its common use as biomass. 
Consequently, this study makes noteworthy contributions to the field of 
converting innovative agricultural biomasses into high-performance 
supercapacitors for sustainable electrochemical energy storage.

2.2. Processing and development of biochar
After collecting the cover of onion flower seeds (OFSC) and rice straw 
(RS) biomasses, the two were cleaned using de-ionized water to elimi -
nate any dirt or other impurities. The cleaned samples were then dried 
for 24 h at 100 
◦
C in a Binder dry oven. Once dried, the samples were 
ground into fine particles using an RRH-500AK grinder and sieved 
through an aperture of 106 μ m. Next, 7 g of the samples were pre ‑ car -
bonized in a muffle furnace under continuous nitrogen gas at 10 
◦
C/min

◦

impregnated with KOH in a 5:1 ratio, sonicated, and refluxed at 65

C

◦

flow with the rate of heating set at 10 
◦
C/min. After activation, the 
samples were purified effectively using a rigorous 2-hour wash with 100 
ml of 2 M HCl, followed by rinsing with de-ionized water until the pH 
level reached neutrality. The biochars were then dried for 24 h at 100 
◦

Having identified that the structural and morphological properties of 
the pristine samples were inferior to the activated samples, the 
elemental compositions of the activated samples were analyzed using an 
elemental analyzer, and the results are documented in Table 2 . The 
activation process caused the biochars to react with KOH, which led to 
the consumption of organic elements and the creation of a porous 
structure, ultimately increasing the specific surface area [ 21 ]. The hy -
drophobic nature of Act_OFSCBC biochar is evident from its lower O/C 
ratio (0.243), while Act_RSBC (0.835) is more hydrophilic. The polarity 
index (O + N)/C of Act_OFSCBC shows a higher percentage of non-polar 
groups than Act_RSBC, indicating that Act_RSBC contains more 
oxygenated functional groups. The Act_OFSCBC possesses a significantly 
higher aromaticity (H/C) in comparison to the control sample, giving it a 
higher tendency for efficient ion transport. It is important to note that 
different biomasses synthesized under common processing conditions 
can yield different characteristic features depending on the type of 
biomass.
The novel Act_OFSCBC was analyzed with a high-resolution trans -
mission electron microscope (HRTEM) to determine its structural and 
morphological characteristics. The chemical activation process in this 
study resulted in the graphitization of the OFSCBC, which functions as a 
framework of the activated porous carbon material. Fig. 3 (a) shows the 
evidence of crumpled layers and thin sheets of Act_OFSCBC, which was 
not so in the case of the TEM characterization of the Act_RSBC (Fig. S1). 
The HRTEM further reveals features of agglomerated crumpled nano -
sheets with voids distributed throughout the material, as seen in Fig. 3
(b). The formation of nanosheets in the activated novel material in -
creases its specific surface area, which suggests that the process of high- 
temperature pyrolysis with KOH modification is effective, as confirmed 
by Raman characterization in Fig. 2 (c). The nanosheets' large surface 
area structure and pore distribution over the sheet surface accelerate ion 
transport in multiple directions by providing abundant transport chan -
nels. Additionally, the varied micro and mesopore distribution act as 
reservoir for ion storage and favour ionic diffusion thereby enhancing 
electrochemical performance. This shortens transmission pathways and 
reduces charge-diffusion resistance, ultimately boosting electrochemical 
performance [ 19 , 36 , 37 ]. The lamellar structure of the novel biochar is 
depicted in a high-resolution micrograph in Fig. 3 (c). It is also possible 
that the transparent sheets with thinner wall structures in Fig. 3 (a) at 
higher activation temperatures resulted from the exfoliation of stacked 
onion-flower-seed-cover biochar after the intercalation of potassium 
(K). The graphitic lamellae in Fig. 3 (e) have a spacing of 0.35 nm, which 
is very near to the value reported in the literature (0.34), [ 38 ] indicating 
some level of crystallinity, which was affirmed by the selective area 
electron diffraction as seen in Fig. 3 (d). This can help with the accu -
mulation of charge in the electric double-layer, resulting in increased

and 2.83 Ω , respectively.

the electrode (known as charge transfer resistance or Rct), was identified 
by the semi-circles that appeared at high frequencies following the ESR. 
It has been noted that activated samples have smaller semi-circle di -

electrolyte to the electrode, resulting in effective and efficient capaci -

Statement of novelty
This study utilized pyrolysis to develop biochar from a novel onion 
flower seed cover biomass, typically considered waste. The resulting 
biochar boasted a distinctive nano-sheet-like structure, enriched func -
tional groups, and graphitic carbon, all while exhibiting an impressive 
surface area of 2538.31 m
2
/g and excellent pore properties. The novel 
biochar exhibited a specific capacitance of 200.37 F/g at 1 A/g and 
143.82 F/g at 5 A/g using a 1 M H
2
SO
4 
electrolyte. It demonstrated 
excellent capacity retention of 100 % over 1000 cycles and exhibited a 
rapid response time of 4 s in a three-electrode system. The novel sample 
outperformed the control sample (rice straw) in all the electrochemical 
tests performed. Therefore, this study provides significant insights into 
the engineering of innovative agricultural biomasses as high- 
performance supercapacitors for efficient energy storage. It also dem -
onstrates the potential of pyrolysis for managing agricultural waste and 
reducing carbon dioxide levels.
