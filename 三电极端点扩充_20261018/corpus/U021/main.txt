Tailoring Porosity and Surface Functionality in Activated 
Carbon via Synergistic Ultrasonic and Microwave Activation 
for Energy Storage Applications

ABSTRACT 
Hazelnut shell–derived activated carbons were synthesized via phosphoric acid chemical activation and subsequently modified 
using microwave (MW) and ultrasonic (US) treatments to tailor their physicochemical and electrochemical properties for 
supercapacitor applications. Structural and spectroscopic analyses confirmed the formation of turbostratic carbon frameworks 
with partial graphitic ordering and abundant surface functional groups. Elemental analysis revealed that microwave treatment 
induced pronounced surface oxidation, whereas ultrasonic treatment preserved high carbon content while introducing moderate 
oxygen functionalities. Thermal analysis demonstrated superior thermal stability for the ultrasound-assisted activated carbon. 
Electrochemical performance was evaluated in a three-electrode system using 3 M KOH electrolyte. Cyclic voltammetry revealed 
dominant electrical double-layer capacitive behavior with minor pseudocapacitive contributions, particularly for the ultrasound- 
treated sample. Among all electrodes, ACA-US exhibited the largest CV area, highest current response, and superior specific 
capacitance, attributed to its exceptionally high BET surface area (2138 m2 g− 1 ) and well-developed microporosity. Galvanostatic 
charge–discharge measurements confirmed excellent reversibility and rate capability, with ACA-US delivering the longest 
discharge times and highest capacitance over a wide current density range. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy revealed 
reduced charge transfer resistance and improved ion diffusion kinetics for ACA-US. Furthermore, the optimized electrode 
retained ∼ 85% of its initial capacitance after 2000 cycles, indicating excellent long-term stability. These results demonstrate that 
ultrasonic post-treatment is an effective strategy for producing high-performance biomass-derived activated carbons for advanced 
supercapacitor applications. 
1 Introduction 
The rising global energy demand, diminishing fossil fuel reserves, 
and growing environmental concerns due to climate change 
have collectively driven a rapid shift toward renewable energy 
sources such as solar and wind. This transition not only supports 
efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions but also promotes the 
development of cleaner transportation technologies, including 
electric and hybrid vehicles. Nevertheless, the intermittent and 
variable nature of renewable energy generation highlights the 
urgent need for efficient and reliable energy storage systems. 
In this context, batteries and electrochemical capacitors have 
emerged as two of the most commonly utilized technologies for 
storing electrical energy. While batteries offer high energy densi- 
ties, electrochemical capacitors—particularly  supercapacitors—
are capable of delivering significantly higher power densities

(up to 10 kW kg− 1 ) and exhibit rapid charge–discharge capability, 
albeit with lower energy densities ( ∼ 5 Wh kg− 1 ) [ 1, 2 ]. 
Supercapacitors have gained significant attention as advanced 
energy storage systems, offering a balance between traditional 
capacitors and batteries. Their ability to deliver high power 
density, sustain rapid charge/discharge cycles, provide extended 
operational lifespans, and maintain cost-effectiveness makes 
them highly suitable for various demanding applications. These 
attribute render them suitable for various sectors, including 
portable electronics, hybrid electric vehicles, and grid stabi- 
lization. Despite these advantages, further advancements in 
materials science and a deeper understanding of charge storage 
mechanisms—particularly  at the electrochemical interface—are  
essential to meet the growing demands of next-generation energy 
systems [ 1, 2 ]. 
A critical factor influencing the performance of supercapacitors 
is the selection of electrode material. Extensive research has 
focused on developing novel electrode materials with optimized 
structure and surface chemistry. Common classes of materials 
include carbon-based materials (e.g., activated carbon, carbon 
nanotubes), transition metal oxides (e.g., NiO, MnO2 , RuO2 ), and 
conducting polymers [ 3 ]. Among them, carbon-based materials 
have garnered considerable interest due to their high electrical 
conductivity, chemical stability, tunable porosity, and low cost. 
In particular, activated carbon ( AC ), known for its high surface 
area and well-developed pore structure, has been extensively 
investigated. The performance of AC is strongly influenced by the 
type of precursor used and the activation method applied [ 4, 5 ]. 
Most commercial activated carbons are derived from fossil-based 
precursors, which are costly and environmentally unsustain- 
able. This limitation has spurred research into biomass-derived 
carbons, offering advantages such as renewability, low cost, 
and reduced environmental impact [ 2 ]. Various agricultural and 
agro-industrial wastes, including rice husks, sugarcane bagasse, 
apricot shells, palm kernel shells, and coffee residues, have 
been successfully converted into porous carbon materials for use 
in electric double-layer capacitors ( EDLCs ) [ 6 ]. These biomass 
wastes are often discarded or underutilized, yet they possess 
inherent porosity and carbon-rich content, making them suitable 
candidates for high-performance electrode materials. 
The physicochemical properties of biomass-derived carbon can 
be significantly enhanced through physical and chemical modifi- 
cation methods [ 7, 8 ]. Among chemical agents, acids (e.g., H3 PO4 , 
HNO3 ), bases (e.g., KOH, NaOH), and oxidants (e.g., H2 O2 , 
KMnO4 ) have been widely employed to improve surface area 
and pore development [ 9, 10 ]. H3 PO4 and KOH are among the 
most commonly studied activating agents due to their efficiency 
in tailoring surface structure and functionality [ 9, 11 , 12 ]. In 
particular, phosphoric acid (H3 PO4 ) is one of the most effective 
chemical activating agents for directing the structural evolution 
and pore morphology of biomass-derived activated carbon [ 13 ]. 
Owing to its strong affinity for oxygen-containing functional 
groups, H3 PO4 readily diffuses into lignocellulosic matrices and 
catalyzes a sequence of temperature-dependent transformations 
that control pore formation and carbon framework stability [ 14, 
15 ]. During the initial heating stage ( ≤ 200◦C), the acid promotes 
hydrolytic cleavage of glycosidic and ether bonds within cellulose 
and hemicellulose, while simultaneously inducing dehydration 
reactions that limit tar formation. At this stage, H3 PO4 under- 
goes condensation to generate polyphosphate species, which 
chemically bridge adjacent biopolymer chains and reinforce the 
precursor structure against thermal collapse [ 16, 17 ]. 
As pyrolysis proceeds to intermediate temperatures (200–400◦C), 
extensive depolymerization and dehydration of the organic 
matrix occur, leading to progressive aromatization of the carbon 
backbone. The presence of phosphate and polyphosphate link- 
ages moderates the rate of carbon consumption and preserves the 
integrity of the developing pore network, while the continuous 
evolution of volatile species initiates the formation of primary 
micropores [ 18–20 ]. This controlled activation pathway results 
in higher carbon yield and more uniform pore distribution 
compared with nonphosphoric activators [ 21 ]. 
At elevated temperatures (400◦C–700◦C), thermally unstable 
phosphate species decompose into phosphorus oxides, such 
as pyrophosphates and P2 O5 , which act as strong dehydrating 
and mild oxidizing agents within the carbon matrix [ 22, 23 ]. 
Simultaneously, the release of gaseous products (H2 O, CO, and 
CO2 ) expands previously formed micropores and generates inter- 
connected mesoporous channels, producing a hierarchical pore 
structure favorable for ion transport [ 23 ]. 
Further heating to very high temperatures ( ≥ 700◦C–900◦C) 
may intensify pore enlargement through carbon–oxide redox 
reactions; however, excessive thermal exposure can diminish 
surface functional groups and partially degrade pore walls, which 
may adversely affect electrochemical performance [ 24 ]. Overall, 
H3 PO4 activation uniquely integrates dehydration-driven cross- 
linking, controlled volatilization, and phosphate-mediated pore 
expansion, enabling precise regulation of pore size distribu- 
tion and surface chemistry. In addition, residual phosphorus- 
containing functionalities enhance electrolyte wettability and 
introduce faradaic contributions, rendering phosphoric-acid- 
activated carbons highly suitable for high-performance superca- 
pacitor electrodes [ 25 ]. 
Furthermore, combining chemical activation with alternative 
energy sources such as microwave irradiation and ultrasonic 
treatment has shown promise in improving activation efficiency 
and material performance. Microwave-assisted activation enables 
rapid and uniform heating, which can significantly shorten 
processing time and improve energy efficiency compared to 
conventional thermal methods. On the other hand, ultrasonic 
treatment generates localized cavitation and turbulence in the 
reaction medium, enhancing the penetration of activating agents 
and promoting the formation of a more accessible porous struc- 
ture [ 26 ]. Notably, the synergistic use of acid treatment, ultrasonic 
pretreatment, and microwave irradiation has been reported to 
significantly modify the surface chemistry and structural features 
of biochar [ 10 ]. 
Although phosphoric acid-activated biomass-derived carbons as 
well as microwave- and ultrasound-assisted activation techniques 
have been individually investigated, their combined and sys- 
tematic comparison within a unified activation strategy remains 
largely unexplored, particularly for hazelnut shell–based carbon 
materials. Addressing this gap, the present study proposes a

Overall, this integrated activation strategy provides new mech- 
anistic insights into synergistic chemical–physical activation 
effects and demonstrates a cost-effective and scalable route 
for converting an underutilized agricultural waste into high- 
performance carbon electrodes for energy storage applications. 
2 Results and Discussions 
2.1 Elemental Composition 
Elemental analysis results of the activated carbon samples are 
shown in Table 1 . The results revealed notable variations in car- 
bon, oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, and sulfur content as a function 
of the applied modification method. The pristine activated carbon 
( ACA ) exhibited a high carbon content (79.60 wt.%) and relatively 
low oxygen content (17.8 wt.%), indicative of a predominantly 
graphitic structure with limited oxygenated surface functionali- 
ties. This composition reflects a low degree of surface oxidation 
and a hydrophobic character. 
Microwave-assisted activated carbon( ACA-MW ) resulted in a pro- 
nounced decrease in carbon content (66.40 wt.%) alongside a sig- 
nificant increase in oxygen content (30.14 wt.%). These changes 
suggest the occurrence of oxidative processes facilitated by 
microwave irradiation, likely involving the formation of oxygen- 
containing functional groups such as hydroxyls, carbonyls, and 
carboxylic acids [ 27 ]. The elevated hydrogen content in ACA- 
MW (2.80 wt.%) further supports the presence of increased 
hydroxylation and/or chemisorbed water molecules [ 28 ]. 
In contrast, ultrasound-assisted activated carbon(ACA-US) main- 
tained a relatively high carbon content (77.50 wt.%) while 
inducing a moderate increase in oxygen content (20.33 wt.%) com- 
pared to ACA . This suggests that ultrasonic treatment promotes 
limited surface oxidation, likely via localized cavitation-induced 
reactions that introduce mild surface defects and oxygenated 
sites without severely disrupting the carbon matrix. The slightly 
reduced hydrogen (2.17 wt.%) and nitrogen (0.36 wt.%) contents in 
ACA-US imply minimal structural degradation or incorporation 
of heteroatoms. 
Sulphur was detectable only in ACA (0.29 wt.%) and ACA-MW 
(0.27 wt.%), while it was below detection limits in ACA-US, 
which may be due to sulphur volatilization or desorption during 
ultrasonic processing. 
2.2 XRD Results of Activated Carbon Samples 
Figure 1a illustrates the XRD patterns of the activated carbon 
samples. The diffraction patterns of all activated samples exhibit 
two broad reflections centered at approximately 2 θ≈ 23-24◦and 
43◦, equivalent to the (002) and (101) planes, respectively. All 
samples show a broad diffraction peak in the 2 θrange of around 
23-24◦irrespective of the activation conditions, suggesting the 
graphitization and carbonization of the biomass [ 29 ]. 
The diffraction feature observed near 43◦, attributed to the (101) 
plane, is associated with in-plane ordering of sp2 carbon atoms 
[ 30 ]. Changes in the intensity and sharpness of this peak reflect 
modifications in electrical conductivity and structural coherence 
of the carbon matrix [ 30 ]. A moderate enhancement of this peak 
is often linked to improved electron transport pathways, which is 
beneficial for electrochemical energy storage applications such as 
supercapacitors.

2.4 Spectroscopic Characterization 
2.4.1 FTIR Spectroscopy 
The surface chemical composition of the activated carbon sam- 
ples was evaluated using FTIR spectroscopy (Figure 1d ). The 
FTIR spectra confirm the presence of multiple oxygen-containing 
surface functionalities, indicating that carbonization was not 
fully completed and that heteroatoms were retained on the carbon 
framework. A broad absorption band centered at approximately 
3402 cm− 1 is assigned to O ─H stretching vibrations, while 
the peak at 1564 cm− 1 corresponds to aromatic C ═ C skeletal 
vibrations. The band observed at 1165 cm− 1 is associated with C ─O 
and C ─C stretching modes originating from cellulose-derived 
or ether-like structures [ 33, 34 ]. Consistent with the TG–DTG 
results, the relative intensity of hydroxyl-related bands is higher 
for ACA-MW and ACA-US compared to ACA, reflecting their 
increased moisture affinity and surface polarity. 
Additional spectral features include a low-frequency band at 495 
cm− 1 , which is characteristic of phosphate-related vibrations and 
confirms the incorporation of phosphorus species through H3 PO4 
chemical activation [ 35 ]. The presence of C ─H deformation 
bands at 889 cm− 1 suggests partial preservation of aliphatic 
moieties [ 36 ], whereas the absorption at 2358 cm− 1 is attributed 
to CO2 -related vibrations, likely arising from surface carbonate 
species formed via oxidation or exposure to ambient air during 
post-treatment [ 37 ]. 
The coexistence of these oxygen- and phosphorus-containing 
groups correlates well with the thermal degradation behavior 
observed in TG–DTG profiles. The initial mass loss below 110◦C 
is consistent with the removal of physically adsorbed moisture 
associated with hydroxyl-rich surfaces, while the gradual weight 
loss at elevated temperatures is attributed to the decomposition 
of labile functional groups and progressive structural rearrange- 
ment of the carbon matrix. In particular, ACA-US exhibits a lower 
overall mass loss, suggesting enhanced thermal stability despite 
its relatively high surface oxygen content. 
2.4.2 Raman Spectroscopy 
Raman spectroscopy was employed to assess the structural order- 
ing and degree of graphitization of the activated carbon samples 
(Figure 2 ). All spectra display two characteristic bands: the D 
band located near 1350 cm− 1 , which arises from lattice defects and 
disordered carbon structures, and the G band centered around 
1580 cm− 1 , corresponding to the in-plane vibration of sp2 -bonded 
carbon atoms within graphitic domains [ 38–40 ]. Variations in 
both band positions and intensity ratios reflect changes in the 
carbon microstructure induced by activation and post-treatment 
processes. 
The intensity ratio of the D to G bands ( ID /IG ) is commonly 
used as an indicator of defect density and structural disorder. 
The pristine activated carbon (ACA) exhibits an ID /IG value of 
approximately 0.90, indicative of a predominantly amorphous 
carbon framework. Following microwave (MW) and ultrasonica- 
tion (US) treatments, this ratio slightly increases to 0.92 for both 
ACA-MW and ACA-US, suggesting the introduction of additional 
interplanar defects and edge sites within the carbon matrix as a 
result of combined chemical and physical activation. 
Despite the increase in defect density, the evolution of the 
Raman features implies a partial reorganization of amorphous 
carbon into more ordered graphitic domains. This structural 
transformation is consistent with the XRD results, which reveal 
the development of turbostratic or short-range graphitic ordering 
in ACA-MW and ACA-US samples. The coexistence of graphitic 
regions and defect-rich sites leads to the formation of a well- 
developed porous carbon architecture. 
From an electrochemical perspective, the relatively high defect- 
to-graphitization ratio is advantageous, as defects and edge 
sites serve as active centers for electrolyte ion adsorption and 
facilitate enhanced electrode–electrolyte interactions. Moreover, 
the presence of sp2 -hybridized conductive domains improves 
charge transport pathways, thereby accelerating charge transfer 
kinetics and supporting surface redox reactions. Consequently, 
the Raman analysis confirms that the combined activation strate- 
gies effectively tailor the carbon structure to achieve a balance 
between electrical conductivity and electrochemically active 
sites. 
2.5 Morphological Characteristics of the 
Activated Carbon Samples 
2.5.1 SEM 
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was utilized to examine the 
surface structures of the activated carbon samples, with selected 
images displayed in Figure 3 to illustrate their morphological 
features. The SEM image of the ACA sample shows a highly 
porous and fragmented surface, which is typical of phosphoric 
acid activation. Phosphoric acid acts as a dehydrating agent, 
promoting bond cleavage and facilitating the formation of cross- 
linked structures during pyrolysis. As a result, a developed 
network of mesopores and macropores is evident. The acid 
also assists in the retention of oxygenated functional groups, 
contributing to the rough, layered surface texture often observed 
in such materials. Similar surface features have been reported 
in the literature for H3 PO4 -activated lignocellulosic carbons 
[ 28, 41 ]. 
The ACA-MW sample, which underwent microwave-assisted 
treatment, reveals a significantly altered morphology with 
enhanced pore opening and more extensive surface fissures. 
Microwave heating provides rapid and volumetric energy input, 
causing local hot spots and accelerating the release of volatile 
matter. This effect leads to the expansion and deepening of 
pre-existing pores, producing a more open structure. Several 
studies have confirmed that microwave-assisted phosphoric acid 
activation improves both porosity and surface area compared to 
conventional heating [ 42 ]. The SEM image of the ACA-US sample 
demonstrates a cleaner and more ordered surface morphology, 
with better-defined pore boundaries and fewer surface residues. 
Ultrasonic treatment introduces high-frequency acoustic cavita- 
tion, which helps remove trapped by products and opens partially 
blocked pores [ 43 ]. This post-treatment also aids in exfoliating 
layered structures, resulting in improved pore accessibility and

2.5.2 N2 Adsorption–Desorption Isotherms and Pore 
Properties of Activated Carbon Samples 
Figure 4 illustrates the nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms 
together with the corresponding pore size distributions of the 
activated carbon materials. All samples exhibit isotherms that can 
be classified as type IV, accompanied by pronounced hysteresis 
loops, indicating the predominance of mesoporous structures. 
The presence of hysteresis loops in the relative pressure (P/P0 ) 
range of 0.4–0.95 corresponds to the H3-type hysteresis according 
to the IUPAC classification [ 46, 47 ]. This behavior is typically 
associated with slit-shaped pores, which are commonly formed 
by aggregates of plate-like particles, microcracks, layered pore 
networks, and wedge-shaped voids. Such textural characteristics 
are consistent with the average pore diameters listed in Table 1 , 
ranging from 26.5 to 28.3 Å, confirming the dominance of 
mesopores. 
Among the investigated samples, ACA-US exhibits the highest 
nitrogen uptake over the entire relative pressure range, resulting 
in the largest BET surface area (2138 m2 g− 1 ) and a compara- 
tively high micropore volume (0.31 cm3 g− 1 ). This enhancement 
can be attributed to the ultrasonic treatment, which promotes 
the opening of previously blocked pores and facilitates the 
development of accessible micro- and mesoporous networks. 
Similar improvements in porosity and surface area induced by 
ultrasound-assisted modification have been reported for activated 
carbon materials in earlier studies [ 48 ]. 
In contrast, the microwave-assisted acid-activated sample shows 
a lower adsorption capacity and a narrower hysteresis loop, 
indicating a less developed pore structure. When evaluated 
together with the XRD, Raman, TGA-DTG, FTIR, and elemental 
composition analyses, this behavior can be linked to the effects of 
microwave treatment, which may induce relatively lower degrees 
of effective carbonization and dehydration, while promoting 
rapid depolymerization, deoxygenation, and partial structural 
collapse. XRD results suggest a reduced development of tur- 
bostratic carbon domains, while Raman analysis reveals changes 
in the disorder degree of the carbon framework. Furthermore, 
FTIR spectra and TGA-DTA results indicate a increase in surface 
oxygen-containing functional groups, supporting the notion that 
excessive or rapid microwave-induced reactions may limit pore 
stabilization. Consequently, the combined structural and chemi- 
cal evidence confirms that ultrasonic activation is more effective 
than microwave treatment in preserving pore integrity and 
enhancing the accessible surface area of the activated carbons.

The evolution of specific surface area and pore structure observed

acid–activated biomass-derived carbons. Megherbi et al. [ 49 ] 
reported the preparation of activated carbon from MC-leaves 
biomass via phosphoric acid–assisted heat treatment, achieving 
a high specific surface area of 1237 m2 g− 1 . Comparable results 
were obtained for bamboo-derived activated carbons, where 
microwave-assisted phosphoric acid activation led to a rapid 
activation process, a carbon yield of 48%, and a surface area of 
1432 m2 g− 1 [ 50 ]. In addition, Özpınar et al. [ 34 ] synthesized 
magnetic activated carbon from hazelnut shell waste using a 
single-step chemical activation and magnetization approach, 
reporting a BET surface area of 1363 m2 g− 1 along with a high 
micropore volume of 0.52 cm3 g− 1 . These literature findings 
quantitatively support the results of the present study, in which 
optimized activation conditions produced activated carbons with 
high surface area, well-developed mesoporosity, and abundant 
accessible active sites, rendering them promising candidates for 
electrochemical energy storage applications. 
2.6 Electrochemical Properties of the Activated 
Carbon Electrodes 
The physicochemical characterization confirms that the 
hazelnut-derived activated carbons possess structural and 
textural features suitable for electrochemical energy-storage 
applications. To evaluate their electrochemical performance, 
cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were first performed, 
as shown in Figure 5 . The electrochemical behavior of the 
activated carbon electrodes was examined in a three-electrode 
configuration using Hg/HgO as the reference electrode, a Pt 
wire as the counter electrode, and 3 M KOH as the aqueous 
electrolyte, within a potential window of − 1.2 to − 0.2 V. 
Figure 5a–c displays the CV responses of the activated carbon 
electrodes at scan rates ranging from 5 to 200 mV s− 1 . All samples 
exhibit nearly rectangular CV profiles, indicating dominant 
capacitive behavior. At lower scan rates, the quasi-rectangular 
shape (see Figure 5d ) is characteristic of electrical double-layer 
capacitance, where charge storage occurs via electrostatic adsorp-

charge storage under high-rate operation, in agreement with 
earlier studies.

tance and rate capability. In contrast, neutral electrolytes such 
as Na2 SO4 impose intrinsic limitations due to their unfavorable 
ionic properties. These findings underscore the necessity of 
simultaneously optimizing carbon pore architecture and elec- 
trolyte composition to achieve high-performance supercapacitor 
systems.

3 Conclusion 
In this work, hazelnut shell–derived activated carbons were 
successfully synthesized via phosphoric acid chemical activa- 
tion followed by microwave and ultrasonic post-treatments, and 
their structural, thermal, and electrochemical properties were 
comprehensively evaluated. Physicochemical characterization 
confirmed that both post-treatment methods significantly mod- 
ified the carbon framework and surface chemistry. Microwave 
treatment promoted extensive surface oxidation and increased 
oxygen functionality, whereas ultrasonic treatment preserved a 
high degree of carbonization while inducing controlled surface 
functionalization through cavitation-driven reactions. 
XRD and Raman analyses revealed turbostratic carbon structures 
with short-range graphitic ordering and defect-rich domains in 
all samples, providing a favorable balance between electrical 
conductivity and electrochemically active sites. Thermal analy- 
sis demonstrated that the ultrasound-assisted activated carbon 
exhibited the lowest overall mass loss and enhanced thermal 
stability, reflecting improved dehydration, carbonization, and 
structural stabilization. 
Electrochemical investigations clearly demonstrated the superi- 
ority of the ultrasound-treated activated carbon. Cyclic voltam- 
metry confirmed dominant electrical double-layer capacitance 
with minor pseudocapacitive contributions arising from surface 
oxygen functionalities. The ACA-US electrode delivered the 
highest specific capacitance, excellent rate capability, and highly 
reversible charge–discharge behavior, as confirmed by GCD 
measurements. Ragone plots revealed that ACA-US achieved 
significantly higher energy densities across a broad power density 
range compared to pristine and microwave-treated samples, high- 
lighting its fast charge–discharge kinetics and efficient utilization 
of active sites. 
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy further confirmed 
improved charge transport properties for ACA-US, including 
reduced charge transfer resistance and enhanced ion diffusion 
within the porous network. The electrode also exhibited excellent 
cycling durability, retaining approximately 85% of its initial 
capacitance after 2000 cycles at high current density, indicating 
robust structural integrity during prolonged operation. 
Overall, this study demonstrates that ultrasonic post-treatment is 
a highly effective strategy for optimizing biomass-derived acti- 
vated carbons by simultaneously enhancing porosity, electrical 
conductivity, and electrochemical stability. The resulting mate- 
rial exhibits outstanding energy storage performance, making 
it a promising and sustainable electrode candidate for next- 
generation supercapacitors.

4.1 Materials and Chemicals 
Hazelnut shells (HS) were selected as the biomass precursor for 
biochar synthesis. The shells were obtained from the Çavuşlar 
region of Ordu, Turkey. Prior to use, the HS were mechanically 
ground into a fine powder using a milling device. The resulting 
material was then sieved to obtain particles in the 250–355 µm 
size range. Phosphoric acid (H3 PO4 ), purchased from Merck 
(Darmstadt, Germany), was used as received without further 
purification.

4.2.2 Activated Carbon Prepared by Acid and

The acid-treated biomass was then subjected to microwave

the penetration and reactivity of phosphoric acid within the 
biomass structure, thereby improving activation efficiency while 
minimizing processing time and energy consumption. Following 
the microwave-assisted pretreatment, the material was pyrolyzed

was dried and subsequently carbonized under the same thermal 
conditions as described above. The sample was denoted as 
ACA-US . 
4.3 Characterization of Activated Carbon 
Materials 
The elemental composition ( C , H , N , and S ) of the activated 
carbon samples ( ACA , ACA-MW , and ACA-US ) was measured 
using an elemental analyzer ( Thermo Scientific Flash 2000/ MAS 
200R ). The oxygen ( O ) content was determined via mass balance, 
based on the differences between the measured elements. Ele- 
mental analysis results represent the average of two independent 
measurements to ensure accuracy. 
The crystalline structure of the samples was examined by X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) using a PANalytical Empyrean diffractometer. 
Measurements were carried out employing Cu K αradiation 
under an operating current of 40 mA and an accelerating voltage 
of 5 kV. Diffraction patterns were collected over a 2 θrange of 10–
90◦with a fixed incident angle of 4◦. The step size was set to 0.013◦
for powder samples to ensure adequate resolution. 
Thermal decomposition of the activated carbo samples was 
analyzed using a PerkinElmer Diamond TG/DTA system 
(PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Measurements were 
performed under nitrogen flow (100 mL min− 1 ) from room 
temperature to 800◦C at a heating rate of 10◦C min− 1 . 
Surface functional groups were identified using Fourier Trans- 
form Infrared ( FT-IR ) spectroscopy ( Bruker Tensor II ). For the 
FT-IR analysis, each sample was mixed with potassium bromide 
( KBr ) at a 2:100 sample to KBr weight ratio. Measurements were 
taken across the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm− 1 , with a 
pure KBr pellet used to obtain the background spectrum. 
Raman measurements were conducted at room temperature 
using a Senterra II from Bruker . The spectra were recorded 
between 800 cm− 1 and 1800 cm− 1 , employing a laser excitation 
wavelength of 532 nm and a laser power of 5 mW. 
The morphology and surface features of the biochar samples 
were determined using a field-emission scanning electron micro- 
scope ( FE-SEM , TESCON Mira III XMU ), equipped with Energy 
Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy ( EDS , Oxford Inst. INCA ). This 
analysis provided insight into the surface texture and elemental 
composition. 
The porosity and surface area of the samples were assessed 
using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) technique. A gas sorp- 
tion analyzer (AUTOSORB 1C, Quantachrome Corp., USA) was 
employed to determine key parameters, including the specific 
surface area (SBET), total pore volume, and average pore diameter, 
offering comprehensive insights into the adsorption behavior of 
the materials. 
4.4 Electrochemical Tests 
Electrochemical characterization, including cyclic voltammetry 
(CV), galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD), and electrochemi- 
cal impedance spectroscopy (EIS), was performed using a Gamry 
Instruments Potentiostat/Galvanostat/ZRA. All measurements 
were conducted in a three-electrode configuration. The working 
electrode was prepared by drop-casting the active material onto 
a glassy carbon electrode (GCE). A platinum wire served as the 
counter electrode. For alkaline electrolytes (3 M and 6 M KOH), 
a Hg/HgO electrode was used as the reference electrode, whereas 
an Ag/AgCl electrode was employed in the neutral 0.5 M Na2 SO4 
electrolyte. 
The working electrodes were fabricated from the activated carbon 
samples by dispersing 2 mg of active material with 10 µL of Nafion 
binder in 2 mL of ethanol. The mixture was ultrasonicated for 
30 min to obtain a homogeneous ink, which was subsequently 
drop-cast onto the GCE and dried at 60◦C for 12 h. 
CV and GCD measurements were carried out within a potential 
window of − 1.2 to − 0.2 V versus the reference electrode. CV 
curves were recorded at scan rates ranging from 5 to 200 mV s− 1 , 
while GCD tests were performed at current densities of 1, 2, 5, and 
10 A g− 1 . Electrochemical measurements were conducted in three 
different electrolytes: 3 M KOH, 6 M KOH, and 0.5 M Na2 SO4 .
