ABSTRACT
Biomass-derived carbon aerogels have attracted considerable attention for applications in CO
2 
capture and 
supercapacitors (SCs). However, these carbon aerogel materials often suffer from an insufficient number of active 
sites. In this study, a novel N, P co-doped porous carbon aerogel with a hierarchical structure was synthesized 
using cellulose biomass as the raw material, a NaOH/urea system as the solvent and activator, and urea and urea 
phosphate as heteroatom dopants. Owing to the synergistic effects of heteroatom co-doping and the hierarchical 
pore structure, the optimal sample, NPCA-600 – 0.5, exhibited a large specific surface area of 582.9 m
2
/g and a 
high pore volume of 0.187 cm
3
/g. It demonstrated a CO
2 
adsorption capacity of 2.89 mmol/g (25 
◦
C, 1 bar), 
along with high CO
2
/N
2 
selectivity and excellent recyclability, retaining 91 % efficiency after 10 cycles. As a SC 
electrode, NPCA-600 – 0.5 achieved a high specific capacitance of 238 F/g at 1 A/g, maintaining 80 % of its initial 
capacitance after 5000 cycles at 10 A/g. This work not only provides a promising adsorbent and electrode 
material for CO
2 
capture and supercapacitors, but also offers valuable insights for the design of advanced carbon- 
based materials.
1. Introduction
As the global population grows and industrialization expands, the 
demand for energy has led to the consumption of vast amounts of fossil 
fuels, resulting in significant greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions [1,2] . 
This situation has triggered a severe energy crisis and exacerbated the 
greenhouse effect. Carbon dioxide, in particular, stands out as the pri -
mary GHG (75 % of total GHG emissions) responsible for climate change 
and accelerated global warming [3,4] . In view of the above problems, 
the development of efficient CO
2 
capture technologies and energy 
storage devices emerges as a potent approach to alleviate the energy 
crisis and mitigate climate change [5,6] .
Based on the capture process principles, several CO
2 
capture 
methods, including chemical absorption [7] , membrane separation [8] , 
and gas – solid adsorption [9] , have undergone extensive research and 
application. Of these, gas – solid adsorption technology has emerged as a 
compelling option, thanks to its high absorption rate, convenient 
regeneration system, energy efficiency, and environmental friendliness 
[10] . Porous carbons are highly regarded as promising CO
2 
gas – solid 
adsorption materials. Their high chemical and thermal stability, exten -
sive specific surface area, adjustable pore structure make them a top 
choice [11] . Furthermore, carbon materials usually possess a layered 
porous structure alongside a substantial specific surface area. Micro -
pores and small mesopores can enhance the storage of charge ions. 
While meso-macro pores provide mass transfer channels to facilitate the 
transport of charge ions in solution [12,13] . This configuration facili -
tates the efficient transfer of ions and electrons, as well as the accu -
mulation of charge, thereby rendering them an excellent candidate for 
energy storage materials [14 – 16] .
In recent years, carbon aerogel-type porous carbon, with its three- 
dimensional structure, high surface area, high electrical conductivity 
and stable physicochemical properties, has been identified as an 
emerging class of functional materials for CO
2 
capture and super -
capacitors [17 – 19] . Zhai et al. [20] prepared the starch-based carbon 
aerogel. When used as an electrode material for supercapacitors, it 
showed excellent electrochemical properties, reaching a specific 
capacitance of 211.5 F/g at 0.5 A/g. Ji et al. [21] prepared a biochar- 
silicon hybrid aerogel using carbon nanotubes and silicon as raw ma -
terials. The hybrid aerogel exhibited good CO
2 
adsorption performance, 
with a CO
2 
adsorption capacity of 2.74 mmol/g at 25 
◦
C and 1 bar. 
Moreover, carbon aerogel-type adsorbents and electrode materials 
derived from renewable biomass sources like cellulose [22] , chitosan

[23] , lignin [12] , and starch [20] have gained increasing attention. 
Cellulose is a macromolecular polysaccharide consisting of glucose 
molecules, and it is widely present in the cell walls of green plants and 
algae [24,25] . As a highly favored renewable biomass material, cellulose 
boasts remarkable attributes such as biodegradability, non-toxicity, 
lightness, and ease of chemical modification [4] . These properties 
collectively render cellulose an appealing feedstock for the production 
of biomass-derived carbon aerogel materials.
However, carbon aerogels ’ limited surface active sites result in low 
CO
2 
absorption capacity or low specific capacitance when used as ad -
sorbents or electrodes [26,27] . Previous research has indeed demon -
strated that doping carbon surfaces with heteroatoms significantly 
boosts CO
2 
capture and electrochemical performance [5,6,14] . N doping 
is the most commonly employed method to improve CO
2 
adsorption and 
discharge capacity by enhancing surface alkalinity and forming pseudo- 
capacitance [13,28] . N doping mainly occurs in the carbon skeleton in 
the form of pyridine-N (N-6), pyrrole-N (N-5), oxide-N (N-X) and gra -
phene (N-Q) [29] . Unlike N atoms, P atoms have a larger radius than 
carbon atoms, which enables them to create vacancy defects and alter 
the electronic structure within the carbon framework. This modification 
enhances the interaction between the adsorbent and CO
2 
molecules and 
provides additional pseudocapacitance [30,31] . Different from single- 
atom doped, co-doped multiple heteroatoms may amplify charge 
transport through the synergistic action of multiple impurity atoms and 
produce more efficient reaction sites [10,32] . Hence, the modification of 
carbon aerogel materials by utilizing the synergistic effect of co-doped 
heteroatoms is an effective approach.
Accordingly, the objective of this study is to develop a novel N, P co- 
doped cellulose-based carbon aerogels for CO
2 
capture and super -
capacitors. Cellulose was selected as the biomass precursor, and the 
NaOH/urea system was used as the pore-forming agent and cellulose 
solvent with urea as the nitrogen source and urea phosphate as the 
phosphorus and nitrogen source. The effects of carbonation temperature 
and heteroatom doping on CO
2 
adsorption and supercapacitor perfor -
mance were systematically determined by adjusting the mass ratios of 
urea phosphate to cellulose and the activation temperature. In addition, 
CO
2 
adsorption thermodynamics and kinetics, as well as CO
2 
cyclic 
adsorption properties and selectivity were systematically evaluated. 
Results indicate that the prepared N, P co-doped porous carbon aerogel 
(NPCA) exhibits a hierarchical porous structure, abundant surface- 
active sites, a good CO
2 
adsorption ability, high selectivity, good 
regeneration, and good capacitance performance.
2. Experimental Section
2.1. Materials
Cellulose (average particle size of 50 μ m) was purchased from 
Aladdin Industrial Co. (Shanghai, China). NaOH, urea, hydrochloric acid 
(HCl), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), 
and acetylene black were obtained from Kelong Chem. Co. (Chengdu, 
China). Urea phosphate was bought from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical 
Co. (Shanghai, China). KOH was purchased from Changliao Chem. Co. 
(Chengdu, China). All reagents were utilized in their original state 
without any further purification.
2.2. Preparation of N, P co-doped carbon aerogels from cellulose
A solution of NaOH (2.50 g), urea (3.83 g) and water (27.0 g) were 
precooled and then cellulose powder (3.83 g) was added. The solution 
was stirred magnetically for 1 h until cellulose was completely dissolved. 
Subsequently, a urea phosphate aqueous solution (10 mL, 0.025   0.1 g/ 
mL) was added dropwise and stirred for another 1 h. The gel was then 
frozen at   25 
◦
C for one day and dried in a freeze-dryer for 2 days to 
obtain a cellulose aerogel precursor.
In order to prepare NPCA, the aforementioned precursor was then 
transferred to a tube furnace and heated under N
2 
protection for 2 h at 
600 
◦
C with a ramp rate of 5 
◦
C/min. Subsequently, the materials were 
washed thoroughly with 1 M HCl and a large volume of DI water until 
the pH of the filtrate became neutral. Finally, the cellulose-derived 
carbon aerogels were dried overnight at 80 
◦
C. The final carbon aero -
gels were labeled as NPCA-T-n, where T represents the activation tem -
perature ( = 500, 550, 600, 650, and 700 
◦
C), and n represents the urea 
phosphate/cellulose mass ratio ( = 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1). For comparison, a 
control carbon aerogel, designated as NPCA-600 – 0 (urea phosphate =
0), was also prepared at 600 
◦
C using a similar synthetic procedure. 
Fig. 1 presents a schematic representation of the synthesis of carbon 
aerogel.

bon aerosol samples.
2.4. Electrochemical measurements
These were firstly conducted in a three-electrode system, employing 
a modified Ni foam as the working electrode, Ag/AgCl, and Pt as the 
reference electrode and the counter electrode, respectively. The elec -
trode preparation process involved the incorporation of 80 % carbon 
aerogel, 10 % acetylene black as the conductive support, and 10 % 
polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) into an N-methyl pyrrolidone (NMP) 
solution under ultrasonic dispersion, thus producing a homogeneous 
ink. The resulting ink was then applied in a dropwise deposition manner 
onto the nickel foam in an area of 1 cm
2
. The modified Ni foam was then 
subjected to a drying process overnight in a vacuum oven at 60 
◦
C. The 
mass loading of the active materials in each electrode was set at 1.2 – 1.6 
mg. Cyclic voltammograms (CV) were obtained from different scanning 
rates (10   200 mV/s), while galvanostatic charge – discharge (GCD) 
tests were carried out at a current density between 1 and 20 A/g. 
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted within a 
frequency range of 0.01 to 100 kHz, with a potential window of 5 mV. 
All the aforementioned measurements were conducted in 6 M KOH 
electrolyte solution using the CHI660E instrument (Shanghai, China).

2.5. CO
2 
adsorption and breakthrough experiment
The intelligent gravimetric adsorption analyzer (IGA-100, Hiden Co., 
UK) was employed to quantify the CO
2 
adsorption capacity of all the 
testing materials at 25 
◦
C and 1 bar. Prior to the experiment, the samples 
were subjected to a vacuum degassing process at 160 
◦
C for 12 h, with 
the objective of eliminating any residual water molecules and other 
impurities present in the material. Subsequently, the CO
2 
adsorption 
isotherm curve was obtained by conducting testing at a constant tem -
perature of 25 
◦
C and a pressure range from 0 to 1 bar. Finally, the 
adsorption isotherm was employed to calculate the capacity of the 
sample. Once the adsorption isotherm experiment had been completed, 
the pumping process was initiated to achieve a specifically reduced 
pressure. The results of the pressure and residual adsorption weights 
were recorded in order to obtain the CO
2 
desorption isotherm curves. 
Through testing the optimal materials, the CO
2 
adsorption kinetic curve 
was acquired by recording the pressure curve over time. In order to 
ascertain the heat of CO
2 
adsorption of NPCA-600 – 0.5, the adsorption 
isotherm of the sample at temperatures between 0 and 25 
◦
C were 
achieved by adjusting the temperature using a circulating water bath. 
The dynamic adsorption curve of CO
2 
was obtained by recording the 
change in CO
2 
adsorption capacity with time and pressure. Specially, at 
25 
◦
C and 1 bar, the pressure was increased to a predetermined value of 
0.01 – 1 bar by means of a flowmeter, and maintained at the pressure for 
10 min. To evaluate cyclic stability of the sample, a continuous ab -
sorption and desorption cycle operation was adopted. Briefly, once the 
sample had reached saturation adsorption, it was subjected to direct 
vacuum at 25 
◦
C for approximately 1 h. This was followed by a second 
adsorption experiment. The adsorption and desorption cycle experi -
ments were repeated 10 times. The adsorption isotherms and adsorption 
kinetic models, along with the thermodynamic parameters for CO
2 
adsorption on the as-prepared N, P co-doped samples, have been 
described in the Electronic Supporting Information (ESI) S 1.1-S 1.4, 
respectively.
The dynamic breakthrough experiments were conducted using a 
BSD-MAB Analyzer (BSD Instrument, Beijing, China) coupled with gas 
BSD-mass spectrometry. Prior to the experiment, the dried carbon aer -
ogel powders were packed into a packed column, which was purged at 
120 
◦
C for 2 h at a constant helium flow rate (10 mL/min). The break -
through experiments commenced with the transition from the purged 
helium gas to a 10/90 CO
2
/N
2 
(v/v) binary gas mixture until adsorption 
saturation was reached.
3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Characterization of the materials
The surface functional groups of the NPCA-T-n samples were 
analyzed using FT-IR spectroscopy ( Fig. 2 ). The results indicated similar 
stretching vibrational peaks across all samples, except for NPCA-600 – 0. 
As illustrated in Fig. 2 a and 2b, the introduction of urea phosphate led to 
the emergence of a distinct peak at 1060 cm
  1

bond [23,33] . Notably, this characteristic P = O peak grew more 
prominent as the urea phosphate/cellulose mass ratio increased. The 
broad peak centered around 3427 cm
  1 
corresponds to the symmetric

hydroxyl groups [34] . Peaks at 2920 and 1255 cm

  1 
repre -

cessful doping of N and P atoms into the prepared carbon aerogels.

600 – 0.5 sample was presented in Fig. 3 e, and it can be seen that the four 
elements, C, N, O, and P are uniformly distributed on the surface of the 
material, indicating the successful introduction of N and P elements.

It is widely recognized that the adsorption and capacitive properties 
of carbon materials are strongly linked to their specific surface area and 
pore structure [42] . To gain insights into the impact of activation tem -
perature and the doping ratio of urea-phosphate on the material struc -
ture, N
2 
adsorption/desorption isotherms were employed to analyze and 
characterize the specific surface area and pore structure of all the sam -
ples. The findings from these analyses are presented in Fig. 5 and 
Table 1 . Referring to Fig. 5 a and 5b and based on the IUPA classification, 
it is evident that nearly all samples presented a blend of type I and type 
IV nitrogen adsorption – desorption curves [30,43] . This suggests that the 
materials possess a hierarchical pore structure where micropores and

3.2. CO
2 
adsorption performance
An ideal adsorbent should exhibit several key characteristics: low 
cost, high CO
2 
adsorption capacity, excellent selectivity, reduced 
regeneration energy, and remarkable stability throughout numerous 
adsorption and desorption cycles [9] . To assess these attributes in the 
prepared carbon aerogels, an intelligent gravimetric adsorption analyzer 
was employed to measure CO
2 
adsorption in all samples under stan -
dardized conditions of 25 
◦
C and 1 bar. Fig. 6 a illustrates how the 
adsorption performance of materials varies with different urea phos -
phate ratios. Initially, as the urea phosphate ratio increases from 0 to 
0.5, the CO
2 
adsorption capacity of the materials rises. However, further 
increasing the urea phosphate content leads to a decrease in perfor -
mance. Similarly, Fig. 6 b reveals that CO
2 
adsorption follows the same 
trend with an increase in activation temperature. As shown in Table S3 , 
the sample NPCA-600 – 0.5 achieves the best CO
2 
adsorption perfor -
mance of 2.89 mmol/g, which is notably higher (25.6 %) than that of the 
control (NPCA-600 – 0, 2.3 mmol/g), primarily attributable to the 
beneficial effects of moderate phosphorus doping that enhances the 
porosity of the carbon material [29] . Furthermore, BET data reveals that 
NPCA-700 – 0.5 possesses the largest specific surface area, yet its CO
2 
adsorption performance is inferior to NPCA-600 – 0.5. This indicates that 
the relative content of heteroatoms may be the dominant factor in the 
CO
2 
adsorption of the materials. As shown in Table S2 , the content of N 
and P atoms in the materials decreased with increasing activation 
temperature, resulting in fewer active sites for CO
2 
adsorption. Addi -
tionally, the adsorption performance of the best samples was compared 
with currently reported porous carbon materials ( Table S6 ).
To further explore the adsorption mechanism of NPCA-600 – 0.5 for 
CO
2
, four isothermal adsorption models (Freundlich, Langmuir, Redlich- 
Peterson and Sips) were fitted to the experimental data of this optimal 
sample. The specific fitting equations are provided in the ESI (S 1.1), 
while the fitting results and related parameters are presented in Fig. 6 c 
and Table S4 . Previous research has demonstrated that the fitting 
parameter χ 
2 
serves as a metric for assessing the accuracy of the fit, with 
lower values indicating higher precision [44,45] . As observed in 
Table S4 , under identical conditions, the Freundlich model exhibits a 
lower χ
2 
value than that of the Langmuir model, suggesting that physical 
adsorption likely plays a dominant role in CO
2 
adsorption. Moreover, 
the Redlich-Peterson model yields the lowest χ
2 
value and the highest 
correlation coefficient R
2
; thus, this model is more in line with the 
adsorption characteristics of the material, indicating both physical and 
chemical adsorption occurring concurrently. Furthermore, Fig. 6 d 
shows that the adsorption capacities (in mmol CO
2
/g) of NPCA-600 – 0.5 
were 3.80 at 0 
◦
C, 3.40 at 10 
◦
C, and 2.89 at 25 
◦
C, suggesting that the 
adsorption process is exothermic.
As a crucial metric for evaluating the adsorption performance of a 
material, Q
st 
was calculated for NPCA-600 – 0.5 using the Clausius- 
Clapeyron equation ( ESI, S1.4 ) based on the adsorption data obtained 
at various temperatures. Fig. 6 e reveals that the slope derived from the 
fitting line corresponds to the ratio of the isothermal heat of adsorption 
(Q
st
) to the gas constant (R). Notably, all slopes are negative, unequiv -
ocally demonstrating that the adsorption of CO
2 
onto NPCA-600 – 0.5 is 
an exothermic process. The values of Q
st 
range from 24.8 to 32.1 KJ/mol 
( Fig. 6 f), suggesting that the adsorption process is primarily 
physisorption-dominated. The initially higher Q
st 
value signifies a strong 
interaction between the adsorbent and CO
2 
molecules [28,46] . How -
ever, as the adsorption amount gradually increases, the value of Q
st 
decreases, primarily attributed to the occupation of strong active sites by 
the adsorbed molecules [26] . Based on the aforementioned results, it is 
evident that the heat of adsorption for NPCA-600 – 0.5 is relatively low, 
signifying that the adsorption and regeneration processes of this mate -
rial are more readily achievable with low energy consumption. The 
favorable characteristic renders it a promising candidate for practical 
production applications.
The adsorption rate of CO
2 
molecules on the surface of NPCA- 
600 – 0.5 was assessed through kinetic experiments conducted at 25 
◦
C 
and 1 bar, as depicted in the respective Fig. 6 g. Notably, NPCA-600 – 0.5 
achieved maximum adsorption within 10 min, signifying its rapid CO
2 
adsorption kinetics. To further analyze the correlation between CO
2 
adsorption capacity and adsorption time, two kinetic models, Fick 
diffusion and Linear Driving Force (LDF) diffusion, were applied to fit 
the kinetic data. The detailed descriptions and formulas for these models 
are provided in the ESI (S 1.3). The fitted curves and corresponding 
parameters are presented in Fig. 6 g and Table S5 . Notably, the LDF 
model exhibited a relatively small χ
2 
value, indicating that the kinetic 
behavior of NPCA-600 – 0.5 aligns more closely with the LDF model. This 
also reflects that the overall adsorption process of NPCA-600 – 0.5 is 
primarily governed by the CO
2 
transfer rate [30] . The dynamic 
adsorption capacity of NPCA-600 – 0.5 was evaluated by adjusting the 
flowmeter ’ s flow rate to simulate dynamic pressure changes, thereby 
measuring the variation in CO
2 
adsorption over time. As depicted in 
Fig. 6 h, the CO
2 
adsorption by NPCA-600 – 0.5 increased in tandem with 
rising pressure, highlighting its excellent dynamic adsorption capacity 
for CO
2
. Notably, the dynamic cumulative adsorption amount of NPCA-

identical temperature and pressure conditions.

potential as a supercapacitor electrode material.
The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis of the 
NPCAs electrodes provides insights into their resistance and ion trans -
port characteristics. In the low frequency region, the electrodes exhibit 
nearly vertical lines, suggesting excellent capacitive performance and 
ion diffusion efficiency. In the high frequency region, the intercept of the 
EIS curves on the real axis corresponds to the equivalent series resis -
tance, while the semicircle width reflects the charge transfer resistance 
of the material. Notably, all samples exhibited smaller intercepts and 
narrower semicircles, indicating a lower impedance for the NPCAs 
electrodes [14] ( Fig. 7 c and S 3a). The CV curves of NPCA-600 – 0.5 were 
evaluated at varying scan rates ranging from 10 to 200 mV/s within a 
voltage window of   1 to 0 V ( Fig. 7 d). As the scan rate increased, the CV 
curves maintained a rectangular shape with minimal distortion, indi -
cating NPCA-600 – 0.5 ’ s swift ion transport capability [12,48] . Similarly, 
when the CV curves of the other samples were tested at different sweep 
speeds ( Fig. S4 ), they exhibited comparable shapes, indicating the 
presence of double-layer capacitance in all samples. The GCD curves of 
NPCA-600 – 0.5 and the remaining samples, at various current densities, 
are depicted in Fig. 7 e and Fig. S5 . All samples exhibited approximately 
symmetrical triangular shapes, demonstrating a typical double-layer 
capacitive behavior and remarkable Coulombic efficiency. The specific 
capacitance of NPCA ’ s electrodes at different current densities is shown 
in Fig. 7 f and S 3b. It is evident that NPCA-600 – 0.5 displays excellent 
rate capability, maintaining a specific capacitance of 122 F/g even at a 
high current density of 20 A/g.

4. Conclusion
This study successfully demonstrated the preparation of N, P co- 
doped porous carbon aerogels from cellulose using a simple carboniza -
tion method with a NaOH/urea system. The optimized NPCA-600 – 0.5 
sample exhibited a notably large specific surface area (582.9 m
2
/g) and 
microporous volume (0.187 cm
3
/g), along with appropriate N (9.78 %) 
and P (0.14 %) doping levels. As a CO
2 
adsorbent, NPCA-600 – 0.5 
showed a superior CO
2 
adsorption capacity of 2.89 mmol/g at 25 
◦
C and 
1 bar, an exceptional CO
2
/N
2 
selectivity of 9.7, and excellent regener -
ability. Furthermore, when applied as a supercapacitor electrode, NPCA- 
600 – 0.5 exhibited a high specific capacitance (238 F/g at 1 A/g) and 
remarkable cycling stability. Notably, in a two-electrode symmetric 
device configuration, the NPCA-600 – 0.5 electrode achieved an energy 
density of 6.5 Wh/kg at a power density of 50 W/kg. This exceptional 
performance could be mainly attributed to synergistic effects of its hi -
erarchical porous structure and N, P co-doping. This study not only 
validates the dual-functional potential of N, P co-doped carbon aerogels 
as CO
2 
adsorbents and supercapacitor electrodes but also provides 
valuable insights into the design of porous carbon materials.
