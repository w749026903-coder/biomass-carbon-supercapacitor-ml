Abstract
Supercapacitors (SCs) are promising energy-storage systems whose efficiency largely depends on electrode composition 
and design. Utilizing carbon waste is vital for advancing sustainable technologies due to its high storage capacity, low cost, 
wide availability and eco-friendly nature. Biomass-derived carbon materials, with their high surface area, conductivity 
and environmental benefits, have emerged as strong candidates for next-generation SC electrodes. This review presents a 
systematic analysis of recent strategies for converting biomass waste into high-performance electrode materials for SCs. 
In addition, this summarizes an innovative thematic area where activated carbon is derived from novel sources like peanut 
shells, jujube shells, tea and coffee waste, hazelnuts, leather waste, paper waste etc. In addition, it encompasses the prepa -
ration of carbon-based products and strategies for enhancing porous structures by activation. Incorporating these materi -
als in SCs offers the dual benefit of enhancing energy storage performance while contributing to waste management and 
resource recycling. Importantly, this review introduces the novel concept of electrochemical double-layer compression due 
to solvated radicals, which modifies classical Helmholtz theory which has no prior literature. We further discuss the role of 
sequential chemical and physical activation in tuning porosity and ion transport with unique design proposal of sequential 
activation. Furthermore, future research directions are highlighted, zeroing in on promoting the scalability, electrochemi -
cal double-layer capacitor performance, and commercial credibility of such electrodes with the effects of impurities and 
quality control. This approach contributes to the development of low-cost, sustainable and scalable energy storage systems.

Electrochemical double-layer capacitance
Electric double-layer capacitance is a mechanism for stor -
ing charge in third-generation electrochemical double-
layer capacitors (EDLCs), whose electric energy relies on 
electrosorption over porous electrodes, which are usually 
made of carbon. A German physicist, Hermann V on Helm-
holtz, marked the theory of EDLC in 1853, patented it in 
1957, and started practical usage. However, commercially, 
a device based on this principle was first developed in the 
Central Research Laboratory of Matsushita Electric Indus -
trial Company in 1978.
Principle and configuration of electric-double layer
The operation of an electrochemical double-layer capaci -
tor (EDLC) relies on the double-layer charge development 
over the electrode/electrolyte interface. The interplay of 
pore size, surface area and ion mobility governs the perfor -
mance of EDLCs. The bi-layered specific capacitance can 
be determined in various ways, such as normalization over 
the volume, surface area, or mass of the system. Presently, 
the aim is to normalize the specific capacitance over the

Why biomass derived carbon yields a different EDLC 
layer configuration?
Biomass derived carbons introduce unprecedented changes 
in EDLC configurations, as the carbons after extraction con-
tain, in addition to the normal pore structure and porous net-
work, charged radical species and organic centres of charge. 
Solvated radical species such as CO 3

cantly alter the dielectric constant at the interface. The outer 
layers of the double layer (Helmholtz or Stern and Gouy-
Chapman layers) contain fixed and diffuse layer of charges 
that in general preserve electrical neutrality. In the case of 
biomass derived carbons, in the Stern layer of fixed posi -
tive charges, charged radicals are introduced partially (the 
charged radicals could be C +, CO−, CO3−, N3+, P5−, etc. in 
the solvated form) and thus alter the net charge density of 
the Stern layer. This results in a thinner electrical double-
layer, with enhanced capacitance per unit area. Thus, in 
opposition to conventional wisdom that fixed Stern charges 
require a fixed number of diffuse charges to maintain elec -
trical equilibrium, in this case, a single solvated radical can 
neutralize higher number of Stern charges. The charged 
radicals are also introduced in the diffuse Gouy Chapman 
layer. Thus, fewer number of charges are required in both 
Stern and Gouy Chapman layers to maintain neutrality and 
leads to same streaming potential with a thinner layer thick-
ness i.e. compressed Stern and compressed Gouy Chapman 
layers [25–33].
The primary reason for this is the higher electrical sphere 
of influence of the single solvated radical and requirement 
of fewer counter charged species to balance the electrical 
circuit. Both inner and outer Helmholtz planes are shifted 
in such a way that the entire layer is compressed. Addition-
ally, the solvated charged radicals provide higher charge 
with degradation efficiency, potentially increasing corrosion 
rates.
Key factors governing EDLC performance: 
electrolyte-surface interactions, pore structure, and 
transport limitations
 
 ●   Internal resistance: This results from the electrode and 
electrolyte leading to a decrease in the effective voltage. 
In applications involving a high-current discharge, it is 
crucial to minimize the internal resistance.
 ●   Pore size: Optimization of pore dimensions in elec -
trode materials is critical for maximizing the capaci -
tance over the double layers. It is essential for the pore 
size to closely match the size of the ions in the electro -
lyte to achieve optimal capacitance. If the sizes are not 
aligned, the capacitance will decrease.
 ●   Electrolyte selection: Careful selection and optimiza -
tion of the electrolyte are pivotal for achieving optimal 
performance of EDLCs. The electrolyte composition, 
which encompasses the type and concentration of ions, 
exerts a substantial influence on the specific capacitance 
and overall functionality of the double-layer capacitor.
 ●   Leakage current: When an EDLC is charged for an 
extended period, the charges diminish but do not reach 
zero, maintaining a constant value known as the leakage 
current.
 ●   Hybridization: Hybrid systems that integrate materials 
for pseudocapacitors and EDLCs on a single-electrode 
substrate have the potential to enable high-voltage op -
eration with superior stability and long-term cyclability 
[18–22].

Electrical behavior of biomass carbon
The electrical characteristics of carbon materials are intrin -
sically governed by their internal structure, particularly the 
ratio of sp 2 to sp 3 hybridized carbon atoms, which defines 
charge transport pathways. Most carbon precursors exhibit 
very high resistivity (> 1012 Ω cm) due to the predominance 
of σ-bonded sp 3 configurations. Upon thermal treatment, 
progressive carbonization induces the transformation of sp³ 
to sp² bonds, generating conjugated networks that enhance 
screening thus enabling further compression of the inner and 
outer Helmholtz planes. The charged solvated species also 
have higher charge mobility. As a basis to further improve 
the aforementioned radical-induced electric double-layer 
compression concept, it is important to clarify some other 
salient assumptions involved in the behavior and levels of 
the generated charged radical particles in the context of the 
electrical double-layer functioning in the aforementioned 
biomass-derived carbon particles. For one, it was assumed 
that the aforementioned generated radical particles in the 
carbon medium have relatively shorter lifetimes, yet still 
sufficient for the required electrochemical responses, due 
to the heteroatom functionality, heterogeneity, and con -
finement within the aforementioned microporous environ -
ment in the carbon particles. This could potentially result 
in repetitive and local quasi-steady radical generation for 
the entire duration of the charging-discharge cycles in the 
carbon particles, thus significantly affecting the consequent 
Stern and diffuse double-layer structures in the carbon par -
ticles, considering the maintenance of electroneutrality in 
the system [30–33].

Structural architectures of biomass carbon 
electrodes
Carbon is a widely available and structurally diverse mate -
rial that exhibits several allotropes in crystalline form, such 
as carbyne (sp 1), graphite (sp 2), fullerenes (distorted sp 2), 
and diamond (sp 3). Graphite and diamond occur naturally. 
Various natural materials, including coal, crude oil, and 
biomass, can serve as precursors for carbon-based materi -
als (Fig. 6). These materials undergo significant changes in 
their properties at the nanoscale. Dimensionality in carbon 
nanostructures from 0D to 3D affects ion transport, surface 
accessibility, and electrolyte interaction. Additionally, mod-
ern EDLCs predominantly utilize carbon with pores as the 
active material for electrode [43–46].
  
0-D frameworks
Copiously organically available materials, including food 
waste, charcoal, wood remains, nutshells, and coconut 
shells, are highly appealing resources for the nature focus -
sing on economic processing of carbon materials with 
active pores. The porous nature of the activated carbon 
significantly affects the capacitance by maintaining a spe -
cific surface area. However, various aspects of the carbon 
structure, including pore dimensions, functional groups on 
the surface, and ionic conductivity, must be carefully noted. 
The unique geometry of 0D carbon dots enables enhanced 
quantum capacitance in nanoscale SC systems. The treat -
ment for activation is essential to enhance the specific area 
in order to boost the specific capacitance, primarily through 
opening pores that may be obstructed, clogged, or closed. 
Porous carbons are typically activated through either high-
temperature physical (thermal) activation using H 2O, CO2 
and air at temperatures of 700–1200 °C, or through chemi -
cal activation at lower temperatures (600–800 °C) using 
H3PO4, KOH, or ZnCl 2 [40]. The resultant ACs exhibit a 
wide distribution of pore sizes, stratified into micropores (< 
2 nm), mesopores (2–50 nm), and macropores (> 50 nm). 
Optimization of activation parameters ensures maximum 
contribution of mesopores to capacitance. Theoretically, the 
range of EDL capacitance is over 15 to 25 µF cm − 2, but 
π-electron delocalization and facilitate electronic conduc -
tion. A pronounced rise in conductivity is typically observed 
near 700 °C, followed by a slower improvement at higher 
temperatures as graphitic ordering approaches equilibrium. 
This trend coincides with the removal of oxygen-containing 
surface functionalities through the evolution of CO 2 and 
H2O around 600–700 °C. When subjected to temperatures 
above 2500 °C, the structure evolves from a near-amor -
phous to a highly ordered graphitic state, accompanied by a 
substantial reduction in electrical resistivity [22–25, 32–38].
Due to the intrinsic anisotropy of graphite, the electrical 
resistivity of carbon varies with crystallographic orienta -
tion. Graphite exhibits a resistivity of approximately 10 − 5 
Ω cm within the aromatic ring plane and about 10 − 2 Ω cm 
perpendicular to it, reflecting the directional dependence of 
charge transport. The degree of graphitic ordering achieved 
through high-temperature treatment can be accessed via 
Raman spectroscopy or X-ray diffraction. At the macro -
scopic scale, the intrinsic resistivity of carbon electrodes is 
further influenced by particle morphology, packing density, 
and compaction pressure. Enhanced compaction reduces 
inter-particle voids and contact resistance, while smaller 
particle sizes shorten charge transport pathways, thereby 
improving bulk conductivity [39, 40].
In porous and powder-type carbon electrodes, the struc -
tured packing determines both ionic and electronic trans -
port. The void spaces between irregularly shaped particles 
influence electrolyte distribution, necessitating an opti -
mized balance to prevent electrolyte depletion. Thin carbon 
coatings are advantageous for minimizing equivalent series 
resistance (ESR) and supporting rapid charge discharge 
processes. Surface oxygen functionalities such as quinones 
and carboxyl groups can introduce redox-active sites, con -
tributing to pseudocapacitance enhancement [41]. However, 
excessive surface oxidation arising from prolonged grind -
ing or oxygen exposure at elevated temperatures can disrupt 
graphitic continuity and increase electrical resistivity. Con-
versely, the removal of these oxides by thermal treatment 
in an inert atmosphere (~ 1000 °C) restores conductivity by 
improving electron mobility across micro-crystallite inter -
faces [22, 41].
Surface chemistry plays a pivotal role in determining 
the electrochemical behavior of carbon-based electrodes.

gens) significantly influence parameters such as the poten -

ion adsorption dynamics. Graphitic surfaces consist of two 
chemically distinct sites basal planes and edges where the 
latter exhibit higher reactivity and stronger interactions 
with ions. A higher edge-to-basal site ratio, often associated 
with structural disorder, enhances redox activity and con -
tributes to pseudocapacitive behavior. During activation or

macropores, improving ion accessibility and charge storage 
performance. Furthermore, coupling CO2 activation directly 
with carbonization simplifies the process and substantially 
reduces production costs while maintaining control over 
porosity and surface area [67].

Chemical activation
Chemical activation is a well-established single-step pro -
cedure to produce AC utilizing various chemical agents, 
including sodium hydroxide, zinc chloride, nitric acid, 
potassium hydroxide, potassium chloride, and phosphoric 
acid. This method has multiple benefits compared with 
the physical activation method as it introduces active 
sites through etching and redox reactions, enabling higher 
capacitance. This process facilitates the development of 
well-formed pores with controllable sizes. It yields materi -
als with a high specific surface area and elevated carbon 
yield. In addition, it operates through a streamlined one-step 
procedure and requires a reduced pyrolysis temperature. In 
the context of energy applications, specifically SCs, the ben-
efits of chemical activation are significant. This activation 
process typically involves the pretreatment of biomass with 
various chemical agents, which may include strong acids 
such as H 2SO4 and HNO 3, weak acids such as H 3PO4 and 
H3BO3, salts such as ZnCl2 and strong bases such as NaOH 
and KOH. Subsequently, the treated composition was sub -
jected to heating in an inert environment with temperatures 
maintained between 400 °C and 900 °C. Among the afore -
mentioned chemicals, KOH is recognized as the most prev-
alent activating agent for the preparation of SC electrode 
materials because it facilitates the production of carbons 
characterized by a high specific surface area and enhanced 
pore volume development [65–69].

To clearly distinguish high-performance biomass-derived 
carbon electrodes, this review defines “high-efficiency elec-
trode performance” based on quantitative electrochemical 
and structural benchmarks. A biomass-derived carbon can 
be considered a high-performance supercapacitor electrode 
when it demonstrates: (i) specific capacitance ≥ 250 F g⁻¹

diffusion characteristics, and (iv) cycling stability ≥ 90% 
after 10,000 charge–discharge cycles [ 4, 8–11]. Structur -
ally, high-efficiency materials must possess hierarchically 
interconnected micro–mesoporous networks that ensure 
both high charge storage and efficient ion transport, along 
with moderate graphitization and low ash/impurity content 
(< 5 wt%).

sites, which significantly integrates the overall capacitance.

of energy-storage technologies [4, 48].

Suggested rational design, activation 
engineering, and scalable synthesis
From the point of view of material chemistry and electro -
chemical engineering, the material comprising cellulose 
and lignin has been found to be an extremely versatile pre -
cursor material for the rational design of high capacitance 
carbon-based electrodes. The agricultural residues, wood 
wastes, and fibers inherently possess biopolymer structures 
that can be carbonized at high temperatures and possess the 
potential of tuning the porosities while designing conduc -
tive carbon structures. However, precursor purification has 
been identified as a cardinal step towards the design of high-
performance carbon-based EDLCs. Mild acid treatment of 
the precursor material using HCl and HNO3 has been found 
effective in leaching naturally occurring metal ions such 
as potassium, calcium, magnesium, and iron; silicates; and 
ash, which adversely affect the microporous structure of the 
carbon material and increase internal resistance while com-
promising the stability of the carbon material during long-
term cycling operation.
A staged carbonization-activation strategy offers the 
most effective way to engineer biomass-derived high-per -
formance carbons. Initial pre-carbonization at 400–600 °C 
under an inert atmosphere removes volatile organics, conse-
quently stabilizing the carbon skeleton while intrinsic micro-
structural features from cellulose and lignin are preserved. A 
second high-temperature step of carbonization from 700 to 
900 °C promotes aromatization with partial graphitization, 
enhancing electronic conductivity and mechanical integrity. 
Among the activation methods, KOH activation remains 
the most efficient in creating ultra-high surface areas along 
materials used. In contrast, activated carbon SC electrodes 
are a more economical option, with costs typically between 
$3 & $8 per unit. This cost-effectiveness is primarily due 
to the well-established production processes and the advan-
tages of economies of scale associated with large-scale 
manufacturing. Zeroing on sustainability and reducing car -
bon footprints, the material under discussion presents viable 
opportunities for commercialization. By further research -
ing and integrating carbonization and activation processes, 
we can enhance biomass processing techniques, ultimately 
making fabrication more economical [133].

Conclusion and future prospects
This review has deeply explored the developments in the field 
of biomass or waste-based carbon materials as an efficient 
tool for the construction of environmentally friendly, highly 
efficient electrodes for the implementation of the concept 
of supercapacitors. Starting from the basic mechanisms of 
double-layer electrical charge accumulation, the discussion 
escalated to various properties of the material that make the 
produced material distinct from the commonly used acti -
vated carbons. A range of waste materials used to develop 
these hierarchically structured porosity-based carbon mate-
rials was explored, from the diverse classes of agro-wastes 
to waste-based products from various industries. Likewise, 
the discussion on the significance of 0D, 1D, 2D, and 3D 
structures of carbon-based materials was addressed to high-
light the collective significance of these structures in mak -
ing the material highly efficient. The review also introduced 
a model of chemical-physical activation via a structured 
sequential strategy, which plays a significant role in the 
development of advanced carbon-based electrodes, par -
ticularly through a correlation with electrochemical perfor -
mance indicators. On the other hand, the activated carbon 
concept in terms of novel electrical double-layer compres -
sion by a radical charge involves an interesting aspect in 
terms of next-generation charge storage. Most importantly, 
it indicates that localized electrostatic effects have the 
potential to enable significant improvements in capacitance, 
which reinforces the scientific and technological importance 
of biomass-based carbon. The above information leads to an 
eventual conclusion regarding the importance of biomass-
based carbon, particularly in terms of its role as an advanced 
electrode material (Fig. 13).
Looking ahead towards the prospect of improving the 
efficiency and widen the application of waste biomass-
derived carbon and composite electrodes, research should 
focus on hierarchical pore engineering, heteroatom dop -
ing, and defect chemistry to enhance ion access and charge 
transfer kinetics. Moreover, hybridization with materials 
that display pseudocapacitive effects such as transition-
metal oxides, sulfides, MXenes, and conducting polymers 
holds significant potential as a means of moving beyond 
the fundamental barrier of increasing the energy density of 
conventional EDLCs and towards the development of mate-
rials with multiple functionalities for energy storage. Addi -
tionally, data-driven optimization via machine learning and 
modeling will help to identify the best precursor-activation 
combinations and tune the performance of the materials.

spectrum to which the technology may be applied. In other
