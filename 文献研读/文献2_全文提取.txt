ABSTRACT
Recently, MXenes have become effective electrode materials for devices including 
batteries, supercapacitors (SCs), sensors, antennas, and optoelectronic devices, as 
well as for sustainable energy storage. They are promising choices for a variety of 
energy conversion and storage materials due to their metallic conductivity, good 
hydrophilicity, and ability to act as ideal templates for developing other materi-
als with tunable features. Recent advances in energy devices and storage using 
MXene as electrode materials are outlined in this article. The fundamental ideas 
of energy storage and renovation systems are examined. The following sections 
highlight the special qualities of Green Synthesis Methods for MXenes. Potential 
fixes for issues that arose during the development of MXene were investigated, as 
was the use of Machine learning techniques as cutting-edge approaches to MXene 
product optimisation. Additionally, MXene’s applications in energy conversion 
devices, such as supercapacitors, catalysis, various batteries, and electrochemi-
cal sensing were highlighted. A discussion of potential future opportunities for 
MXene-based materials in energy industries for sustainable technology wraps 
up the study.

Green synthesis method
The need for sustainable synthesis has prompted a 
surge in the creation of HF-free MXenes, sometimes 
known as "green MXenes," since the late 2010s [1 , 3]. 
Figure 3a–h shows a schematic sequence of the current 
techniques of green MXenes that produce sustainable 
products. These include halogen gas etching, chemi-
cal vapour deposition (CVD), alkali, electrochemical, 
Lewis acidic, UV light-induced, hydrothermal, and 
microwave. This section describes the safer, more 
sustainable approaches these techniques offer for the 
green synthesis of MXenes.
Alkali etching method
The greater chemical reactivity of M–A bonds in com-
parison to M–X bonds causes the selective elimina -
tion of "A" atoms from MAX phases. For instance, Al 
is removed as  AF3 and  ACl3 by halogen-based etch-
ing techniques that use F⁻ and  Cl− ions, respectively 
[42]. According to Li et al. [43], building on these 
earlier discoveries, the Li group found that NaOH 
functions similarly to HF, facilitating the etching pro-
cess as depicted in Fig. 3a. The etching mechanism is 
expressed in Eqs. 1 and 2.
Li et al. [44] affirmed that MXene surfaces with –OH 
and/or –O terminations are produced using this tech-
nique, leading to high hydrophilicity. Furthermore, 
since Na⁺ has a larger ionic radius than Li⁺ (used in 
the HCl + LiF etching), the insertion of Na⁺ cations 
increases the interlayer gap. As an alternative, pow-
erful alkalis like tetramethylammonium hydroxide 
(TMAOH) and KOH can also work well as etchants 
(1)
Ti3AlC2 + NaOH + H2O → Ti3C2 + NaAlO2 + 1.5H2

[45]. To achieve a higher yield and finer delamination, 
Natu et al. [46] reported that the intercalation of  TMA+ 
helps control the response among the Al layer and 
TMAOH. However, the limited etching power of alkali 
etching techniques, compared to active HF-based 
acidic etching, which is accompanied by incomplete 
Al removal, makes them difficult to commercialise. 
Further impeding reactions with low overall yields is 
the OH⁻ attack, which also produces Al hydroxides (Al 
(OH)3) and its dehydrated derivatives (AlO (OH)) [47].
Electrochemical etching technique
The electrochemical etching method is an alternative 
technique that does not require acidic or alkaline solu-
tions. Because the weak M–X bond permits the selec-
tive etching of the "A" layer under an applied electric 
field, it permits the direct removal of "A" elements [30, 
68]. Selective electrochemical etching of "A" sites can 
be achieved by adjusting the etching potential and 
time. This method produces cleaner MXenes with only 
Cl–, O–, and OH-terminal groups by avoiding the use 
of fluoride ions, in contrast to HF-free etching [61, 68]. 
The electrochemical etching process for producing 
 T i3C2Tx, as shown in Fig. 3b, was initially published by 
Sun et al. [61].  Ti3AlC2 was electrochemically etched 
in a binary aqueous electrolyte of tetramethylammo-
nium hydroxide (TMA⋅OH) and ammonium chloride 
 (NH4Cl) [69–71]. The –Cl surface termination on the 
MAX surface was created by the oxidation of the free 
 C l− from the NH4Cl electrolyte [30]. Equations 3 and

Lewis acidic etching method
Lewis-acidic molten salts can effectively replace con-
ventional etching techniques as an alternative green 
chemical strategy. In a molten-salt liquid medium, this 
technique selectively eliminates the “ A” layers with-
out the need for an acid-based solvent [ 62]. Molten 
salts, which usually form above 500 °C, remove the 
functional groups –O and –OH, allowing for con-
trolled halogenated terminations (–Cl, –Br, and –I). 
As reported by Li et al. [62], the study used  ZnCl2 at 
550 °C to produce  Ti3C2Cl2 MXene, as shown in Fig. 3c. 
Equations 5, 6, and 7 summarise the process mecha-
nism for the expected MXene formation. The process 
involves  Zn2
+ ions in molten  ZnCl2, which accept elec-
trons and act as Lewis acids.
In a redox reaction, Al is oxidised to  Al3+ because 
the bond between Al and Ti is weaker. The resultant 
 A l3
+ ion subsequently joins forces with Cl− to cre-
ate  AlCl3, which vaporises around 550 °C due to its 
low boiling point of about 180 °C. This promotes the 
reaction and guarantees effective etching. Based on 
these findings, other A-site elements (Zn, Al, Si, and 
Ga) in the MAX phases were modified, and the Lewis 
acid melt composition was optimised, to expand the 
synthesis of MXenes further [29, 75]. An ammonium 
persulfate (APS) solution was used to eliminate any 
remaining Cu particles, prepared in molten  CuCl2 
at 750 °C [76]. The adaptability of this method was 
further demonstrated by the successful exfoliation of 
several Ti-, Nb-, and Mo-based MAX phases using dif-
ferent chloride-based molten salts [77– 79]. The Lewis 
acid method’s principal drawback is the presence of 
metallic contaminants that remain after the etching 
process, despite its broader etching spectrum and 
enhanced chemical safety. Consequently, further acid 
treatment is required for total elimination [80, 81]. 
Additionally, compared to wet etching techniques, 
etching in molten salts requires temperatures up to 
800 °C and longer dwell times (about 24 h), which may 
increase energy consumption.
Ultraviolet (UV)‑induced etching method
Recent studies have centred on photochemical tech-
niques that permit MXene production at ambient or 
near-room-temperature settings to overcome the high-
temperature constraints of molten-salt-based etching. 
Mei et al. [63] demonstrate the sequence of UV etching 
techniques, as shown in Fig. 3d. This technique used 
phosphoric acid as the etchant to create mesoporous 
 M o2C MXenes from  Mo2Ga2C MAX using ultraviolet 
(UV)-induced selective etching. According to Thomas 
et al. [82], by speeding up surface responses under UV 
irradiation, which deteriorated Ga bonds and helped 
selective etching,  Mo2Ga2C, a potent UV-absorbing 
material, makes it easier to remove Ga layers. Impor-
tantly, UV irradiation is a better technique because it 
does not require dangerous, extremely caustic acid 
solutions. Additionally, compared to traditional HF 
techniques, this technology reduces processing time 
by enabling green MXene production in just hours. 
However, if the MAX candidates are UV-sensitive, 
this method is not applicable. Further oxidation occurs 
when UV light interacts with  TiO2, producing  O3 and 
 O H4 radicals. To produce quality green MXenes under 
these UV conditions, further research is needed.

Microwave‑assisted hydrothermal etching 
process
To develop a more reliable MXene synthesis method, 
the current study has improved the methodology 
developed by Latif et al. [65] which used a NaOH 
solution and a traditional hydrothermal heating 
method for MXene production, as illustrated in 
Fig. 3f. The current investigation prepared solutions 
at concentrations of 5, 10, 20, 27.5, and 30 mol/L by 
adjusting the prior NaOH concentration. In a Teflon 
vessel, 0.2 g of  Ti3AlC2 MAX phase was mixed with 
50 mL of the produced solution [65]. After agitating 
the mixture for fifteen minutes, the bottle was filled 
with argon gas and sealed. Before the microwave 
etching, the capped Teflon bottle was bath-sonicated 
for thirty minutes. The microwave was ramped to 
180 °C at 2 °C/min and held for 45 minutes. Accord-
ing to Latif et al. [65], the resultant alkaline solu-
tion was then cleaned and centrifuged for 5 minutes 
at 10,000 rpm until the supernatant’s pH reached 
approximately 8. After multiple rounds of washing, 
the samples were bath-sonicated under argon for 
approximately 1 h to delaminate them, then freeze-
dried to produce the etched MXene powder. The 
generated results and the observed performance 
were similar to those of the recently conducted anal-
ysis [65, 87].
CVD of MXenes
Through interactions of gaseous precursors on a sub-
strate, CVD is a flexible method for creating films, 
heterostructures, as well as wide-ranging materials, 
as demonstrated in Fig. 3g. While CVD can be used 
to generate transition-metal carbides and nitrides 
like  Mo2C,  Mo2N, and TiCx [88, 89], MXenes have 
not previously had access to such a synthetic alterna-
tive. Through CVD, investigations provide the direct 
synthesis of MXenes and demonstrate a path towards 
novel MXene geometries with exposed catalytically 
active edges and more accessible surfaces [66, 88]. The 
study used a diluted  CH4 and  TiCl4 gas mixture in Ar 
to grow MXenes via CVD at 950 °C on a Ti surface as 
depicted in Fig. 4a [66]. XRD was used to character-
ise the as-synthesised product (referred to as CVD-
Ti2CCl2) after a 15-min exposure (Fig. 4b). The lattice 
parameters a = 3.2225(2) Å and c = 8.7658(8) Å matched 
the reported values for  Ti2CCl2 MXene, as determined 
by Rietveld refinement [90].
The purity of  Ti2CCl2 MXene was further verified 
by Raman spectra, as shown in Fig. 2c. The crystallin-
ity and stoichiometry of CVD-Ti2CCl2 were validated 
by EDX analysis and high-resolution STEM-EELS. 
According to STEM images, the centre-to-centre 
interlayer spacing for  Ti2CCl2 MXenes was 0.88 ± 0.05 
nm. A substrate completely shielded with a furrowed 
layer of  Ti2CCl2 was visible in the SEM image shown 
in Fig. 4d. High-resolution HAADF images and EELS 
elemental mapping are shown in Fig. 4e. Convention-
ally synthesised MXenes would struggle to produce 
such a carpet of  Ti2CCl2 MXene sheets growing per-
pendicular to the substrate. Previously noted for other 
CVD-grown two-dimensional materials like  MoS2 
[91], this morphology seemed especially capable for 
active ion intercalation, as in supercapacitors [51, 92]. 
Phase-pure nitride  Ti2NCl2 MXene, which was pro-
duced when Ti foil doped with  TiCl4 and  N2 above 
640 °C, was feasibly the most fascinating outcome of 
the direct mixture (Fig. 4a–e). Many desirable charac-
teristics, such as ferromagnetism and higher conduc-
tivity than carbide MXenes, have been anticipated for 
nitride MXenes [93].

Machine learning technique for MXene 
optimisation
To overcome the drawbacks of conventional mate -
rial design and synthesis techniques for MXenes, 
Machine learning (ML) is essential. This provides a 
mechanism to expedite study and innovation in this 
area [94, 95]. ML algorithms are excellent at handling

Supercapacitors
Research on MXenes has been conducted to increase

Enhancements result from higher charge storage

 g−1 at 128C, enabling increased charge storage den-

Conclusion
This article summarises the sustainable potential of 
MXene materials in energy technology and their sus-
tainable synthesis methodologies. Many novel MXene 
families have been synthesised and studied for tech-
nological uses since the first MXene materials were 
discovered decades ago. In recent years, interest in 
MXene materials has significantly increased. Further-
more, the conductivity’s versatility through different 
combinations of functional groups enhances its utility. 
In this review, we begin by exploring the significance 
of energy in modern society. A brief description of 
the use of several MXene materials, Green Synthesis 
Methods, and potential MXene material optimisation 
using Machine learning algorithms is also included in 
the study. The potential use of MXene in energy con-
version and storage technologies, including batteries, 
capacitors, composites, and sensors, and its electro-
chemistry were also thoroughly reviewed. Due to their 
unique layered structure, abundance of surface termi-
nations, superior electrical conductivity, and interca-
lation pseudo-capacitance features, MXene materials 
are essential for improving the performance of energy 
conversion and storage devices.
Summary and future perspectives
In response to its huge interlayer spacing, custom-
isable surface chemistry, hydrophilic surfaces, and 
metallic conductivity, MXenes have become one of the 
most promising two-dimensional (2D) materials for 
energy storage and conversion devices. MXenes are 
appealing for lithium-ion batteries, sodium-ion batter-
ies, potassium-ion batteries, zinc-ion batteries, super-
capacitors, metal-air batteries, fuel cells, and electro-
catalytic applications due to their fast ion transport, 
plentiful electrochemically active sites, and superior 
charge-transfer kinetics.
Yet, despite their impressive progress, MXenes still 
have a long way to go before being fully utilised and 
commercialised in real-world energy storage and con-
version applications for a sustainable future.
• In recent decades, more than 30 types of MXenes 
have been developed, though only a few have been 
fully investigated for specific applications. There-
fore, it is crucial to conduct further studies on dif-
ferent MXenes to enhance their functionality and 
develop multifunctional applications. It is impor-
tant to consider and address MXene’s intrinsic low 
ambient stability before device manufacture begins. 
The fact that MXenes are often produced at high 
temperatures or with hazardous chemicals (such as

HF) has limited their practical use. Therefore, it is 
essential to synthesise MXenes under mild condi-
tions (e.g., non-toxic conditions and lower tempera-
tures) with high yield and purity at a reasonable 
cost for potential industry-scale applications.
• Despite the inherent benefits of MXene, develop-
ing complex, high-power/energy devices remains 
challenging. An integrated method of interfacial 
modification and engineering design (e.g., creating 
3D nanostructures) may be suitable for energy stor-
age devices by increasing the number of active sites 
for ion adsorption and storage during charge and 
discharge. Because of its greater surface area and 
simpler charge separation, it also improves energy 
conversion efficiency in energy harvesting systems.
• The high cost and challenging manufacture of MAX 
phase powders are two challenges impeding the 
commercial development of MXene. It is necessary 
to address the environmental toxicity of MXene-
based materials, and it would be ideal if a coordi-
nated effort could be made.
• Manufacturing production is problematic. The 
majority of research uses colloidal MXenes pro-
duced via solvent sonication or prolonged mixing/
shaking, neither of which is desirable for an indus-
trial-scale-up approach. The industrial incorpora-
tion of MXene into the electrodes of energy storage 
devices will encounter the same issues as graphene 
if colloidal MXenes are not avoided to achieve the 
anticipated property benefits. Reducing the existing 
high production costs is necessary for the applica-
tion of MXene in industrial settings.
• Research on the impact of defect engineering on 
MXenes is currently lacking. The overall perfor-
mance of MXene-based energy devices can be 
enhanced by optimising their flaws and active sites 
through a combination of engineering approaches. 
It might be necessary for future research to employ 
theoretical calculations and Machine learning to 
forecast and confirm how flaw engineering will 
affect material properties.
• MXenes, especially  Ti3C2Tx, are highly prone to 
oxidative deterioration. To preserve or improve 
their oxidation stability, careful consideration of 
synthesis techniques, ambient factors, and surface 
modification is crucial. For MXenes to reach their 
full potential in a variety of applications, such as 
energy storage, catalysis, and electrical devices, 
these tactics are essential. To reduce degradation 
and increase the usefulness of these promising 2D 
materials, studies are still working to improve the 
synthesis and handling conditions. This involves 
investigating novel approaches to surface modi-
fication, protective coatings, and synthesis.
Notwithstanding these drawbacks, pure MXenes 
and MXene-based materials have a bright future in 
energy storage and conversion devices due to their 
unique properties. To improve performance and 
deepen understanding of the electrochemical charge 
storage mechanism of MXene-based materials, exten -
sive work, innovative concepts, and the use of state-
of-the-art research methods are needed.
