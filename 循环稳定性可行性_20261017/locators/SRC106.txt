Electrode materials are critical to the operation of CDI systems and contribute significantly to how effectively a CDI system can desalinate water [24],[1]. The performance of CDI electrodes is influenced by several interrelated material parameters, including (i) specific surface area (SSA), which indicates the number of available sites for ion adsorption; (ii) pore size distribution, where micropores provide sites for high capacitance (less than 2 nm) and mesopores promote rapid ion transport (2–50 nm); (iii) electrical

Activated carbon has become a dominant electrode material used in CDI applications owing to a distinct combination of its properties, namely, large SSA (usually between 1,000 and 3,000 m²·g⁻¹), hierarchical porosity, excellent chemical stability, good electrical conductivity, and low cost [28],[29]. Electrochemical evaluation confirmed that the electrode exhibits typical EDLC behavior, as indicated by quasi-rectangular CV curves at scan rates of 5– 100 mV·s⁻¹, low polarization with minimal IR drop, and stable charge–discharge profiles. The electrode also demonstrated excellent cycling stability, retaining 96%–98% of its capacitance after 100 charge– discharge cycles at a current density of 3 A·g⁻¹, indicating strong durability and resistance to repeated electrochemical cycling. Regarding CDI desalination performance, the electrode effectively removed ions under various feed operating conditions. For a feed conductivity of ~1,050 µS·cm⁻¹, the effluent conductivity decreased to ~840 µS·cm⁻¹ at an applied voltage of 0.8 V and to ~630 µS·cm⁻¹ at 1.0 V after 120 min of operation. Additionally, the electrode exhibited strong electrosorption capability, achieving an SAC of ~38 mg·g⁻¹ at a salinity of 500 ppm, along with favorable adsorption kinetics characterized by an SAR of 1.3–1.4 mg·g⁻¹min⁻¹ at the SAC of 35–38 mg·g⁻¹.
