ABSTRACT Supercapacitors are promising energy storage devices owing to their high power density and excellent cycling stability. The conversion of biomass waste into activated carbon with hierarchical pore structures has emerged as a sustainable strategy for developing high-performance supercapacitor electrodes. Herein, activated carbons were synthesized from macadamia nut shell (MNS) waste via a two-step process comprising carbonization at 600 °C and subsequent chemical activation with potassium hydroxide (KOH) at 800 °C, using weight ratios of carbonized MNS to KOH of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3. The resulting activated carbons exhibited an amorphous structure with a predominant microporosity. KOH activation substantially increased the specific surface area from 325 to 1362 m2 g–1 through gas evolution, etching, and potassium intercalation, thereby promoting the predominant micropore formation along with partial mesopore development. Increasing the KOH ratio induced greater structural disorder, widened micropores, increased the mesopore fraction, enriched C–O functionalities, and suppressed CO and O–CO groups. Electrochemical measurements in a 6 M KOH electrolyte using a three-electrode system revealed that the sample activated at a 1:3 ratio delivered a maximum specific capacitance of 170 F g–1 at 1 A g–1 and exhibited predominantly electric double-layer capacitor (EDLC) behavior with a minor pseudocapacitive contribution, as confirmed by power-law, Trasatti, and Dunn analyses. This performance is attributed to its large specific surface area, partially developed mesoporosity, and enhanced surface wettability. A symmetric coin-cell supercapacitor assembled using this material delivered an energy density of 5.4 W h kg–1 and a power density of 2500 W kg–1, while maintaining excellent cycling stability over 10,000 charge–discharge cycles at 3 A g–1. These results reveal the potential valorization of MNS-derived activated carbons as sustainable and efficient electrode materials for high-performance supercapacitors.
BODY_P0 The global transition toward sustainable energy has escalated the demand for efficient, reliable, and environmentally friendly energy storage devices. In particular, the increasing adoption of renewable energy sources, such as solar and wind, introduces inherent intermittence, which poses challenges in maintaining a stable and continuous power supply. , To mitigate these power fluctuations, energy storage systems are essential for balancing energy generation and consumption, thereby ensuring uninterrupted power availability regardless of the time or environmental conditions. − Among various energy storage technologies, supercapacitors have attracted considerable attention owing to their high power density, rapid charge–discharge capability, and excellent cycling stability. , These advantages make supercapacitors ideal candidates for energy storage applications in renewable energy integration, grid stabilization, load leveling, uninterruptible power supplies (UPS), and portable power sources for consumer electronics and medical devices. ,
BODY_P1 Supercapacitors are typically categorized into three types: electric double-layer capacitors (EDLCs), pseudocapacitors (PCs), and hybrid supercapacitors (HSCs). Although PCs and HSCs offer higher energy densities, EDLCs stand out for their long cycle life, excellent thermal and chemical stabilities, and safety. In EDLCs, the charge storage mechanism, based on electrostatic ion adsorption at the electrode–electrolyte interface, ensures minimal degradation and long-term stability, making them a particularly attractive choice for practical applications. , The electrochemical properties of EDLCs are strongly influenced by the physicochemical properties of the electrode materials. Ideal electrode materials require large surface areas, well-controlled and interconnected pore structures, high conductivity, and robust chemical and thermal stabilities. − Among various explored materials, activated carbons (ACs) have emerged as leading candidates because of their cost effectiveness, hierarchical pore structure, and suitability for large-scale production. − A large surface area provides more active sites for electrostatic ion adsorption, enhancing the charge storage capacity. Furthermore, a hierarchical pore structure, comprising micropores (<2 nm), mesopores (2–50 nm), and macropores (>50 nm), facilitates efficient electrolyte diffusion and ion transport. Micropores predominantly contribute to the capacitance by storing charges, while mesopores and macropores serve as ion-buffering reservoirs, facilitating rapid ion accessibility to microporous regions. This synergistic pore architecture is crucial for improving the rate capability and specific capacitance of supercapacitor systems. −
BODY_P2 Recently, the development of sustainable and low-cost carbon sources has attracted considerable attention, particularly the use of biomass waste as renewable precursors for AC production. − Agricultural and agro-industrial processes generate large quantities of biomass residues, including husks, peels, shells, and other lignocellulosic byproducts. , Despite their high carbon content, these materials are often overlooked or discarded. Numerous studies have investigated various biomass wastes, such as rice, corn, and wheat husks; coconut, cashew nut, peanut, walnut, and hazelnut shells; and mangosteen, banana, pomelo, and orange peels, for their potential in producing ACs for supercapacitor applications. The carbon yield, porosity, and electrochemical properties of the resulting materials are influenced by intrinsic biomass properties, including lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose contents, morphological structures, densities, and ash compositions. − Moreover, processing conditions, including the carbonization temperature and duration, activation method (physical or chemical), type and ratio of activating agents, and atmospheric conditions, play critical roles in determining the AC properties (e.g., morphology, porosity, surface chemistry, and conductivity). − Therefore, the optimization of both the biomass precursor and processing conditions is essential for designing high-performance carbon electrodes for EDLCs.
BODY_P3 Among various biomass wastes, macadamia nut shells (MNS) have recently attracted attention as a promising precursor for AC production because of their hard and dense structure, which indicates a high fixed-carbon content and superior structural stability compared with those of softer lignocellulosic residues. Therefore, MNS provide a robust carbon framework that can withstand high-temperature treatments, making them highly suitable for conversion to porous carbons through chemical activation. With the global expansion of macadamia cultivation, the generation of MNS waste is rapidly increasing. In 2022, the global macadamia nut production reached ∼298,914 t (equivalent to ∼90,000 tons of kernels), with shells comprising nearly 75% of the total nut weight. According to projections from the World Macadamia Organization and the International Nuts and Dried Fruits Council, the global supply of macadamia nuts is expected to double by 2027 and triple by 2030. Consequently, vast quantities of MNS waste are produced annually, creating an urgent need for sustainable waste management and valorization strategies. To date, MNS-derived carbons have been mainly studied for adsorption and environmental remediation, while their electrochemical performance at the EDLC device level has only been reported in a limited number of studies. Furthermore, the effects of activation conditions, particularly the activating agent-to-carbon ratio, on the pore hierarchy and charge storage mechanism have rarely been investigated. Addressing these gaps is essential for advancing MNS-derived ACs toward practical supercapacitor applications. Therefore, the valorization of MNS waste into ACs not only provides an environmentally responsible approach to managing agricultural waste but also enables the design of sustainable electrode materials aligned with circular economy principles in energy storage technologies.
BODY_P4 Herein, ACs derived from MNS waste (AC-MNS) were synthesized through a two-step process comprising carbonization at 600 °C followed by chemical activation with potassium hydroxide (KOH) at 800 °C. The carbonized MNS-to-KOH weight ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 were employed to investigate the effect on activation conditions. The resulting AC-MNS samples were comprehensively characterized to evaluate their morphology, surface area, pore structure, phase structure, and surface chemistry. Mechanistically, KOH activation promotes the formation of micropore-dominant networks, while the mesopore fraction can be tuned, depending on the KOH ratio. The electrochemical properties of AC-MNS were examined using a three-electrode system by cyclic voltammetry (CV) and galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) in a 6 M KOH electrolyte. In addition, the relative contributions of EDLC and PC behaviors to the charge storage capacity were quantified using multiple methods. These insights provide a framework for understanding the structure-property relationships of AC-MNS and for rationalizing their electrochemical performance. The best-performing sample was prepared as an electrode and assembled into a symmetric coin-cell supercapacitor (two-electrode system) to demonstrate its practical potential for energy storage applications.
BODY_P5 The MNS waste was collected from Doi Tung under the Mae Fah Luang Foundation in Chaing Rai, Thailand. Potassium hydroxide (KOH), isopropanol (C3H8O, 99.8%), and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (C5H9NO, NMP, 99.5%) were purchased from LabScan. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%) was purchased from QRëC. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, molecular weight ≈534,000 g mol–1) and Nafion DE 521 solution (5 wt % in a mixture of lower aliphatic alcohols and water) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich. Potassium bromide (KBr, ≥99.0) was acquired from Thermo Scientific Chemicals. Acetylene black and graphite sheets were supplied from AME Energy, while a cellulose separator (30 μm thick) was sourced from the Nippon Kodoshi Corporation. All the chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification.
BODY_P6 The collected MNS waste was washed with tap water, sun-dried for 48 h, and subsequently carbonized under oxygen-limited conditions at 600 °C for 18 h using an industrial furnace, and then cooled to room temperature. The carbonized sample was ground into fine powders using a high-speed industrial grinder (Yun Bang YB-2500A) for 1 min and passed through a 150 μm sieve (100 mesh), yielding a sample referred to as C-MNS. Subsequently, C-MNS was impregnated in KOH solution at C-MNS:KOH weight ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 in deionized water and continuously stirred at 500 rpm for 1 h to obtain a homogeneous slurry. Then, the slurry was transferred to an alumina boat and dried in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h. Samples were heated at 5 °C min–1 in a tube furnace and chemically activated at 800 °C for 1 h under argon flowing at 0.2 L min–1. The activated products were washed thoroughly with 1 M HCl solution and deionized water until the filtrate reached a neutral pH. Finally, the samples were dried at 105 °C for 24 h and ground into fine powders. The samples prepared using C-MNS-to-KOH ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 were denoted as AC-MNS-1, AC-MNS-2, and AC-MNS-3, respectively. The yields of AC-MNS-1, AC-MNS-2, and AC-MNS-3 relative to C-MNS were 74%, 66%, and 52%, respectively. BODY_P9 For the three-electrode system, AC-MNS (5 mg) was dispersed in mixtures of 475, 475, and 50 μL of deionized water, isopropanol, and Nafion DE521, respectively, and sonicated for 1 h to form a homogeneous suspension. A glassy carbon (GC) electrode (3 mm in diameter, ALS Co., Ltd.) was polished on a polishing pad using 0.1 μm diamond and 0.05 μm alumina slurries, respectively. The polished GC electrode was rinsed with deionized water and air-dried. A 3 μL aliquot of the AC-MNS suspension was drop cast onto the GC electrode and air-dried for 3 h, producing a mass loading of 0.015 mg. To confirm reproducibility, three modified GC electrodes were prepared for each sample using the same procedure.
BODY_P10 Electrochemical properties were measured at room temperature in a three-electrode system using a 6 M KOH electrolyte. A platinum wire (ALS Co., Ltd.) and Hg/HgO in 1 M NaOH solution (ALS Co., Ltd.) were the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The AC-MNS-modified GC electrodes were used as the working electrodes. A Biologic VSP potentiostat/galvanostat equipped with EC-Lab software was employed for CV and GCD measurements.

BODY_P15 Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted for both two- and three-electrode systems at 0 V over the frequency range from 100 kHz to 10 mHz, using a sinusoidal perturbation of 5 mV. For detailed analysis, raw EIS data were fitted using the ZFit module in EC-Lab software, and the equivalent circuit was constructed by selecting the minimum number of parameters necessary to accurately represent the electrochemical system. The equivalent circuit consisting of two constant phase elements (CPEs) was employed to account for the nonideal capacitive behavior arising from surface heterogeneity and porous electrode morphology. Specifically, CPE1 represents the electric double-layer capacitance at the electrode–electrolyte interface, while CPE2 reflects ion diffusion and charge accumulation within the porous carbon network, as evidenced by the Warburg element.
BODY_P16 The equivalent series resistance (ESR) of the coin-cell supercapacitor was estimated from the IR drop observed in the GCD curves. Its cycling stability was further assessed through 10,000 continuous GCD cycles at a current density of 3 A g–1. In addition, the open-circuit voltage decay was also monitored over 24 h after charging to 1.0 V to assess its self-discharge behavior.
BODY_P17 The structural properties of C-MNS and AC-MNS were examined by XRD and Raman spectroscopy, as presented in Figure a,b, respectively. The XRD pattern of C-MNS showed a broad diffraction peak centered at ∼23° and a weaker hump at ∼43°, corresponding to the (002) and (100)/(101) carbon planes, respectively, indicating amorphous carbon with a low degree of graphitization. When the AC-MNS samples were activated using KOH, the (002) diffraction peak progressively broadened and weakened with increasing KOH content, indicating a gradual decrease in graphitic order and the formation of increasingly disordered carbon structures because potassium intercalation and etching disrupted interlayer stacking. Additionally, in the low-angle region, the scattering intensity noticeably increased for AC-MNS-2 and AC-MNS-3, suggesting enhanced development of microporous structure. The absence of sharp crystalline peaks in the XRD patterns implies the high purity of all the samples and the effective removal of residual activating agents and impurities during washing.

BODY_P19 The Raman spectra of C-MNS and AC-MNS revealed two distinct peaks at 1350 cm–1 and 1595 cm–1, corresponding to the D and G bands, respectively. The G band arises from the E2g vibration mode of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms, representing the graphitic domains. In contrast, the D band is associated with structural defects and disordered carbon. The overlap between the D and G bands further supports the amorphous structure of these carbon materials. The calculated D-to-G band intensity ratios (I D/I G) for C-MNS, AC-MNS-1, AC-MNS-2, and AC-MNS-3 were 0.85, 0.92, 0.98, and 1.02, respectively. This increasing I D/I G ratio with increasing KOH content indicates increased structural disorder and reduced graphitization within the carbon lattice because the extensive development of micropores and mesopores during activation exposes a greater fraction of the amorphous carbon edge, which agrees with the XRD patterns.
BODY_P20 The surface morphology of the C-MNS and AC-MNS samples was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Figure a–d. C-MNS exhibited a relatively flat and compact surface with minimal porosity, which is characteristic of nonactivated biomass-derived carbon. By contrast, KOH activation induced substantial morphological transformation. The AC-MNS surface was rough, and pores began to develop. Moreover, the surface was textured, and a sponge-like, interconnected porous network developed. This morphological evolution indicates effective chemical etching by KOH activation. However, finer features such as micro- and mesopores could not be directly observed because of the resolution limits of SEM.

BODY_P22 To further examine the internal structure, the AC-MNS-3 microstructure was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Figure e,f, low-magnification TEM images of AC-MNS-3 reveal irregularly shaped carbon fragments. A high-magnification TEM image (Figure g) displays a highly disordered structure with short-range graphitic ordering. BODY_P23 EDS mapping analysis was used to further verify the purity of the C-MNS and AC-MNS (Figures S2 and S3). C-MNS contained mainly carbon (C) and oxygen (O), with a trace of potassium (K). By contrast, AC-MNS samples had no detectable residual K or any other inorganic compounds (Table S1), indicating that acid washing and neutralization effectively removed residual K-containing species. The ash content was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) over the temperature range of 50–900 °C under airflow (Figure S4). At 30–100 °C, a slight weight loss corresponded to the removal of adsorbed moisture, while the weight remained nearly constant up to 500 °C, indicating good thermal stability. Above 500 °C, a major single-step weight loss occurred because of carbon decomposition, leaving residual weights of <1 wt % at 800 °C. These results confirm that AC-MNS possessed high carbon purity, negligible residual potassium originating from the activation process, and minimal ash content. Such a low ash content is particularly important for electrochemical applications in alkaline electrolytes, as residual inorganic species may dissolve or participate in parasitic reactions, leading to increased internal resistance and degraded cycling stability.
BODY_P24 The surface area and porosity of C-MNS and AC-MNS samples were evaluated using N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms, as presented in Figure a. All the samples exhibited a type I isotherm, indicating microporosity. Upon KOH activation, N2 uptake substantially increased, particularly at higher KOH contents, indicating a substantially large surface area. The specific surface areas (S BET), determined using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, of C-MNS, AC-MNS-1, AC-MNS-2, and AC-MNS-3 were 325, 825, 1286, and 1362 m2 g–1, respectively. The pore volume (V total) showed a similar trend, increasing from 0.176 to 0.575 cm3 g–1 (Table ), highlighting the effectiveness of KOH activation in developing porous structures. Nonlocal density functional theory (NLDFT) pore size distribution profiles (Figure b) revealed that all the AC-MNS samples were predominantly microporous, with pore sizes concentrated in the range of 0.5–2.0 nm. AC-MNS-1 exhibited sharp and intense peaks centered at ∼0.8 nm, indicating the dominance of uniform micropores and contributing to a high micropore fraction. By contrast, AC-MNS-2 displayed a broader pore size distribution, with a peak at ∼0.8 nm and an extended tail toward larger pores (∼1.0–1.5 nm). AC-MNS-3 showed a relatively broader pore size distribution, with wider distributions in larger micropores and small-mesopore regions. The NLDFT cumulative pore volume curves of AC-MNS samples are shown in Figure c. AC-MNS-1 exhibited the lowest pore volume, dominated by narrow micropores, while AC-MNS-2 and AC-MNS-3 showed substantially higher pore volumes, with extended distributions toward the larger micropore region. To further elucidate the micropore and mesopore contributions, t-plot analysis was employed to evaluate the micropore surface area (S micro) and volume (V micro). The results confirmed that across all samples, micropores dominated both surface area and pore volume, while mesopores contributed a smaller but increasing fraction, as shown in Figure d and Table . The mesopore contributions to S BET increased with increasing KOH content: 11.6%, 17.4%, and 26.5% for AC-MNS-1, AC-MNS-2, and AC-MNS-3, respectively. The mesopore volume followed a similar trend, rising from 15.2% to 21.0% and 30.1% for AC-MNS-1, AC-MNS-2, and AC-MNS-3, respectively. This progressive mesopore development revealed the effects of increasing the KOH ratio on pore widening and network evolution. By contrast, the micropore area and volume were slightly reduced in AC-MNS-3, indicating that the overall increase in S BET mainly originated from mesopore formation. Collectively, these results revealed that higher KOH contents enhanced the total surface area by widening micropores and generating additional mesopores, thereby improving the hierarchical porosity.

BODY_P33 To further elucidate the porosity development in AC-MNS driven by chemical activation reactions between C-MNS and KOH, the etching and intercalation processes are described and discussed according to the following reactions 5 6KOH+2C→2K+3H2+2K2CO3 6 K2CO3+2C→K2O+2CO 7 K2CO3→K2O+CO2 8 2K+CO2→K2O+2CO
BODY_P34 These reactions begin with the formation of potassium carbonate (K2CO3) at ∼400 °C. As the temperature increases to ∼600 °C, KOH is almost entirely converted to K2CO3. Further heating to ∼700 °C decomposes K2CO3, generating potassium oxide (K2O), accompanied by the evolution of carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon dioxide (CO2). Although traces of these gases are released at lower temperatures, their evolution intensifies above 800 °C, serving as activating agents for physical activation. At higher KOH contents, the activation process becomes more intensified. Initially, KOH promotes micropore formation through chemical etching and intercalation. However, with excess KOH, these micropores widen and coalesce because of intensified etching, gas evolution (CO, CO2, and H2), and the penetration of metallic potassium into the carbon framework. As a result, many of the micropores merge into larger pores and small mesopores, thereby decreasing and increasing micro- and mesopore contributions, respectively. The combination of gas evolution, chemical etching, and intercalation between KOH and the carbon matrix promotes the formation of both micro- and mesopores, producing a hierarchical micromesoporous network with enhanced surface area in the AC-MNS samples. −
BODY_P35 The chemical functionalities in C-MNS and AC-MNS were analyzed by FTIR spectroscopy, as shown in Figure a. The C-MNS spectrum showed weak absorption bands because of substantial degradation of the lignocellulosic framework at high temperatures. The weak absorption bands at 2920 and 2850 cm–1 were attributed to the C–H stretching vibrations of CH3 and CH2 groups, respectively, while bands in the 1500–1400 cm–1 region were assigned to CC stretching vibrations. Upon KOH activation, a broad absorption band at ∼3400 cm–1, corresponding to the O–H stretching vibrations of the hydroxyl group or water molecules adsorbed on the surface, began to emerge. Additionally, a weak absorption band between 1300 and 900 cm–1, associated with the C–O stretching vibrations in ether and hydroxyl groups, appeared. With increasing KOH content, the O–H and C–O absorption bands gradually intensified, consistent with previously reported results. , Notably, the low-intensity absorption bands in the C-MNS and AC-MNS spectra were attributed to the blackbody absorbance of carbon materials.

BODY_P37 To further elucidate the surface chemical compositions and bonding states, XPS measurement was conducted. The XPS survey spectra exhibited only C 1s and O 1s peaks, confirming the absence of residual activating agents and other impurities (Figure b). The surface chemical compositions (C and O contents) obtained from the quantitative analysis are summarized in Table . The C-MNS contained C and O contents of 81.8 atom % and 18.2 atom %, respectively. Following KOH activation, both the O contents and corresponding O/C ratios of the AC-MNS samples gradually increased with increasing KOH contents, indicating the incorporation of additional oxygen-containing surface groups. The high-resolution C 1s XPS spectra of the C-MNS and AC-MNS samples (Figure c) were deconvoluted into three distinct peaks: CI at 284.5 ± 0.2 eV (C–C/CC bonds), CII at 286.4 ± 0.2 eV (C–O bonds), and CIII at 288.6 ± 0.2 eV (CO bonds). − The CI peak, corresponding to graphitic carbon, weakened with increasing KOH content, indicating increased oxygen-containing surface functional groups during activation. The high-resolution XPS O 1s spectra (Figure d) were deconvoluted into three distinct peaks: OI at 530.6 ± 0.2 eV (CO bonds: carbonyl and quinone), OII at 532.4 ± 0.2 eV (C–O bonds: phenol, ether, and epoxide groups), and OIII at 534.5 ± 0.1 eV (O–CO bonds, carboxylic groups). − Among these peaks, the OII peak (C–O bonds) was the most intense, revealing that C–O bonds were the predominant oxygen-containing functionality on the carbon surface, whereas the OI- and OIII-peak–associated functionalities contributed to a lesser extent (Table ). With increasing KOH content, the OII peak remained the most prominent, whereas the OI and OIII peaks progressively weakened, indicating fewer carbonyl and carboxyl functionalities. This change can be attributed to the chemical reactivity during activation. KOH preferentially reacts with the carboxylic groups rather than hydroxyl groups because of the higher acidity of the former, decreasing the O–CO content with increasing KOH content. Additionally, at elevated temperatures, CO groups were converted to C–OH functionalities via hydrogenation, contributing to the increased number of C–O bonds. , These findings are consistent with the FTIR spectra, revealing the development of C–O absorption bands at higher KOH contents.
BODY_P38 Oxygen-containing functional groups directly influenced the surface wettability of the AC-MNS, as clearly confirmed by water contact angle measurements. AC-MNS-1 exhibited a high angle of 83.9° ± 5.2°, whereas AC-MNS-2 and AC-MNS-3 showed substantially lower angles of 58.6° ± 8.4° and 51.0° ± 8.3°, respectively. The reduced contact angle with increasing oxygen content highlights the enhanced hydrophilicity of AC-MNS-2 and AC-MNS-3 relative to that of AC-MNS-1. This finding reveals that oxygen functionalities play important roles in promoting water adsorption and hydrophilicity, as they increase surface polarity and facilitate hydrogen bonding with water molecules. In addition, in the carbon framework, increased mesopore development enhances wettability by facilitating electrolyte infiltration and distribution while promoting the exposure of oxygenated surface groups that further improve interfacial compatibility. , The enhanced wettability of AC-MNS-3 should improve electrolyte accessibility to its porous structure, thereby facilitating ion transport during electrochemical processes and ultimately improving charge storage performance.
BODY_P39 Electrochemical measurements were conducted using a three-electrode system in 6 M KOH electrolyte within a potential of −1.0–0 V to evaluate the charge storage behavior. The CV profiles of C-MNS and AC-MNS scanned at 50 mV s–1 are shown in Figure a. The CV profile of C-MNS exhibited a distorted CV shape and a low current response, indicating limited capacitance. By contrast, the CV profiles of all the AC-MNS samples displayed a quasi-rectangular feature with no redox reaction, which is characteristic of EDLC behavior. , According to the CV profiles, the current response increased in the following order: AC-MNS-3 > AC-MNS-2 > AC-MNS-1. AC-MNS-3 possessed the highest current response, suggesting its superior capacitive performance. The rate capabilities of the AC-MNS samples were further assessed by varying the scan rates from 10 to 100 mV s–1 (Figures b and S5). Across all the scan rates, the CV profiles retained their quasi-rectangular shape with minimal distortion, indicating excellent ion-transport kinetics and low internal resistance. The GCD profiles at a current density of 1 A g–1 for all the samples are shown in Figure c. C-MNS showed a nonlinear triangular GCD profile, indicating poor charging and discharging. In contrast, the GCD profiles of the AC-MNS samples exhibited nearly symmetric, linear-triangular shapes, further confirming their EDLC behavior. Among them, AC-MNS-3 delivered the longest discharge time, in line with the current response determined from the CV profiles. For all the samples, further increasing current density from 1 to 20 A g–1 shortened the charge–discharge time (Figures d and S6). Even at high current densities, the GCD profiles of AC-MNS retained a nearly linear-triangular shape with negligible IR drops (Figure S7), indicating minimal internal resistance. The specific capacitance (C s) values, calculated from the GCD profiles, were 6, 153, 158, and 170 F g–1 for C-MNS, AC-MNS-1, AC-MNS-2, and AC-MNS-3, respectively, with an estimated error of ∼3–5%. BODY_P41 EIS measurements were conducted to further elucidate the impedance behavior of the AC-MNS electrodes, with the corresponding Nyquist plots shown in Figure S8. The high-frequency intercepts indicate solution resistances (R s) of 6.07, 8.89, and 6.75 Ω for AC-MNS-1, AC-MNS-2, and AC-MNS-3, respectively, while the depressed semicircles in the midfrequency region correspond to charge-transfer resistance (R ct) values in the range of 0.83–1.87 Ω (Table S2). In the low-frequency region, all samples exhibited nearly vertical lines with slight deviations, indicating dominant EDLC behavior with minor diffusion effect, which is commonly observed for porous carbon electrodes. Although AC-MNS-1 exhibited the lowest overall impedance, it delivered the lowest capacitance among the samples. This apparent discrepancy highlights that electrochemical performance is not solely governed by resistance but is strongly dependent on pore accessibility and surface chemistry. AC-MNS-1 was dominated by narrow micropores with limited pore widening and a relatively low concentration of oxygen-containing functional groups. While such a structure minimizes internal resistance and enables rapid ion transport along accessible surfaces, it restricts electrolyte penetration into the internal pore network. Consequently, a significant portion of the available surface area remains electrochemically inaccessible, resulting in limited effective charge storage despite low impedance. In contrast, AC-MNS-2 and AC-MNS-3 exhibited moderately higher impedance, likely due to increased structural disorder induced by more intensive KOH activation, which increases ion-transport tortuosity. However, this activation also promotes pore widening and the development of interconnected mesopores, significantly enhancing pore connectivity and electrolyte accessibility. In particular, AC-MNS-3 showed a reduced relative contribution from narrow micropores but achieved the highest capacitance, clearly demonstrating that accessible surface area and hierarchical pore architecture, rather than absolute microporosity, govern electrochemical performance. The interconnected mesopores act as ion-buffering pathways and transport channels, facilitating efficient ion diffusion toward adjacent micropores and improving utilization of the available surface area. − Moreover, the increased oxygen-containing functional groups in AC-MNS-3 enhance surface wettability, further promoting electrolyte infiltration and ion mobility throughout the porous carbon network. Although these structural features introduce moderately higher impedance, their positive effect on pore accessibility and ion transport outweighs the resistance increase, leading to superior capacitance performance. Notably, the slightly lower R s of AC-MNS-3 compared with AC-MNS-2 can be attributed to its more developed mesoporosity and improved wettability, which partially compensate for increased tortuosity.
BODY_P42 Overall, these results emphasize that low impedance alone does not guarantee high capacitance. Instead, optimal electrochemical performance arises from a balance between pore connectivity, accessible surface area, and surface wettability. The superior performance of AC-MNS-3 therefore originates from its hierarchical micro–mesoporous structure and favorable surface chemistry, which collectively maximize electrolyte accessibility and effective charge storage.
BODY_P43 The C s value achieved by AC-MNS-3 in our work (170 F g–1) was either superior or comparable to those of other biomass-derived ACs reported in the literature (Table ), highlighting its potential as an alternative electrode material for supercapacitor applications. BODY_P44 To elucidate the charge storage mechanisms and distinguish the relative contributions of the EDLC and PC, the current responses obtained from the CV profiles scanned at different rates were analyzed using the power-law relationship, Trasatti’s method, and Dunn’s method. The detailed methodology of these approaches is provided in the Supporting Information. According to the power law (I = av b ), the logarithmic plots between the currents and scan rates of all the samples (Figure S9) yield slopes corresponding to b values. A b value of 1 indicates a surface-controlled process (EDLC), whereas b value of 0.5 indicates a diffusion-controlled process (PC). As shown in Figure a, the AC-MNS samples exhibited b values approaching 1 (∼0.98), indicating that charge storage is largely governed by EDLC mechanisms, with a minor PC contribution. In contrast, C-MNS possessed lower b values (0.51–0.78), suggesting a greater PC contribution.

BODY_P47 Although the AC-MNS samples contained high levels of oxygen-containing functional groups, the Faradaic redox reactions in alkaline aqueous electrolytes are primarily associated with carboxylic groups through their interaction with hydroxyl ions (OH–), while other oxygen functionalities, such as phenolic or quinone-like groups, contribute only weakly or indirectly to the overall electrochemical. , However, the variation in the PC contribution was not only correlated with the carboxyl group concentration but also influenced by both the surface wettability and pore architecture. For the AC-MNS-2 electrode, the lowest PC contribution can be attributed to its well-developed mesopores, which serve as ion-buffering pathways that facilitate electrolyte diffusion into micropores, thereby enhancing EDLC dominance. In contrast, the slightly increased PC contribution for AC-MNS-3 arises from the combined effects of additional mesopore formation and enhanced surface wettability, exposing more carboxylic groups while providing sufficient space for ion diffusion during redox reactions. Therefore, the hierarchical micro–mesoporous structure of AC-MNS, together with oxygenated functional groups, synergistically balances both EDLC and PC contributions, optimizing the electrochemical properties.
BODY_P48 To evaluate the applicability of AC-MNS-3 as an electrode material for practical supercapacitor devices, a symmetric coin-cell configuration was assembled in a two-electrode system. As shown in Figure a, the CV profiles scanned from 10 to 100 mV s–1 exhibited a quasi-rectangular shape without any redox peaks, confirming the predominant EDLC behavior. To prevent water decomposition and minimize the risk of gas evolution within the coin cell, the operating potential window was limited to 1.0 V. At high scan rates, negligible distortion indicates excellent rate capability and capacitive performance, suggesting efficient ion transport and low internal resistance within the electrode material. The GCD profiles recorded at current densities ranging from 0.1 to 5 A g–1 displayed a symmetric and linear-triangular shape (Figure b), further confirming the ideal capacitive behavior and a high degree of reversibility of charging and discharging. At higher current densities, a small IR drop became more evident, as shown in Figure c. The ESR of the supercapacitor was determined using the relation: ESR = ΔV/2I, where ΔV represents the IR drop. The calculated ESR was as low as 0.31 Ω, indicating very low internal and diffusion resistances. Such a low ESR contributes to the high power performance of the supercapacitor and minimizes energy loss as heat during electrochemical charging and discharging. The Nyquist plot of the coin-cell supercapacitor displayed a small high-frequency intercept (R s), a modestly depressed semicircle, and an almost vertical low-frequency rise (Figure d). The limited arc diameter indicated low R ct, consistent with efficient ion access to the carbon pore network, while the near-vertical tail confirmed the ideal EDLC-dominant behavior, with only minor diffusion limitations. From the fitting with the equivalent circuit, R s and R ct were 0.24 and 0.08 Ω, respectively (Table S4). The combination of low R s and R ct values indicated minimal ohmic and interfacial losses in the coin-cell configuration.

BODY_P52 This study reveals the synthesis of ACs from MNS waste via the KOH activation at 800 °C. Increasing the KOH content produced a more disordered carbon structure and substantially enhanced the specific surface area through the progressive development of micro- and mesopores. Although AC-MNS samples were predominantly microporous, higher KOH contents promoted mesopore formation and micropore widening, thereby creating a hierarchical pore structure. With increasing KOH contents, the surface oxygen functionalities, particularly the C–O group, increased, improving surface wettability. The electrochemical evaluation of a three-electrode system revealed that AC-MNS-3 exhibited the highest capacitance, achieving a maximum C s value of 170 F g–1 at 1 A g–1, with 77% capacitance retention at 20 A g–1. This superior performance can be attributed to its largest specific surface area, well-developed hierarchical micro–mesoporosity, and favorable surface wettability. The charge storage behavior of AC-MNS was dominated by the EDLC, with a minor contribution from the PC. Variations in the PC contribution were closely linked to oxygen functionalities, surface wettability, and pore architecture. When assembled into a symmetric coin-cell supercapacitor, the device exhibited an energy density of 5.4 W h kg–1 and a power density of 2500 W kg–1, with excellent cycling stability and low self-discharge. These findings highlight the critical role of the KOH content in tailoring pore structure and surface chemistry, while demonstrating the potential of AC-MNS as an efficient, sustainable, and cost-effective electrode material for supercapacitor applications. From an industrial perspective, the use of KOH activation with MNS waste further suggests that AC-MNS could be produced at a large scale through processes broadly compatible with existing carbon-processing infrastructure. Future studies will focus on heteroatom doping to introduce controlled PC contributions and the development of a scalable aqueous-electrolyte pouch cell to further validate their practical application feasibility.
